# Essay 2 — Two Evolutions (pp. 31–58) — pitches

Sections: Similarities (33) · Differences (38) · The First Cause (50) · Several Naïve
Questions (55). Notes at ~356–358 (read before drafting, per AGENT).

Load-bearing passages:

- pp. 33–38 **the analogy**: new forms arrive small, primitive, copying their predecessor
  ("the first automobile was a spitting image of the coach with a guillotined shaft; the
  airplane had been 'copied' from the kite"); then radiation into niches; then gigantism
  before decline ("The last zeppelins of the 1930s can be easily compared with the
  atlantosauruses"); then lateral branches persisting as "the last proof of the primeval
  abundance and power of a species". Dated specifics: the classical airplane answering the
  helicopter with vertical take-off (p. 35); cinema fighting television by enlarging and
  "surrounding" the viewer (p. 36); and the prediction that when the car is replaced by an
  "air-cushion vehicle", "a small combustion-driven lawnmower will be the last descendant of
  the classical car" (p. 36). Also fashion as the counterpart of sexual selection (pp. 36–37)
  and mimicry in design (p. 37).
- pp. 39–41 **against the jackpot**: the probabilistic account of biogenesis is "a false
  approach"; self-organisation is "not unique but rather typical", and life is "only one
  possible enactment of the process of homeostatic organization, which is widespread in the
  Universe" — "cosmic panevolutionism".
- p. 43 **the universality claim**: "the universality of our technologies is minimal.
  Technical evolution has so far been moving in a kind of reverse direction to biological
  evolution in that it has only been creating narrowly specialized devices", and the route out
  is "a further development of the theory of self-organizing systems—which would be capable of
  adaptive self-programming" (pp. 43–44). Plus: the end of the road is not "the 'duplication'
  of the human design… inside the electrical circuits of digital machines" (p. 44).
- pp. 45–47 **theory versus empiricism**: "First we had the steam engine, then
  thermodynamics, just as first we had the airplane and then flight theory"; "whereas a dynamo
  can be built through trial and error, an atomic reactor cannot" (p. 45); and the forecast
  that what is left "requires the highest clarity of theoretical thinking—is still ahead of
  us" (p. 47).
- pp. 48–49 **morality is not transhistorical**: "it is because technology constructs us and
  our views, including our views on morality"; moral judgements "depend first of all on their
  irreversibility"; "there is no system of trans-historical judgment".
- p. 51 **money versus speed**: twenty billion dollars put men on the Moon around 1969, but
  two hundred billion "certainly would not have landed on the Moon within six months" — there
  is a limit to technoevolution's speed "after which any further spending will not have any
  impact".
- pp. 55–58 **the naïve questions**: "Apparently it will only be possible to automatize
  noncreative mental labor. But where is the evidence? Let us be clear about it: there is
  none, and what is more, there can be none" (p. 56); automation "will not happen in the
  narrow sense of laborers and technicians being replaced by programmers of digital machines
  because future generations and future types of these machines will not need programmers
  anymore" (p. 57); and "Surely these intermediaries will be in some sense 'cleverer' than
  their human designer—but 'cleverer' does not yet mean 'rebellious'" (p. 57).

## 2-A · "The last lawnmower"

Grade the analogy that gives the chapter its title. Technologies, he says, live and die like
species — small copying prototypes, radiation into niches, gigantism before decline, lateral
branches surviving as relics — and he stakes it on datable cases. Test the ones he named
(zeppelin, radio, cinema, VTOL) and the one he got wrong in an instructive way: the car was
not replaced by an air-cushion vehicle, but the small combustion engine really is the last
holdout as electric drive takes the roads, so the *shape* of his prediction survived the
*content*. Then the harder question the frame raises: software does not go extinct. It
accretes, emulates and keeps backwards compatibility, which is one thing bioevolution cannot
do and his analogy has no room for.

## 2-B · "There is none, and there can be none"

The four pages of "Several Naïve Questions" contain the most 2026-facing sentences in the
first half of the book. He refuses the consolation that only non-creative mental work can be
automated — flatly, and on the ground that no evidence for it could exist. He predicts that
the replacement will not be programmers-for-labourers because the machines will stop needing
programmers. And he separates capability from motive in one clause — "'cleverer' does not
yet mean 'rebellious'" — which is precisely the inference the 2026 risk argument makes and
he declines. Test against generative models, coding assistants, the labour-market evidence
since 2023, and the alignment literature. Overlap risk: essay 4 owns Intelectronics and the
black box, so this must stay on *labour and motive*, not on capability.

## 2-C · "First the steam engine, then thermodynamics"

His sequence claim: practice runs ahead of theory only up to a point, and past that point —
his example is the atomic reactor — you cannot get there by trial and error. Sixty years on
the largest artifacts we build are built the old way: scaled empirically, with theory
trailing and no accepted account of why they work. Test the claim as he states it, including
the cases where he is right (nobody trial-and-errors a reactor or a vaccine platform).
Coordination: essay 5 already scored the Designer's pragmatism as an epistemology, so this
essay must stay on the *historical sequence* — does empiricism run out? — and not re-score
the same ground.

## 2-D · "Panevolutionism"

He rejects the cosmic-jackpot account of biogenesis and argues that self-organisation is
typical rather than exceptional, life being "only one possible enactment" of it. Test against
sixty years of origin-of-life work: RNA world, hydrothermal alkaline vents, protocell
synthesis, the dating of LUCA, and the fact that nobody has made life from scratch — plus
what exoplanet and biosignature work has and has not established. Coordination: essay 3 owns
the statistics of civilisations and the silence, so this stays on abiogenesis only. Risk: the
verdicts may be mostly OPEN, which essay 5 has already spent.
