# Essay 1 — Dilemmas (pp. 24–30) — pitches

The book's methodological preface: seven pages on why forecasts fail, written by someone about
to spend 350 pages forecasting. Notes ~354–355 (read before drafting).

Load-bearing passages:

- p. 25 **the premise**: "man hardly ever knows what he is actually doing—at least he does not
  know for sure", illustrated at the limit: "the destruction of Life on Earth, which is entirely
  possible today, was not actually the goal of any of the discoverers of atomic energy."
- p. 26 **the remedy he will not soften**: "The only way to deal with technology is with another
  technology. Man knows more about his dangerous tendencies than he did a hundred years ago, and
  in the next hundred years, his knowledge will be even more advanced. Then he will make use of
  it." Explicitly not "any promises… any assurances built on so-called humanism".
- p. 27 **graded forecasters**: Blackett's 1948 predictions on atomic weapons to 1960 were wrong
  "as inadequately as one could only imagine" — he thought warheads would not exceed kilotons
  because megatons "would not have any targets that would be worth destroying". And the reverse
  error: "around 1955, it was believed that the fusion of hydrogen into helium observed in the
  stars would generate industrial energy in the near future. Current estimates situate the
  production of the microfusion cell in the 1990s, or even later" (pp. 27–28).
- pp. 28–29 **the three failure modes**, his own taxonomy:
  1. sudden turns — transformations "sometimes burst forth like Athena from Zeus's head—to the
     surprise of everyone, including the experts" (cybernetics is his example);
  2. straight lines — "we are always inclined to extend the course of new technologies into the
     future by means of straight lines, hence 'the world full of balloons' or 'the total steam
     world'"; History "does not show us any straight paths of development but rather uses the
     curvilinear zigzags of nonlinear evolution";
  3. narrative closure — "a literary work has a beginning, a middle, and an end… we have a
     tendency to place any phenomenon within a closed schema" (p. 29).
- p. 29 **the sick man**: a 1930s thinker asked what happens when two halves of the world can
  annihilate each other answers "total annihilation or total disarmament". But "the world is like
  a sick man who believes that either he is going to get better soon or die very quickly and to
  whom it does not even occur that he can, moaning as he does… go on living until old age."
- p. 29 **the price of a cure**: "Liberation from oppression… has its price, while the amount of
  expected repayment and its period, as well as the method of its delivery, usually come as a
  surprise" — his example is radioactive waste, "which we still do not know what we should do
  about".
- p. 30 **the obsolescence of antagonism**: not just weapons and staffs are becoming obsolete,
  "so is the very idea of global antagonism. I have no idea how it is going to evolve."
- p. 26 **Bellamy** as the standing warning: a utopia that projected the present forward because
  its author "thought that changes caused by technoevolution were not significant".

## 1-A · "The three ways to be wrong"

Grade Lem's taxonomy of forecasting error against the only dataset that can test it fairly:
this book's own ledgers. He names three modes — the sudden turn, the straight line, the closed
schema — and the essays already written say which ones caught him. The air-cushion successor is
a straight line; the language models he never saw are a sudden turn; the unit of selection
moving from the couple to the embryo is a fourth mode he did not name. This is the essay that
sets up essay 0, and the only one in the book that can use the book as evidence.

## 1-B · "The sick man"

His best single image (p. 29) and his most testable claim: that the two dramatic endings people
demand of history — annihilation or disarmament — are both narrative artifacts, and the real
outcome is chronic survival. Test against what actually happened to the balance of fear: no
annihilation, no disarmament, arsenals cut by most of their peak and then modernised, treaties
lapsed, the thing simply continuing. Then ask whether the same schema is running now in
arguments about AI, where the available endings are again two.

## 1-C · "The microfusion cell"

The self-referential essay. In the middle of a chapter mocking failed forecasts — Blackett on
kilotons, the utopians on steam — Lem dates industrial fusion to the 1990s "or even later", and
notes that radioactive waste is a problem "we still do not know what we should do about". Sixty
years later fusion is still not on the grid and the waste is still in pools. The chapter is
therefore its own best exhibit, and the essay writes itself as a demonstration that knowing the
failure modes does not protect you from them.

## 1-D · "Another technology"

His flat refusal of humanism as a remedy: "The only way to deal with technology is with another
technology" (p. 26), with the confidence that knowledge of our dangerous tendencies will grow
and be used. Test against how the 2026 argument about dangerous technology is actually
organised — technical safety work versus treaties, norms and appeals to restraint — and against
the one case with a sixty-year record, nuclear weapons, where the answer was arms control rather
than a counter-technology. Coordination: essay 4 owns the regulator and its failures.
