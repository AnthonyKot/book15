### 1. The frame says Lem priced conversation; the chapter and the essay’s own “worst” paragraph say he did not

**Location:** Meta description: “on a channel he priced as the hard one: conversation.” Lede: “on a channel he priced as the expensive one.” What happened: “The detection game got played anyway, on the channel he priced as expensive.” Worst: “He priced the wrong thing. He measured a fake by the complexity of the thing faked … and did not price the *channel*.”

**Problem:** These cannot both be true. On p. 206 he ranks two *phantomats* — a travel device that stages a saucer landing inside a wired-in world versus a “human-producing” device that adds an autonomous interlocutor *in that same world*. He is ranking the complexity of the thing faked, not the cost of a conversational channel. The essay now says so in “What Lem said” (“He is ranking cost, not date”) and in the OPEN row. The title, lede, metadata, and the first clause of “What happened” still convert that ranking into “he priced conversation as expensive,” which is the comparison the last panel told this draft to stop making. The arm’s-length disclaimer sits in the same sentence as the misreading.

**Fix:** Make the frame match the worst paragraph. He did not price the channel. The 2026 fact is that a cheap textual channel is where the detection game is being lost; that is an analogy the essay is drawing, not a ranking he made. Rewrite the meta, lede, and the “priced as expensive” opener so they say that.

### 4. Bostrom is not a HIT on p. 207

**Location:** Problem-level row: that one is in the real world “can always be only probable … but never absolutely certain” (p. 207) → HIT, because “The simulation argument (Bostrom 2003) formalises the same conclusion.”

**Problem:** It does not. Lem’s conclusion is a brain-in-a-vat result: if a phantomat can close every test, certainty is impossible. Bostrom’s conclusion is an empirical trilemma about ancestor-simulation fractions, and he says so: the simulation argument “is fundamentally different from these traditional philosophical arguments,” and “the conclusion of the simulation argument is not simply that we cannot be certain that we are not living in a simulation” ([Simulation Argument FAQ](https://simulation-argument.com/faq/), Q3). A live skeptical neighbour is not “the same conclusion.” HIT here credits a 1964 detection argument with a 2003 population argument.

**Fix:** Score p. 207 as OPEN or as a problem-level HIT only against the live *skeptical* literature (BIV / evil demon / “can you tell?”), and describe Bostrom as a related but different argument. Do not say he formalised the same conclusion.

### 5. Teletaxy RA/WR launders a missing object with a live constraint

**Problem:** The object is a nerve-linked double whose receptors and motor nerves are yours (p. 222). Telesurgery is hands, screens, and a robot. Latency as the binding constraint is a real problem-level point; it is being used to pay for an object that was not built. The same stretch, milder, is in the neck-muscle row: head-tracked re-rendering is a real functional cousin of p. 202, but RA/WR at object level still treats “view follows head” as the thing he described.

**Fix:** Split teletaxy. Object-level: nerve-wired remote-I → EARLY or OPEN (no dated nerve-link path). Problem-level: light-speed delay bounds telepresence → HIT. For neck muscles, either keep RA/WR and say the *scored object* is “the view must change with intended head motion,” or drop to EARLY on the nerve-side loop.

### 7. Three 2026-side overstatements, one of them unmarked as its own claim

**Location / problem:**

- “the first commercial implants read motor intent out — Neuralink’s first human participant” (`neuralink-2024`). Neuralink’s N1 is an investigational device under IDE, not a commercial implant. Neuralink says so.
- “the latency budget he computed for the Moon is the one operators still work to” (`lindbergh-2001`). Lem’s Moon figure is ~1 s each way (p. 222). Published telesurgery tolerances are on the order of 100–200 ms, with ~300 ms as a ceiling. Operators work to a *tighter* budget; the Moon is still too far. The Lindbergh marker receipts a 2001 operation, not this sentence.
- Ledger: “First vitrified mammalian organ rewarmed and transplanted, 2023.” False. Fahy et al., *Organogenesis* 2009, report a vitrified rabbit kidney that supported life after transplant. Han et al. 2023 is the first reproducible *rat* nanowarmed case with long-term normal function, which is what the TSV actually claims.

**Fix:** “Investigational implant, 2024, motor read-out.” Cut or invert the latency sentence (the Moon figure is still the bound that keeps closed-loop surgery Earth-local). In the ledger, “reproducible long-term vitrified-kidney transplant, 2023; Fahy 2009 was a single impaired rabbit case; no whole mammal.” Mark the latency claim or delete it.

### 8. Guess 2023 is not a test of p. 213 + p. 217, and “best 2026 evidence” is overstated

**Location:** “Better than us”: Lem cannot determine attitudes *directly* (p. 213) *and* prolonged staged exposure can change a deep-rooted opinion (p. 217). “That is roughly where the best 2026 evidence sits,” then Guess et al., *Science* 2023 (three months of chronological Facebook/Instagram feeds; attitudes did not move), tagged “Short exposure, formed adults, no direct write.”

**Problem:** Three months is the duration Lem would call prolonged, not short. Guess changed *ranking*, not a staged sequence of identical experiences. It can support the “no direct write” half; it does not confirm the pair, and it sits awkwardly against p. 217 (prolonged maneuvers *can* move opinion). A 2026 field experiment on X reported that switching *to* an algorithmic feed *did* shift political opinions ([Nature, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10098-2)). “Best 2026 evidence” is a selection.

**Fix:** Use Guess only for “short/indirect ranking change did not move formed adults,” or drop it. Do not claim it locates the best current evidence on Lem’s two-part claim.
