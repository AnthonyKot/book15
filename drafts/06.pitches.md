# Essay 6 — Phantomology (2013 tr., pp. 197–236) — pitches, 2026-08-16

Read in full. Four candidate angles; the user picks one (or a merge). Unpicked are banked.

## A. The order came out backwards
Anchor: "a 'travel' phantomat and a 'human-producing' one are two different kinds of devices.
It is incomparably more difficult to construct the second one than the first" (p. 206); "it is
easier to imitate a Miss World than Einstein. In the latter case, the machine would have to
have the complexity, or intelligence, that would match the brain of a genius" (p. 203); and
"neither a phantomatic machine nor any programs for it exist" (p. 202). 2026: the perfect
travel phantomat still does not exist (headsets plateaued; presence, accommodation and the
"eye-freedom" problem he names on p. 199 are still unsolved), while the human-producing
device — a conversational partner good enough to be mistaken for one — arrived first, cheaply.
Tension: HIT that the human-producer is the pivotal device; MISS on which is harder; and his
proof that "the easiest way to debunk a vision is through similar details" (p. 206) now runs
against chatbots, not headsets.

## B. "Can you tell?" — the squats, the drawer, and the neurotics
Anchor: the reality tests — a hundred squats and lactic acid (p. 205), the drawer that sticks
(p. 206), the wife who "laughs pityingly" (p. 206) — ending in "only probable, sometimes highly
probable, but never absolutely certain" (p. 207); the prediction that "psychiatrists would
still see various neurotics ... haunted by obsessions of a new type — the fear that what they
are experiencing is not true" (p. 204); "a phantomatic world is a world of total solitude"
(p. 207). 2026: the simulation argument (Bostrom 2003) as a philosophy and celebrity
industry; deepfakes turning "can you tell?" from a nerve problem into a media problem;
derealization/"AI psychosis" reporting. Tension: problem-level HIT with receipts forty years
early; object level (input via the nerves) EARLY at best — BCIs mostly read, don't write.

## C. Central phantomatics: the wirehead and the attention economy
Anchor: Olds & Milner's rat at "eight thousand times per hour" (p. 210), "central
masturbation", a "technology of facilitated ecstasy" that "cannot in any case be considered an
'art'" (p. 210); "no civilization can become 'fully phantomatized'" — that would be "a
pleasant suicide of a civilization" (pp. 207–208); the entertainment role "may turn out to be
rather marginal" next to training, medicine, the blind (p. 215). 2026: "wireheading" is a
term of art in alignment; engagement-optimised feeds are peripheral phantomatics tuned by a
black box (link to essay 4); Nozick's experience machine (1974) came a decade after Lem.
Tension: he predicted the device and rejected the outcome; the outcome arrived by a route he
didn't rate (peripheral, not central) — RIGHT QUESTION, WRONG ANSWER on "marginal
entertainment", OPEN on "no civilization can".

## D. Personality and information — the teletransporter, twenty years before Parfit
Anchor: telegraphing Mr. Smith, the hammer, "Smith is going to be dead, forever and ever"
(pp. 225–229); the corpus-callosum Smiths (p. 227); the frozen-atoms Smith no. 2 (p. 232);
"individual identity stops being a phenomenon to be investigated and becomes a phenomenon
to be defined" (p. 224); cerebromatics as murder in small steps, "removing a few blades of
grass from a haystack" (p. 219). 2026: mind-uploading and whole-brain-emulation debates,
cryonics as an industry, "griefbots"/digital doubles, split-brain research; Parfit's
teletransporter (Reasons and Persons, 1984) is Lem's thought experiment with a bibliography.
Tension: OPEN by construction — but a HIT on the problem level with a philosophy receipt,
and the essay can show 2026 has not moved his argument an inch, which is itself a finding.
