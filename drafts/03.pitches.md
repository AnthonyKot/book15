# Essay 3 — Civilizations in the Universe (pp. 59–92) — pitches

Sections: The Formulation of the Problem (59) · The Formulation of the Method (61) · The
Statistics of Civilizations in the Universe (64) · A Catastrophic Theory of the Universe (67) ·
A Metatheory of Miracles (70) · Man's Uniqueness (73) · Intelligence: An Accident or a
Necessity? (77) · Hypotheses (80) · Votum Separatum (83) · Future Prospects (87). Notes ~358–360.

Load-bearing passages:

- p. 62 **the 1964 instrument limit**: astronomy "is incapable of determining, either directly
  (e.g., in a visual way) or indirectly, the existence of planets around stars—unless they are
  the nearest stars, while the mass of those planets' bodies exceeds that of Jupiter."
- p. 63 **the premise everything rests on**: Bracewell and von Hoerner start "from the premise
  that only 1 out of 150 stars in our Galaxy has planets"; 150 billion stars → ~1 billion
  planetary systems; if civilizations last as long as their suns, "the average distance between
  two civilizations would be less than ten light-years."
- pp. 64–67 **the silence**: Drake at Green Bank from 1960; "Day after day, week after week,
  their instruments registered nothing but the monotonous cosmic noise generated by dead
  matter" (p. 64). Von Hoerner's ladder of lifespans down to "less than twenty thousand years",
  a thousand light-years apart. Then the question: "Why, in other words, are we seeing no
  'miracles'?" (p. 67).
- p. 67 **the rejection**: von Hoerner gives self-destruction a 65% chance; Lem: "I cannot
  accept such a statistical hell—not because it is too terrifying but because it is too naïve."
- pp. 70–73 **the metatheory of miracles**: technetium in stellar spectra as a marker; then
  Occam's razor, which "inexorably forbids us to accept such hypotheses" — and the sting: the
  scientist "may have actually been seeing it for a while, yet he is banned from classifying it
  as a separate phenomenon… by the very science to which he is in service" (p. 72). Plus the
  anthropocentrism charge: "we are not looking for 'any civilizations' but predominantly for
  anthropomorphic ones" (p. 73), and "There is something deeply saddening in the silence of the
  stars… a silence so absolute that it seems eternal" (p. 73).
- pp. 73–76 **against rare Earth**: Baumsztejn's dice model fails because evolution "is more
  like a river that avoids obstacles by changing its course" (p. 75) — it proves only the
  improbability of repeating *Earth's* evolution.
- pp. 77–80 **is intelligence necessary?**: insects are three-quarters of the animal kingdom
  and produced no intelligence, so "probability calculus is not a determining criterion in
  psychogenesis"; psychogenesis is "possible yet not inevitable" (pp. 79–80).
- pp. 82–83 **his own answer, hypothesis III**: civilizations are frequent and long-lived but
  not orthoevolutionary — only the exponential phase is short, "less than twenty thousand
  years", after which "the dynamic characteristics of the developmental process change" without
  self-destruction or degeneracy.
- pp. 85–86 **forms of Intelligence**: a world with disturbance beyond its regulatory capacity
  may get "biological technology prior to physical technology" — "a planned and directed
  autoevolution, a kind of 'adaptation retreat'" — which would be invisible to us.
- pp. 89–90 **the practicalities**: star flight needs annihilating roughly the mass of the Moon;
  the search "would require us to construct a great amount of equipment… more substantial than
  the current investment in research on nuclear energy", and scientists "still need to
  'cultivate' a generation of rulers who will be inclined to dip deeply enough into the public
  coffers"; and the compression argument — an efficiently coded message "starts to resemble
  noise", so we may already be receiving conversation and calling it background.
- p. 90 **the Dyson sphere**: "What has so far been put forward as a 'model' of such a
  'miracle,' for example, a Dyson sphere, is most likely not being built anywhere."

## 3-A · "Sixty years of silence"

The chapter's own question, graded. He asks why we see no miracles, and he asks it from a
premise that has since been inverted: he worked from one star in 150 having planets, and we now
know planets are ordinary — which makes the silence louder, not quieter. Test the specific
markers he named (Dyson spheres, technetium lines), the searches that ran, and his own answer,
hypothesis III: that the exponential phase is short but the civilization is not. That answer is
still untestable, and the live answers in 2026 are different ones. The essay the chapter is
asking for, and the one with the most datable receipts in the book.

## 3-B · "The razor and the megastructure"

His sharpest methodological claim (pp. 70–73): Occam's razor forbids the astronomer from
calling an anomaly artificial, so a civilization may already be in the data, unclassifiable by
the discipline that found it. Test against the one case where astronomy genuinely entertained a
megastructure and then talked itself down, against the argument over 'Oumuamua, and against the
technosignature criteria the field has since written to handle exactly this problem. Where he
is better than us: he saw that the taboo is a methodological choice with a cost, not a neutral
default. Risk: leaves the chapter's main question mostly unaddressed.

## 3-C · "Not looking for any civilization"

The anthropomorphism thread (p. 73) run through to its conclusion in "Votum Separatum" (pp.
83–86): that we search for multiplications of ourselves, and that a civilization facing
disturbance beyond its regulatory capacity might turn inward instead — biological technology
before physical, "a planned and directed autoevolution, a kind of 'adaptation retreat'" — and be
invisible for that reason. Test against how far technosignature searches actually broadened.
Coordination: essay 8 owns autoevolution on Earth; this stays on its cosmic form.

## 3-D · "Who pays for the telescope"

The unglamorous thread (pp. 89–90). He predicts the search needs money beyond nuclear research
and a generation of rulers willing to spend it; test that against what actually happened to
public SETI funding and where the money finally came from. Add the compression argument — an
optimally coded signal is indistinguishable from noise — which is now standard and shapes what
the searches look for. Risk: the narrowest of the four, and it would leave the silence itself
to a later essay.
