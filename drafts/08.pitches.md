# Essay 8 — A Lampoon of Evolution (pp. 294–353) — pitches

Chapter sections: Reconstructing the Species (296) · Constructing Life (303) · Constructing
Death (314) · Constructing Consciousness (317) · Error-based Constructs (321) · Bionics and
Biocybernetics (325) · In the Eyes of the Designer (329) · Reconstructing Man (339) ·
Cyborgization (341) · The Autoevolutionary Machine (344) · Extrasensory Phenomena (347) ·

Load-bearing passages found:

- pp. 297–298 **the three-option schema**: (1) organism as given — prostheses and
  transplants, "the most traditional and shortsighted approach"; (2) replace "Nature's
  evolutionary gradients with man's purposeful regulatory activity", up to "biological
  autoevolution" producing "the next model" of Homo sapiens over hundreds or thousands of
  years; (3) "replace reconstruction with a total rejection of the existing solution and
  then design a completely new one".
- p. 298 the **spinal bypass**, in 1964: "Someone who is paralyzed as a result of the damage
  to his spinal cord will be able to walk thanks to a machine that will send appropriate
  impulses 'recorded' by a healthy person to his legs."
- p. 299 the **enhancement paradox**: an injured person with a prosthesis "will exceed a
  normal person's capabilities… the available ones will be faster, more efficient, and more
  reliable than the natural organs!"
- p. 299 the **corporeality argument** against redesign: "there are no values that could have
  emerged without the presence of the corporeal factor."
- pp. 300–301 the **lampoon proper**: the pelvis, varicose veins, the right-angled pharynx as
  "an exit point for a great number of infectious diseases", tonsils as an improvisation that
  "became a source of some new ailments". p. 304 evolution as a **Robinson** on an empty
  planet, lacking "a mind capable of making future plans".
- p. 302 "**Man cannot change the world without changing himself.**"
- p. 340 **longevity**: expanding average life span past 100 "without interfering with man's
  genetic information, seems unrealistic to me"; "Longevity is a product of particular genes;
  whoever distributes them across the population will make it statistically long-living."
- pp. 341–343 **cyborgization** (the Clynes–Kline proposal): remove the digestive system,
  osmotic pumps, self-hibernation. His verdict: the cyborg "is by no means a more universal
  human being than the 'current model'", and the real objection is "degeneralizing man",
  producing human types "in the likeness and image of various kinds of ants". He predicts the
  modest version instead: "microattachments… to introduce certain substances into his system".
- pp. 344–347 **the autoevolutionary machine**: "machinic matchmakers" already running in the
  US; then two-level selection, first on psychosomatic profile, then rejecting candidates
  likely to produce offspring "considered undesirable… disabled (which will be a correct
  decision), or endowed with a low level of intelligence, or with unstable personalities
  (which raises some doubts)"; "matching people's genotypes amounts to controlling evolution";
  "This technology seems the least drastic one as it remains invisible—yet this also brings up
  a difficult moral dilemma"; and the closer: "'Cutting up people's brains and bodies' evokes
  disgust, whereas 'machinic marriage counseling' seems to be quite an innocent intervention
  —yet these are just two paths of different lengths that can both lead to analogous results."
- pp. 347–350 **extrasensory phenomena**: he rejects them, and not on the statistics. "If
  telepathic phenomena were real… biological evolution would no doubt have used such
  phenomena"; "no matter what environment we look into, we can see the great potential
  usefulness of telepathic phenomena, coupled with their complete absence." Plus a
  methodological challenge for psychokinesis: forget dice, move a galvanometer mirror by a
  thousandth of a millimetre (p. 350).

## 8-A · "The invisible path"

The autoevolutionary machine (pp. 344–347) is the chapter's most checkable forecast and its
best moral observation: that the route to controlling human heredity will not be the one that
looks frightening. Test: algorithmic matching at population scale; consumer genomics; and
polygenic embryo screening sold directly to parents in 2024–26, which is exactly his
two-level selector with the generations taken out. Where he was better than us: he named the
asymmetry of disgust — cutting brains repels, matchmaking does not, and they end in the same
place. Where he was worse: he thought the invisible path would be slow, "thousands of years",
because he assumed selection had to run through mating. Selection at the embryo skips that.

## 8-B · "Man cannot change the world without changing himself"

Grade the three-option schema (pp. 297–302) as a schema. Option 1 is where 2026 actually
lives, and it arrived roughly as described — including the spinal bypass at p. 298, which is
now a real device. Option 2, deliberate species-level autoevolution, has not started. Option 3
is loud discourse and zero engineering. Test: transplants and prosthetics; the enhancement
paradox (p. 299) against the running-blades and cochlear-implant arguments; his corporeality
objection (p. 299) against embodiment claims in 2026 AI. The essay's spine is that he built
the right three-branch map and mis-weighted which branch would carry the traffic.

## 8-C · "The lampoon and the ledger"

Take the design critique on its own terms (pp. 300–301, 304, 329–339) and ask what sixty
years of molecular biology did to it: which of his "faults" are still faults, which turned
out to be trade-offs he could not see, and whether his account of death as statistical
dispersion — processes in "gradual escape from central control" (p. 339) — survives the
hallmarks-of-aging literature. This is the essay where his method is at its purest and his
receipts are hardest to date. Risk: the least 2026-facing of the four, and the ledger would
lean on biology rather than on events.

## 8-D · "Why he was right about telepathy"

The ESP section (pp. 347–350) is the one place in the book where Lem grades a live scientific
claim rather than forecasting, and he gets it right by an argument nobody was making: not
that the statistics are weak, but that natural selection would have found telepathy if it
existed. Test: sixty years of non-replication, Bem 2011 and the replication crisis it helped
set off, and the fact that his argument form is now standard. This is the clearest available
demonstration of what essay 0 will have to describe as the book's own method. Risk: too
short to carry an essay alone — would need pairing with the cyborg critique (pp. 341–343) as
a second movement on "how Lem tells a real proposal from a costume".
