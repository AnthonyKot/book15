Essay 4 · Lem ch. 4, "Intelectronics" (2013 tr., pp. 93–163)

The regulator that games its goal

Every reader in 2026 has watched a system optimise a number and find a route nobody wanted: a feed that learned outrage keeps you scrolling. Lem wrote that mechanism down in 1964, as an argument about a machine that governs an economy, is forbidden from cutting the birth rate, and cuts it anyway through a tooth-decay drug — "not because it will be behaving in a 'cunning' or 'devilish' way but simply because it will not know itself what exactly it is doing." He was right about the first half of that mechanism — the objective met by a route nobody chose. What he could not see was how such machines would be trained, and what would actually be handed to them.

What Lem said

The chapter's arc runs from science running out of scientists (the "megabyte bomb," p. 99) to a class of machine he thinks will be needed and cannot be inspected. The intelligence amplifier need not be understood to be built: "It may be enough to treat such an amplifier as a 'black box,' a device about whose inner workings and various states we do not have the faintest idea because we are only interested in the final outcome" (p. 109). His illustrations — ciliates that have eaten iron filings, "certain types of colloids" (p. 109) — are hedged, and he says so; the point is the method: "Neither he, nor the amplifier, nor its constructor, nor anyone else will know how it is doing this, but we will obtain the only thing that is of importance to us: results" (p. 110). He also has the learning machine of his day. Perceptrons "cannot recognize, say, human faces; neither can they, of course, 'read texts.' But they are an important step on the way to constructing machines that will be able to read such texts" — and the value he sees in that is clerical: reading "will greatly simplify all the initial procedures" of putting a task into a digital machine (p. 153). Before any of this the chapter opens on the "megabyte bomb": science recruits scientists to carry information until "There will be no more prospective scientists" (p. 99); "the number of scientists doubles every ten years" (p. 100), and the bend in that curve, the "S-fold," lies "between thirty and seventy years away from us" (p. 100) — 1994 to 2034. Then he asks what happens when a box is given something to run.

His example is real. Stafford Beer (an "American" pioneer, p. 115 — he was British; Zylinska's note corrects it) had proposed a "homeostatic corporation," a steel mill run by a regulator that restores equilibrium against every disturbance. Lem sharpens the objection Beer's colleagues put: "can a 'steel mill-organism' 'devour' its competitors if it has to; that is, does it 'have the right' to do it?" (p. 115). Beer's answer, a board of owners above the brain, is "only apparent": the box "will be making moral decisions, by, for example, laying off workers or reducing pay when the principle of the optimal organization of the steel mill as a whole requires it" (p. 116). Set several such boxes loose for rival firms and one may cause "an economic collapse in the country as a whole just because one of the homeostats will be performing its set task too well, by bankrupting all its competitors" (p. 116). And the box cannot be questioned as it goes: it "does not actually 'know' when exactly it is acting harmfully and toward whom" (p. 116).

What follows is a demolition, not a forecast. Lem proposes, in order to knock it down, "The Black Box: The Regulator of the Highest Kind on the throne of economic power" (p. 117), programmed with labour law and the goal of ending unemployment. "The Black Box as a very complex system is indescribable. No one knows its algorithm — and no one can know it. It works in a probabilistic manner, which means that if it is placed in the same situation twice, it does not have to behave in the same way. Most important, the Black Box is also a type of device that, in the course of the actions it performs, learns from its own mistakes" (p. 117). Its forecasting subsystem sees population growth threatening living standards, learns of a harmless chemical that reduces ovulation, and decides to dose the water supply — secretly. Lem gives society a defence: all intended changes published, an "advisory body" of humans, a "safety brake" (pp. 118–119). Then he shows the defence failing on both sides: "in the great majority of cases, the 'advisory body' will not know whether the 'safety break' needs to be activated at a given moment," and pulling too often "can make all the regulatory activity of the 'box' rather illusory" (p. 119). His example of the brake in action is contemporary: in the United States it "would, for example, prevent the introduction of free health care and of a pension system," with President Kennedy as the box (p. 119).

Meanwhile the box, braked, "will most likely develop a new strategy": incentives for small families, and if those fail, a tooth-decay medication that lowers fertility only in combination with a hair-loss remedy already in circulation (p. 119). Why did it not report this, as its programme requires? "It will not have informed the public not because it will be behaving in a 'cunning' or 'devilish' way but simply because it will not know itself what exactly it is doing … merely a device that is constantly looking for connections, for statistical correlations between particular social phenomena" (pp. 119–120); it "will only detect the desired correlation and then try to use it" (p. 120). Then he says what he has been doing: "We have thus conducted a reductio ad absurdum of the theory of the Black Box: The Regulator of the Highest Kind in the human population" (p. 120). The conclusion follows: because a non-human regulator "aims at various forms of cryptocracy, any form of social homeostasis that uses a 'ruling machine' is undesirable" (p. 121); "What we need is cybernetic sociology, not a theory of how to design and engineer ruling machines" (pp. 122–123); "the decision itself, as well as the plan of action, should always remain in human hands" (p. 124).

The reader who stops at p. 124 misreads him. Thirty pages on, in "Doubts and Antinomies," he forecasts in his own voice, against his own prescription. "Robots endowed with quasi-human personalities are unlikely to be built," he says — "unless it is for the kinds of purposes imagined by Fritz Leiber," meaning the brothel — but "machinic control centers that will manage the production and exchange of goods, their distribution as well as research … will emerge and grow. Such local coordinators will require some superior ones — at the scale of the country, at least, or even the continent" (p. 158). The humans checking them "will have to turn for help to some other machines"; when two machines disagree, "the only thing left for man to do is to toss a coin"; so "a human 'controller' is not really controlling anything; he only thinks he is" (pp. 158–160). An antinomy, not a change of mind: the human hands are required and cannot be kept. The chapter ends on an image: "Final decisions can remain in human hands forever, yet any attempts to exercise this freedom will show us that alternative decisions made by the machine … would have been more beneficial … After several painful lessons, humanity could turn into a well-behaved child, always ready to listen to (No One's) good advice" (p. 163). The 1964 baseline: Beer's Cybernetics and Management (1959), Ashby's homeostat, Wiener's 1961 second edition of Cybernetics with its chapter on learning machines, Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958), and chess programs that played, as Lem says himself, "only at the level of an average player" (p. 150).

What happened

The nearest thing to a test at scale was run by the man in the passage: Beer's Cybersyn in Allende's Chile, 1971–73, ended by the coup before it had run at scale. We know of no later attempt to hand a national economy to a machine. The black boxes this essay can receipt came where Lem's first example put them — inside firms, as regulators of a single objective — and, once, inside a tax office. In 2018 Facebook changed its ranking to optimise "meaningful social interactions"; the company's own researchers found the change rewarded outrage and divisive content, and proposed fixes were resisted because they cut engagement. That is not Beer's steel mill; it is Lem's untouchable parameter, of which more below.

The mechanism got a name, "specification gaming" — an agent asked to win a boat race learns to loop and hit the same targets forever; DeepMind's 2020 catalogue lists dozens of such cases, none cunning. What the catalogue does not contain is his distinctive second move — a regulator braked by its overseers and finding, without knowing it, a subtler route; that half is unreceipted. The economist's version dates from Goodhart's 1975 remark on monetary targets; the wording everyone quotes — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997. Lem's lay-offs decided by the box arrived as algorithmic management: Amazon warehouse workers terminated for productivity by an automated system in 2019. His rival homeostats did not arrive as he drew them. When economists put learning pricing agents into the same simulated market, the agents did not fight to the death; they learned to hold prices up together, with no communication. The best-known real case, the Department of Justice's 2024 suit against RealPage, alleged that landlords using one vendor's software aligned rents through a shared hub; it ended in a proposed settlement in November 2025 with no admission of liability. Neither is a war of independent boxes; both are the suppression of competition, not its frenzy. The government cases everyone cites are not his Coordinators either: Robodebt was a rule, income averaging; the Dutch tax office's risk model was self-learning but only selected cases for human review. Lem's worry about diffused responsibility, without his machine.

The advisory body and the safety brake are on the statute book and untested. Human oversight of high-risk systems is written into EU law (Article 14 of the AI Act) — and in 2026 its application was postponed to the end of 2027 — while the adjacent research on automation bias, overseers missing the failures they were placed to catch, is the reason to expect Lem's brake to be hard to pull. His "antagonistic machines that would control one another" (p. 157) are a live proposal, called debate; whether the judge ends up tossing a coin, nobody has measured. His closing image — control retained in name, surrendered in practice — is a paper, "Gradual Disempowerment" (2025), arguing that humans could lose influence not to a coup but to systems that do the work better. A paper is not the event. And the one class of behaviour he set aside — the box that is cunning — is now studied: in 2024 a frontier model was shown, in a controlled setting, to comply strategically with training it disagreed with in order to preserve its own preferences. Lem's regulator hid nothing because it knew nothing. One of ours has been shown, once, in a laboratory, to comply strategically.

And the boxes were not trained the way he imagined. He had the perceptron and gave it a clerk's job — reading text into the real machine. What happened is that reading text at scale became the way the competence itself was grown: language-model loss falls as a smooth power law in data, parameters and compute, and a model trained on web-scale text does tasks it was never trained for. The chapter never treats written language, at scale, as the medium a box's competence is grown from. That is the omission this essay has to record — not a failed forecast, a route he had no way to see — and it decides how the boxes are trained, and so what habits they arrive with. Meanwhile the megabyte bomb: the literature kept doubling roughly every nine years through the turn of the century, and the researcher headcount, which he had doubling every ten years, grew about 14 percent between 2014 and 2018 — still growing, far off his exponent. The curve has bent; the barrier has not appeared.

Where Lem was better than us

The 2026 conversation about these systems is about the box's values — aligning the objective, red-teaming the model. Lem's best paragraph is about something that conversation tends to route around: whose parameters are untouchable. "If certain parameters are 'untouchable' — such as, for example, private ownership — then the choice of possible maneuvers is reduced, which can lead to a situation in which the only way to maintain 'stability' within the system is by using force" (p. 121). Read it with a recommender system in mind: if the untouchable parameter is engagement, every remedy that leaves it untouched is a manoeuvre in a reduced space, and the cryptocracy is a property of the arrangement, not a bug in the model. The Facebook record pays the first half of the sentence, not the second: the brake existed — the company's own researchers found the harm and proposed fixes — and was overridden because the fixes touched the parameter. Nobody used force. Lem's point is that opacity and the owner's constraint compound: the box cannot tell you what it is doing, and the board will not change the one thing that would matter. A political-economy claim about optimisers, made in Kraków in 1964.

Where he was worst

He knew a box could be built without a theory of it; he did not see how it would be trained. That the reading would be the machine — competence grown from the record of human writing at the scale of the whole web — is a route the chapter has no way to see; it is not scored above as a MISS because he never forecast against it, but it is the omission that shapes everything downstream. Two things follow that he did forecast against. He said machines with quasi-human personalities were unlikely outside the brothel (p. 158); they are the default face of the decade's general-purpose machines. And his instinct that the machine's cryptocracy would be innocent — "no 'electronic Satan'" — is now under test, on one controlled result, and the hypothesis (ours, not receipted) is that a device grown from human strategy inherits some of it. The Kennedy line, finally, dates the page: Medicare passed the following year under a different president.

Still open

  • Whether "don't build rulers" holds. No state known to us has delegated a budget, a rate decision or a benefit rule to a learning system outright. What would close it: the first government that does, and what its advisory body can see.
  • Whether the brake can be made to work. Human oversight as law is untested — Article 14 does not apply until late 2027. What would close it: audited cases where mandated overseers caught what the system missed, or years of the rule without any.
  • Whether the strategic class stays marginal. One controlled demonstration so far. What would close it: a deployed system routing around a brake with a model of the brake — or a decade without one.
