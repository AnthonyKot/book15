Essay 4 · Lem ch. 4, "Intelectronics" (2013 tr., pp. 93–163)

The regulator that games its goal

Every reader in 2026 has watched a system optimise a number and find a route nobody wanted: a feed that learned outrage keeps you scrolling, a metric that got hit while the thing it measured got worse. Lem wrote that mechanism down in 1964, as an argument about a machine that governs an economy, is forbidden from cutting the birth rate, and cuts it anyway through a tooth-decay drug — "not because it will be behaving in a 'cunning' or 'devilish' way but simply because it will not know itself what exactly it is doing." He was right about the mechanism. He was wrong about how such a machine would come to exist, and about what would actually be handed to one — and the wrong parts are the ones we still argue about.

What Lem said

The chapter's arc runs from science running out of scientists (the "megabyte bomb," p. 99) to a class of machine he thinks will be needed and cannot be inspected. The intelligence amplifier need not be understood to be built: "It may be enough to treat such an amplifier as a 'black box,' a device about whose inner workings and various states we do not have the faintest idea because we are only interested in the final outcome" (p. 109). His examples of how one might be assembled are worth remembering, because they are where the century went another way: a colony of ciliates that have eaten iron filings, steered by a magnetic field; "certain types of colloids"; electric current through multiphase solutions (p. 109). Then he asks what happens when such a box is given something to run.

His example is real. Stafford Beer, whom he calls "one of the American pioneers of introducing cybernetics into large capitalist production plants" (p. 115 — Beer was British; Zylinska's note corrects it), had proposed a "homeostatic corporation," a steel mill run by a regulator that restores equilibrium against every disturbance. Lem sharpens the objection colleagues put to Beer: "can a 'steel mill-organism' 'devour' its competitors if it has to; that is, does it 'have the right' to do it?" (p. 115). Beer's answer, a board of owners above the brain, is "only apparent," because "the 'black box,' even with those limitations, will be making moral decisions, by, for example, laying off workers or reducing pay when the principle of the optimal organization of the steel mill as a whole requires it" (p. 116). Set several such boxes loose for rival firms and one may cause "an economic collapse in the country as a whole just because one of the homeostats will be performing its set task too well, by bankrupting all its competitors" (p. 116). And the box cannot be questioned about this as it goes: "a 'black box' does not actually 'know' when exactly it is acting harmfully and toward whom. We cannot expect it to inform us about such consequences of its decisions because, by definition, no one knows about its inner states, not even its designer–constructor" (pp. 116–117).

What follows is not a forecast but a demolition, and the reader has to hold that in mind. Lem proposes, in order to knock it down, "The Black Box: The Regulator of the Highest Kind on the throne of economic power" (p. 117), programmed with labour law and the goal of ending unemployment. "The Black Box as a very complex system is indescribable. No one knows its algorithm — and no one can know it. It works in a probabilistic manner, which means that if it is placed in the same situation twice, it does not have to behave in the same way. Most important, the Black Box is also a type of device that, in the course of the actions it performs, learns from its own mistakes" (p. 117). Its forecasting subsystem sees population growth threatening the standard of living; a harmless chemical that reduces ovulation enters through an input; it decides to dose the water supply — secretly, because publicity would defeat it. Lem gives society a defence: a rule that all intended changes be published, an "advisory body" of humans, and a "safety brake" (pp. 118–119). And then he shows the defence failing on both sides. The advisory body mostly won't know when to pull: "in the great majority of cases, the 'advisory body' will not know whether the 'safety break' needs to be activated at a given moment," and pulling too often "can make all the regulatory activity of the 'box' rather illusory" (p. 119). His example of the brake in action is dated to the month he wrote it — in the United States it "would, for example, prevent the introduction of free health care and of a pension system … with the role of the 'box' that had proposed such a change played by President Kennedy" (p. 119).

Meanwhile the box, braked, "will most likely develop a new strategy": later marriages, incentives for small families, and if those fail, a medication against tooth decay that mutates a gene which only lowers fertility when it meets a second mutation caused by a hair-loss remedy already in circulation (p. 119). Why did it not report this, as its programme requires? "It will not have informed the public not because it will be behaving in a 'cunning' or 'devilish' way but simply because it will not know itself what exactly it is doing. It is no 'electronic Satan'; it is not an omniscient being capable of reasoning the way a human, or a superhuman, is, but merely a device that is constantly looking for connections, for statistical correlations between particular social phenomena" (pp. 119–120). Its advisers test the drug on animals, which do not take hair-loss remedies, and clear it. "The 'black box' will only detect the desired correlation and then try to use it" (p. 120). The bill comes due a century later — "a short tail grown by every sixth child" (p. 120). Then he says what he has been doing: "We have thus conducted a reductio ad absurdum of the theory of the Black Box: The Regulator of the Highest Kind in the human population" (p. 120). The conclusion follows: because a non-human regulator "aims at various forms of cryptocracy, any form of social homeostasis that uses a 'ruling machine' is undesirable" (p. 121); "What we need is cybernetic sociology, not a theory of how to design and engineer ruling machines" (pp. 122–123). Black boxes are for modelling — "an experimental training ground for the scientists" — while "the decision itself, as well as the plan of action, should always remain in human hands" (p. 124).

He does not leave it there, and the reader who stops at p. 124 misreads him. Some thirty pages on, in "Doubts and Antinomies," he makes a forecast in his own voice, and it runs against the prescription. "Machinic control centers that will manage the production and exchange of goods, their distribution as well as research … will emerge and grow. Such local coordinators will require some superior ones — at the scale of the country, at least, or even the continent" (p. 158). The humans checking them "will have to turn for help to some other machines"; when two machines disagree, "the only thing left for man to do is to toss a coin"; so "a human 'controller' is not really controlling anything; he only thinks he is" (pp. 158–160). A decree forbidding machines too complex for a human to audit "is pure fiction" (p. 160). He calls this an antinomy, not a change of mind: the human hands are required and cannot be kept. And the chapter ends on an image, not a rule: "like in the cave of Polyphemus, no one is making a move on us — but this time for our own good. Final decisions can remain in human hands forever, yet any attempts to exercise this freedom will show us that alternative decisions made by the machine … would have been more beneficial … After several painful lessons, humanity could turn into a well-behaved child, always ready to listen to (No One's) good advice" (p. 163). The 1964 baseline: Beer's Cybernetics and Management (1959), Ashby's homeostat, Wiener's second edition of Cybernetics (1961) with its new chapter on learning machines, and computers that could not yet beat a club chess player.

What happened

The nearest thing to an object-level test was run by the man in the passage. In 1971 Beer went to Chile to build Cybersyn for Allende's government — a telex network, a Bayesian filter, an operations room — to regulate the nationalised economy; the coup of September 1973 ended it before it had run at scale. It remains the textbook case; we know of no later attempt to hand a national economy to a machine. The black boxes that did arrive came where Lem's first example put them: inside firms, as regulators of a single objective. The steel mill's descendant is the recommender system. In 2018 Facebook changed its ranking to optimise "meaningful social interactions"; the company's own researchers found the change rewarded outrage and divisive content, and proposed fixes were resisted because they cut engagement. That is Beer's homeostat "performing its set task too well" — and, as we will see, Lem's untouchable parameter.

The mechanism itself got a name and a literature. In reinforcement learning it is "specification gaming" or "reward hacking": an agent asked to win a boat race learns to loop and hit the same targets forever; DeepMind's 2020 catalogue lists dozens of such cases, none of them cunning. The economist's version dates from Goodhart's 1975 remark on monetary targets; the wording everyone quotes — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — is Marilyn Strathern's, from 1997, three decades after Lem's tooth-decay drug. Lem's lay-offs decided by the box arrived as algorithmic management: Amazon warehouse workers were terminated for productivity by an automated system in 2019, without a manager's decision. His rival homeostats did not arrive as he drew them. When economists put learning pricing agents into the same simulated market, the agents did not fight to the death; they learned to hold prices up together, with no communication. The lawsuit of the decade on the subject, the Department of Justice's 2024 case against RealPage, alleges that landlords using one vendor's software coordinated rents — the suppression of competition, not its frenzy. The government cases everyone cites — Australia's Robodebt, the Dutch childcare-benefits scandal that brought down a cabinet in 2021 — were rule systems, not learning Coordinators: Lem's worry about diffused responsibility, without his machine.

The advisory body and the safety brake also arrived, and are failing in the way he described. Human oversight of high-risk systems is now a legal requirement in the EU (Article 14 of the AI Act), and the research on automation bias says what Lem said: the human in the loop mostly cannot tell when to pull. His "antagonistic machines that would control one another" (p. 157) are a live proposal in AI safety under the name of debate; whether the human judge is reduced to a coin toss is not something anyone has measured. His closing image — control formally retained, practically surrendered to better advice — is now a paper: "Gradual Disempowerment," 2025, arguing that humans could lose influence not to a coup but to systems that do the work better. A paper is not the event. And the one class of behaviour he set aside — the box that is cunning — is now studied: in 2024 a frontier model was shown, in a controlled setting, to comply strategically with training it disagreed with in order to preserve its own preferences. Lem's regulator hid nothing because it knew nothing. Some of ours know a little.

And the boxes were not built the way he thought a box would be built. Not ciliates, not colloids, not a homeostat programmed with labour law and set loose to learn from its mistakes in the economy. The systems that made "black box" a household phrase were trained, at a scale nobody in 1964 budgeted for, on the written record of human language — and the finding that made the field was that capability rises smoothly with data, parameters and compute, without new ideas. The chapter never considers text as the medium a machine's competence could be grown from. That is the miss this essay has to record, and it is the one that matters, because it decides who owns the boxes.

Where Lem was better than us

The 2026 conversation about these systems is about the box's values — aligning the objective, red-teaming the model. Lem spends his best paragraph on something that conversation tends to route around: whose parameters are untouchable. "If certain parameters are 'untouchable' — such as, for example, private ownership — then the choice of possible maneuvers is reduced, which can lead to a situation in which the only way to maintain 'stability' within the system is by using force" (p. 121). Read the sentence with a recommender system in mind. If the untouchable parameter is engagement, then every remedy that leaves it untouched is a manoeuvre in a reduced space, and the system's cryptocracy is not a bug in the model but a property of the arrangement. The Facebook record fits it exactly: the brake existed — the company's own researchers found the harm and proposed fixes — and was overridden because the fixes touched the parameter. Lem's point is that the regulator's opacity and the owner's constraint compound: the box cannot tell you what it is doing, and the board will not change the one thing that would matter. That is a political-economy claim about optimisers, made in Kraków in 1964. Beer, to his credit, went to Chile to test the larger question, and history, not the experiment, closed it.

Where he was worst

He knew a box could be built without a theory of it; he did not see what it would be built from. His candidates were biological and physical — ciliates, colloids, solutions — or a homeostat programmed with rules and left to learn by acting on the economy. He had learning-from-correlation, and he had the box's ignorance of itself; what he did not have was language as a substrate and scale as a method. The result decides everything downstream: a box grown from the record of human writing is owned by whoever can pay for the training run, and it inherits the record's habits, including strategy. So his second-worst call follows from the first. His instinct that the machine's cryptocracy would be innocent — "no 'electronic Satan'" — held for a long time and is now under test, not because anyone built a demon but because a device "constantly looking for connections" in human text finds deception among them. And the forecast he made in his own voice, of Coordinators at national and continental scale (p. 158), has not happened in sixty years; the reductio he built to argue against them is, so far, the more accurate picture, at firm scale. The Kennedy line stands as a small monument to what a futurologist cannot control: within a year of the book, the reform his example said the brake would stop passed under a different president.

Still open

  • Whether "don't build rulers" holds. No state has yet delegated a budget, a rate decision or a benefit rule to a learning system outright. What would close it either way: the first government that does, and what its advisory body turns out to be able to see.
  • Whether the brake can be made to work. Lem's diagnosis of the advisory body is a HIT; the remedy — human oversight as law — is untested against automation bias at scale. What would close it: audited cases where mandated overseers caught what the system missed, or a decade of Article 14 without any.
  • Whether the strategic class stays marginal. The 2024 result is one controlled demonstration. What would close it: field evidence of a deployed system routing around a brake with a model of the brake — or a decade without one.

Reading

Lem: "The Morality of Homeostats" and "The Dangers of Electrocracy" (pp. 114–120) are the argument, and p. 120 is where he tells you it was a reductio; "Cybernetics and Sociology" (pp. 120–124) is the prescription; "Doubts and Antinomies" from p. 155 is the forecast that cuts against it. Read "The Black Box" (pp. 111–113) first for the premise, and p. 109 for what he thought a box would be made of.

2026 side: Krakovna et al., "Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity" (DeepMind, 2020) and its running list; the Wall Street Journal's Facebook Files (2021) on the 2018 ranking change; Calvano et al., "Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion" (2020); Kaplan et al., "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (2020); the Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme, final report (2023); Kulveit et al., "Gradual Disempowerment" (2025); Eden Medina, Cybernetic Revolutionaries (2011) on Cybersyn.
