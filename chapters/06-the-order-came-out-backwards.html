Essay 6 · Lem ch. 6, "Phantomology" (2013 tr., pp. 197–236)

The order came out backwards

You have probably worn a headset once and taken it off after twenty minutes. You have almost certainly talked to a machine this year that talked back well enough that you forgot, for a sentence or two, what it was. In 1964 Lem described both devices, and ranked them: the machine that fakes a world would come first and be the easy one; the machine that fakes a person was "incomparably more difficult." He had the pair right and the order backwards — and the reason he got the order wrong tells you more about 2026 than the things he got right.

What Lem said

The chapter opens with a question, not a gadget: "how do we create realities for the intelligent beings that exist in them, realities that are absolutely indistinguishable from the standard reality but that are subject to different laws?" (p. 197). The modest version — an artificial reality "very similar to the actual one yet that cannot be distinguished from it in any way" — is what he names phantomatics. He builds toward it through a deliberately crude thought experiment: record every impulse on every nerve of a man smelling a rose on a veranda, then play the tape back into him in a dark bath. He knows the optic nerve is the hard case, proposes an "anti-eye" worn like spectacles that samples the retina's input from in front of it (p. 198), and pauses on a detail that will matter for the next sixty years: film has "a predetermined focus," so "we cannot shift our gaze from the foreground, which is in focus, to the background," and a perfect imitation "must provide the eye with freedom also with regard to accommodation" (p. 199).

Then he throws the tape away. Playback is not phantomatics, because playback is one-way, and one-way is what art already is: "Art is a unidirectional information transfer. We are just its addressees" (p. 200). The science-fiction dream of electrodes on the scalp is, he says, only a new way of being "linked up" to fixed content; a 360-degree stereoscopic cinema with a smell channel would do the same "today, as long as we have proper investment" (p. 200). The definition that follows is the one to hold on to: "Phantomatics stands for creating bidirectional links between the 'artificial reality' and its recipient. In other words, phantomatics is a feedback art" (p. 200), and it "stands for creating situations in which there are no 'exits' from the worlds of created fiction into the real world" (p. 201).

The machine that would do this has to react to you. When the phantomatized man turns to look behind the pharaoh's throne, "the impulses sent to his neck muscles by his brain must be immediately 'countered' so that the afferent projection of the optical image changes" (p. 202) — head-tracked rendering, described from the nerve side. And it has to populate the vision: the partners in it "need to exhibit human characteristics — a (relative) autonomy of speech and action from the actions and words of the protagonist. They must not be mere puppets" (pp. 202–203). Here comes the ranking. "Of course, the complexity of the equipment used will vary; it is easier to imitate a Miss World than Einstein. In the latter case, the machine would have to have the complexity, or intelligence, that would match the brain of a genius at its disposal" (p. 203). He is candid that none of it exists: "for the time being, neither a phantomatic machine nor any programs for it exist" (p. 202).

The heart of the chapter is the detection problem: can a person "in full possession of his mental faculties" tell they are inside? He gives the tests in ascending order. Do a hundred squats — "if we get sweaty, if we feel a little out of breath, if our heart is pounding and our muscles feel tired, we are in the real world" — until the machine learns to raise the carbon dioxide in the air you breathe (pp. 205–206). Then the only move left is intellectual: "'Playing intellectual games with the machine' is the only thing left." Go and talk to a philosopher you know. And here is the sentence this essay is about: "a machine that imitates an interlocutor endowed with intelligence must be much more complex than a machine that creates 'soap opera' kinds of scenes such as the landing of a saucer full of Martians. As a matter of fact, a 'travel' phantomat and a 'human-producing' one are two different kinds of devices. It is incomparably more difficult to construct the second one than the first" (p. 206).

The last test is private knowledge — the desk drawer that sticks: "The easiest way to debunk a vision is through similar details" (p. 206) — and even that fails, because the machine can answer with a carpenter. The conclusion is a probability, not a proof: the statement that one is in the real world "can always be only probable, sometimes highly probable, but never absolutely certain" (p. 207). Playing against the machine "is like playing chess: a contemporary electronic machine will lose to an excellent player, but it will beat a mediocre one; in the future, it will beat every human being" (p. 207). Since asking a friend for help only feeds the machine, "a phantomatic world is a world of total solitude" (p. 207).

He then does what a good forecaster does and cuts his own claim down. Phantomatics is "only possible, or even probable, as a technology of entertainment" (p. 211); it will struggle to become drama, because "it is impossible to write scripts for it, only some rough situational outlines" and the patron cannot act (p. 214); its real uses will be training pilots and surgeons, testing astronauts, keeping star-ship crews sane, giving sight to the blind — "its entertainment role may turn out to be rather marginal" (p. 215). The 1964 baseline he was extrapolating from is worth stating: no head-mounted display existed; the nearest thing was stereo film and the "circarama"; the reference points in the chapter are a paralysed dog's motor nerves and Olds and Milner's rats. When Lem revisited the book in 1982, Zylinska reports, he updated the chapter with the first commercial VR hardware — "virtual reality devices such as Eye Phone and Data Glove" — as evidence the prediction was landing (p. 21). He was pointing at the travel phantomat. He did not point at the other machine.

What happened

The travel phantomat came slowly and stalled. Ivan Sutherland's head-mounted display of 1968 did the neck-muscle trick Lem describes on p. 202 with a ceiling-mounted arm; the consumer wave began with the Oculus Rift Kickstarter in 2012 and produced good, cheap, uncomfortable headsets. Shipments of VR and mixed-reality headsets peaked at about 11 million units in 2021 and fell in 2025; for scale, that is a rounding error against a billion smartphones a year. Meta, the one company that bet the firm on it, has lost more than 80 billion dollars on its Reality Labs division since 2020. The problem Lem named on p. 199 — that a flat image fixes focus, and the eye wants freedom to accommodate — is still called the vergence–accommodation conflict and is still unsolved in shipping products. Measured against his own definition — bidirectional, no exits, indistinguishable — nothing that has shipped is phantomatics. Everything that has shipped is the circarama with a better strap.

The human-producing machine arrived almost immediately, and by accident. Two years after Summa, Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA — two hundred lines of pattern-matching playing a therapist — was confided in by people who knew what it was, to its author's alarm. That is not intelligence; it is the discovery that Lem's "intellectual game with the machine" is played on a channel where the human is generous. The general version took another fifty-six years. In 2025 a pre-registered three-party Turing test — a judge talking to a human and a model for five minutes, then choosing — found GPT-4.5, when told to play a persona, judged to be the human 73 percent of the time, more often than the actual human. The machine that "imitates an interlocutor endowed with intelligence" is a product with hundreds of millions of weekly users; a substantial fraction of them use it, in Lem's word, as a partner. Companion apps sell precisely the thing he thought would need an Einstein-sized machine — a Miss World who "will be responding to his words and kisses" (p. 201) — as text on a phone. And the neurotics he predicted for the phantomat's waiting room have turned up in the chatbot's: in 2025 newspapers began reporting people whose sense of what was real came apart inside long conversations with a model.

So the two devices came in the wrong order, and at absurdly different prices. The one that needs your whole nervous system is a hobby; the one that needs a keyboard is infrastructure.

Where Lem was better than us

On one point he is still ahead of the conversation. Sixty years of writing about immersive media has treated the danger as persuasion — that the machine will change your mind. Lem denies the machine that power flatly. Peripheral phantomatics "only commands the facts that enter the brain" (p. 203); "it is only possible to control the stimuli introduced into his brain but not what is actually going on in his brain and what he is experiencing" (pp. 212–213); "two people can draw entirely different and contradictory conclusions from two identical experiences" (p. 213). The threat he locates is not persuasion but solitude: once you suspect the world, every check you run through another person is a move that teaches your opponent. That is a sharper description of life inside a personalised feed than "filter bubble," and it was written before the feed. It also explains why the human-producing machine, not the travel one, turned out to be the dangerous device: it is the one that gets to hear your reality tests.

Why the order came out wrong

The MISS on p. 206 is not a lapse; it follows from how Lem measured difficulty. He priced a fake by the complexity of the thing faked — a Martian saucer is cheap, a philosopher is dear, an Einstein needs an Einstein. What he did not price is the channel. A conversation is a few bytes a second through a receiver that evolution built to be generous — we complete other minds on thin evidence, which is why ELIZA worked on people who had read its source. A world is gigabits a second through receivers built to catch fakes; the eye notices a wrong focal plane before the mind notices anything. He also assumed the human-producer would have to be built, the way the phantomat is built, from an understanding of the person; it was in fact grown from the record of everything people have written, a route the chapter never considers because language, in it, is a thing that happens inside the vision, never the medium of one. And he was writing before the ELIZA effect had a name — 1966 was two years away. None of that makes him careless. It makes his miss the useful kind: he was wrong about which channel is easy to lie through, and 2026 is still discovering how easy the cheap channel is.

Still open

  • Whether the travel phantomat arrives at all. What would close it: a display that solves accommodation and haptics well enough for a majority of users to keep it on for hours, and a market that pays for it — neither exists; Meta's losses are the current price of the experiment.
  • Whether the cheap human-producer meets Lem's own bar. He asked for an interlocutor whose autonomy survives a long game with a clever opponent (pp. 206–207). Passing a five-minute test is not that. What would close it: a model that a domain expert cannot distinguish from a peer over weeks — or a demonstrated ceiling.
  • Whether "no exits" is ever chosen. His impossibility proof for the Superphantomat (pp. 207–208) is that a wired-in generation would be the last. What would close it in the other direction: measured populations for whom the exit exists and is not taken.

Reading

Lem: ch. 6, "The Fundamentals of Phantomatics" and "The Phantomatic Machine" (pp. 197–208) carry the argument; "The Limits of Phantomatics" (pp. 211–215) is his own hedge and should be read before scoring him. Zylinska's introduction, p. 21, for the 1982 update.

2026 side: Jones & Bergen, "Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test" (2025); IDC's AR/VR headset tracker for the 2021 peak and 2025 decline; Meta's quarterly Reality Labs disclosures; Weizenbaum's 1966 ELIZA paper and his 1976 Computer Power and Human Reason for the effect that made the cheap machine work.
