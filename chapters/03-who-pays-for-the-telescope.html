Essay 3 · Lem ch. 3, "Civilizations in the Universe" (2013 tr., pp. 59–92)

Who pays for the telescope

Thirty pages of this chapter ask why nobody answers, and the answer Lem settles on is not in the chapter at all — it is in note 3, where he says which hypothesis he has "selected as the dominant" one. The last two pages then ask a different kind of question: what a search would cost, who would pay for it, who would be willing to transmit, and whether we could recognise a message if it arrived. Those two pages have aged better than the thirty, and they explain something the thirty cannot: after sixty years of listening, we have searched about a drinking glass of the ocean.

What Lem said

The problem is stated with numbers. Drake's instruments at Green Bank had listened since 1960, and "Day after day, week after week, their instruments registered nothing but the monotonous cosmic noise generated by dead matter" (p. 64). Bracewell and von Hoerner had computed civilisation density "from the premise that only 1 out of 150 stars in our Galaxy has planets" (p. 63) — Lem calls it modest — which still gives a billion planetary systems, and if civilisations last as long as their suns then "the average distance between two civilizations would be less than ten light-years" (pp. 63–64). Since nothing is heard, the estimates for civilisation lifetime were ratcheted down until they fit the silence: to a hundred million years, then to "less than twenty thousand years" (p. 65), a thousand light-years apart. Lem's question, put twice: "Why, in other words, are we seeing no 'miracles'?" (p. 67).

He refuses the fashionable answer. Von Hoerner assigned self-destruction a 65 percent chance, and Lem will not have it: "I cannot accept such a statistical hell—not because it is too terrifying but because it is too naïve" (p. 67). His objection is statistical rather than moral. Von Hoerner's own figures are averages, and averages admit outliers; "the presence of just a few civilizations with an 'abnormal' life span in a galactic set would influence the whole Galaxy" (p. 83), producing expansions visible across star fields. Turning a statistical rule into universal extinction is "an additional premise, plucked out of thin air" (p. 83).

His own proposal, hypothesis III, keeps civilisations frequent and long-lived and shortens only one phase: "It is not their existence that is short-lived but only a certain phase within it, which is characterized by exponential growth", lasting "less than twenty thousand years" (p. 82), after which the dynamics change without suicide or decay. In "Votum Separatum" he goes further and doubts the category itself — "we are not looking for 'any civilizations' but predominantly for anthropomorphic ones" (p. 73) — and sketches an alternative: a world whose disturbances exceed its regulatory capacity might develop "biological technology prior to physical technology", its inhabitants transforming themselves rather than their surroundings, in "a planned and directed autoevolution, a kind of 'adaptation retreat'" (p. 86). Such a civilisation would be invisible for the same reason it was successful.

Which of these he actually believes is stated only in note 3 (pp. 358–359). Having rejected prompt extinction as "an absurd kind of determinism" and rarity as a contradiction of cosmic homogeneity, he concludes: "the hypothesis about the 'isolation' of civilizations from the Universe so that their activity cannot be easily observed on an astronomical scale seems to be the most probable one. It has therefore been selected as the dominant hypothesis in the course of writing this book."

Then the last two pages change register from cosmology to accounting. Star flight first, on von Hoerner's arithmetic rather than his own: even annihilation propulsion fails, because "The amount of matter that would have to be annihilated to fly from one galaxy to another in a 'reasonable' time span… almost equals the mass of our Moon" (p. 89), so such flights "are thus considered impossible for the time being and are likely to remain impossible over the next several hundred years" (p. 89) — an engineering wall, since astronautics "is not prohibited by the laws of nature" (p. 89).

Then the search, and the sentence this essay is named for. Radio contact "requires some serious material investment, as it turns out, if it is to be actualized" — "a great amount of equipment that would enable a 'search for extraterrestrials'—and possibly also transmitting stations" — and "This kind of investment would have to be even more substantial than the current investment in research on nuclear energy" (p. 90). He knows where the obstacle sits: "No doubt scientists still need to 'cultivate' a generation of rulers who will be inclined to dip deeply enough into the public coffers to finance issues that, worrying as it may seem, traditionally belong to science fiction territory" (p. 90). Inside the same paragraph, the asymmetry, which he credits to others: "If, for economic reasons, all the civilizations were only working on reception, then no one would ever hear anyone else, as many have already pointed out" (p. 90).

And the information argument. "the more precisely the transmitted message uses the capacity of the information channel… the more it starts to resemble noise" (p. 90), so a receiver ignorant of the code has "tremendous difficulties not just with deciphering the information he receives but also with recognizing it precisely as information" (p. 90) — hence "We cannot thus exclude the possibility that already today, we are receiving, as noise… fragments of 'interstellar conversations'" (p. 90), and hence anyone wishing to be found must waste bandwidth on "special 'call signs' with a relatively simple, clearly ordered, and repeatable structure" (p. 90). Baseline: Shklovsky's monograph of 1962; Cocconi and Morrison's proposal and Drake's Ozma, already searching for artificial regularity against galactic noise (p. 64); Kardashev's three types from Byurakan in 1964; and no exoplanet detected by anyone. This translation follows the altered fourth edition of 1974 — note 5 records that Lem extended his statement that nothing specific was yet known to cover 1974 as well.

What happened

Start with how much listening has been done, because every other verdict depends on it. In 2018 Wright, Kanodia and Lubar built an eight-dimensional model of the radio search space — sky position, frequency, sensitivity, polarisation, drift, duration and the rest — and computed the fraction that decades of programmes had collectively covered. It is minute: comparable to examining a drinking glass of seawater for fish in all of Earth's oceans, and a later survey using the same framework reported a search fraction around two parts in ten thousand trillion. Sixty years of silence is a real observation about a very small volume.

The money went as he feared, though not in the shape he described. NASA's High Resolution Microwave Survey — the last major federally funded American search — began observing in October 1992 at about $10 million a year, under 0.1 percent of NASA's budget, and was killed about a year later by a last-minute Senate amendment. After that, dedicated American radio SETI leaned on philanthropy: roughly $30 million from Paul Allen for the array that carries his name, which went into hibernation in April 2011 when federal and state funding collapsed and returned that December partly by renting itself to the Air Force to track orbital debris; then $100 million from a technology billionaire in 2015; then, in 2023, a $200 million bequest from the estate of a Qualcomm co-founder, against an operating budget of $25–30 million a year. But the instruments themselves stayed public. Breakthrough Listen does its observing on the NSF-funded Green Bank Telescope and CSIRO's Parkes dish, not on anything it built. The rulers did not fund the search; they had already funded the telescopes.

That also complicates his price. He asked for "a great amount of equipment" and possibly transmitters, and compared the bill to nuclear-energy research; what happened was a programme commitment of about $10 million a year riding on public infrastructure, against $790 million for US fusion in a single year and more than $20 billion sunk into ITER. Those are not like-for-like quantities, and nobody has assembled the all-in figure his sentence actually names. What can be said is that no state ever treated the search as a project of that size, and that the searching which did happen was cheap because it borrowed dishes built for something else.

The transmission asymmetry is real and remains the field's live dispute, though the history is less tidy than "nobody transmits". Frank Drake broadcast the Arecibo message toward M13 in 1974, with considerable fanfare; a Russian astronomer sent several messages from Evpatoria; and METI International was founded in 2015 to send more. What is true is that sustained deliberate transmission never became normal, and that in the same year a statement issued around the AAAS meeting held that any decision to transmit "must be based upon a worldwide consensus, and not a decision based upon the wishes of a few individuals with access to powerful communications equipment." Everyone listens and almost nobody calls, which is Lem's economic trap with a moral argument now bolted onto it.

His information argument is sound and its novelty is smaller than it looks: Ozma was already hunting artificial regularity against galactic noise when he wrote, as he says himself at p. 64. What the argument explains is why the method stayed that way. Radio SETI still concentrates on narrowband, repetitive signals, because natural astrophysical emission is broadband while an efficiently encoded transmission is statistically hard to distinguish from noise — so the detectable beacon is by construction the one somebody wasted bandwidth to make detectable. His stronger claim, that the conversations may already be in the archives as background, remains untestable and is a live reason the null result is weaker than it sounds.

Star flight has not moved, but sixty years is not the several hundred he allowed. No propulsion has appeared that changes von Hoerner's arithmetic. The only funded interstellar programme abandoned the crewed ship entirely: Breakthrough Starshot, announced in 2016 with $100 million, proposes gram-scale laser-driven sails to a neighbouring star — a different journey from the intergalactic crossing von Hoerner costed, and a proof-of-concept programme rather than a mission. It is privately funded too.

Where Lem was better than us

He noticed that the question has a budget, and that the budget has a politics. Most writing about contact — most of it far more recent than this — treats the search as something that simply happens, as though telescopes pointed themselves, and treats the silence as data arriving free of charge. Lem asked who would pay, and then asked the harder version: if listening is cheaper than shouting, everyone will listen, and a universe of listeners is silent for reasons that have nothing to do with whether anyone is there. He also had the discipline to state the information problem in its strong form, that the better the message the more it looks like nothing, so the only thing findable is what somebody deliberately wasted effort to make findable. And in note 3 he did the thing this book most often finds him failing to do: he said which hypothesis he had chosen and why, in writing, where it could be held against him.

Where he was worst

He costed the wrong object and framed the problem as scale. His estimate — more than nuclear-energy research — set contact up as something only a state could attempt, which made political will the binding constraint. What actually bound was neither money nor will but attention. The sums were small enough that a single donor could cover them and small enough that no government felt obliged to; a programme costing 0.1 percent of NASA's budget is not killed because it is expensive but because it is cheap enough to cut without argument and strange enough to make an easy vote. And the equipment problem dissolved in a way he did not foresee: you do not need to build the great amount of equipment if you can rent time on dishes built for radio astronomy.

The second cost lands on the chapter's main argument, and by his own standard. Thirty pages treat the silence as something the theory of biogenesis must answer to, and he does say, carefully, "Today it is still too early for this" (p. 80). Sixty more years of listening have not changed that, because the listening covered a fraction of the search space best expressed in scientific notation. The silence may yet be the universe's answer. It is not yet evidence of anything, and the chapter's confidence that a sufficiently long null result would force a revision of biogenesis is a promissory note that has not begun to come due.

Still open

  • What the null result is worth. The completeness accounting exists now and gives a number close to zero. What would close it: a search that covers enough of the space for the silence to constrain anything — or an argument that the space is too large in principle for any null result to constrain.
  • Whether anyone will transmit. His asymmetry predicts permanent mutual listening, and the record since Arecibo is consistent with it. What would close it: a sustained transmission programme with international sanction — or a formal prohibition, which would confirm the trap rather than escape it.
  • Whether isolation is why. Note 3 selects it as his dominant hypothesis and nothing since bears on it. What would close it: any technosignature search sensitive to a civilisation that is not expanding — or a demonstration that a civilisation which stops growing necessarily stops being detectable, which would make the hypothesis unfalsifiable and settle it the other way.

Reading

Lem: "Future Prospects" (pp. 87–92) carries this essay, and within it the two pages from p. 89. But read note 3 (pp. 358–359) first: it is where he says which hypothesis he actually chose, and the chapter never does. "Votum Separatum" (pp. 83–86) is the alternative he sketches to anthropomorphic search.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. Wright, Kanodia and Lubar on how much of the haystack has been searched (AJ, 2018), which reframes everything; the record of NASA's High Resolution Microwave Survey and its cancellation, for what happened to public funding; and the 2015 statement on messaging extraterrestrial intelligence, for why almost nobody transmits.
