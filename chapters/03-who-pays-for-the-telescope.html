Essay 3 · Lem ch. 3, "Civilizations in the Universe" (2013 tr., pp. 59–92)

Who pays for the telescope

The famous part of this chapter is the silence — thirty pages on why nobody answers. The part nobody quotes is two pages near the end where Lem stops asking whether they are out there and starts asking what the search would cost, who would pay for it, who would be willing to transmit, and whether we would recognise a message if it arrived. Those two pages have aged better than the thirty. He was wrong about the price and right about almost everything else, including the thing that has actually shaped the field: nobody wants to pay, and nobody wants to answer.

What Lem said

The chapter's problem is stated early and never softens. Drake's instruments at Green Bank had been listening since 1960, and "Day after day, week after week, their instruments registered nothing but the monotonous cosmic noise generated by dead matter" (p. 64). The statistics of the day made that hard to explain: Bracewell and von Hoerner worked "from the premise that only 1 out of 150 stars in our Galaxy has planets" (p. 63), which still gives a billion planetary systems, and if civilisations last as long as their suns then "the average distance between two civilizations would be less than ten light-years" (pp. 63–64). So either they are rare, or they are brief. Lem's question, repeated twice: "Why, in other words, are we seeing no 'miracles'?" (p. 67).

He rejects the fashionable answer without ceremony. Von Hoerner put the odds of civilisations destroying themselves at 65 percent, and Lem will not have it: "I cannot accept such a statistical hell—not because it is too terrifying but because it is too naïve" (p. 67). Nor will he accept the opposite comfort. His own proposal, hypothesis III, is that civilisations are frequent and long-lived but not orthoevolutionary — that only the exponential phase is brief, "less than twenty thousand years" (p. 82), after which the dynamics change without either suicide or decay.

Then, in "Future Prospects", the register changes from cosmology to accounting, and this is the part of the chapter this essay grades. Interstellar travel first: von Hoerner's energy balance shows that even annihilation propulsion fails, because "The amount of matter that would have to be annihilated to fly from one galaxy to another in a 'reasonable' time span… almost equals the mass of our Moon" (p. 89). Such flights "are thus considered impossible for the time being and are likely to remain impossible over the next several hundred years" (p. 89) — though he is careful that this is an engineering wall, not a physical prohibition: astronautics "is not prohibited by the laws of nature" (p. 89).

Then the search itself, and the sentence that gives this essay its title. Radio contact "requires some serious material investment, as it turns out, if it is to be actualized… This kind of investment would have to be even more substantial than the current investment in research on nuclear energy" (p. 90). And he knows where the obstacle sits, which is not in the sky: "No doubt scientists still need to 'cultivate' a generation of rulers who will be inclined to dip deeply enough into the public coffers to finance issues that, worrying as it may seem, traditionally belong to science fiction territory" (p. 90).

Inside the same paragraph he identifies the asymmetry that has since become the field's central political problem. Listening is cheap relative to shouting, so everyone will listen: "If, for economic reasons, all the civilizations were only working on reception, then no one would ever hear anyone else, as many have already pointed out" (p. 90).

And then the information argument, which is the best thing in the chapter. The better a message is encoded, the less it looks like a message: "the more precisely the transmitted message uses the capacity of the information channel (i.e., the higher the reduction of the excess of this transmission), the more it starts to resemble noise" (p. 90). The consequence is that a receiver who does not know the code has "tremendous difficulties not just with deciphering the information he receives but also with recognizing it precisely as information, as opposed to cosmic background noise" (p. 90). So — "We cannot thus exclude the possibility that already today, we are receiving, as noise, via our radio telescopes, fragments of 'interstellar conversations'" (p. 90). It follows that anyone who wants to be found must deliberately waste bandwidth, sending "special 'call signs' with a relatively simple, clearly ordered, and repeatable structure" (p. 90), which are a fraction of their traffic — which makes the receiving equipment, once again, the expensive part.

The mid-1960s baseline: Shklovsky's monograph of 1962; Cocconi and Morrison's proposal and Drake's Ozma; the Byurakan conference of 1964 where Kardashev proposed his three energy types; and no exoplanet detected anywhere, by anyone.

What happened

The price was wrong, and wrong in the generous direction. A serious search never cost anything like nuclear-energy research and never needed to. NASA's High Resolution Microwave Survey — the last major federally funded American search — ran at about $10 million a year, under 0.1 percent of NASA's budget. The largest programme ever mounted, Breakthrough Listen, committed $100 million across ten years: the same $10 million a year. Against that, the US fusion programme alone was $790 million in a single year, and ITER's construction has passed $20 billion. Listening to the whole sky turns out to be two orders of magnitude cheaper than not-quite-building a reactor.

His political forecast, though, was exact, and it failed in the way he feared rather than the way he hoped. The generation of rulers was never cultivated. HRMS began observing in October 1992 and was killed about a year later by a last-minute Senate amendment, and no comparable public programme has replaced it. What kept the search alive instead was private money, repeatedly and precariously. Paul Allen paid roughly $30 million for the array that carries his name; in April 2011 it went into hibernation when federal and state funding collapsed, and came back that December partly by renting itself to the US Air Force to track orbital debris, topped up by about $200,000 from a public appeal. The telescope stayed alive by taking day-labour. Then a technology billionaire funded the largest search in history in 2015, and in 2023 the field was finally endowed by a dead man: $200 million from the estate of a Qualcomm co-founder, against an annual operating budget of $25–30 million. Lem asked which rulers would pay. The answer was none of them.

The transmission asymmetry became the field's live controversy, exactly as stated. Almost nobody transmits. The deliberate messages that exist are a handful sent by one Russian astronomer from a dish in Crimea, and a small organisation founded in 2015 to send more; the same year, a statement circulated around the AAAS meeting held that any decision to transmit "must be based upon a worldwide consensus, and not a decision based upon the wishes of a few individuals with access to powerful communications equipment." Sixty years on, the reason no one hears anyone is at least partly that everyone has decided listening is the responsible posture — which is Lem's sentence with a moral argument bolted onto the economic one he named.

The information argument is now simply how the searches are built. Radio SETI hunts narrowband signals, because natural astrophysical sources are broadband and an efficiently encoded transmission is statistically indistinguishable from noise; a beacon is therefore expected to be simple and repetitive rather than information-dense, which is precisely the "call sign" he described. His stronger claim — that the conversations may already be in the data, filed as background — remains unfalsifiable and is the reason the field's null result is weaker than it sounds.

The travel budget held. No propulsion has appeared that changes von Hoerner's arithmetic, and the only funded interstellar programme is the small version: Breakthrough Starshot, announced in 2016 with $100 million, proposing gram-scale laser-driven sails instead of a crewed ship. It is privately funded too. Every serious attempt to leave or to listen has been paid for by individuals, which is the one thing about the whole enterprise Lem did not predict — he assumed that something this expensive would have to be a state's decision, and it turned out to be cheap enough to be a hobby of the rich.

Where Lem was better than us

He noticed that the question has a budget. Sixty years of writing about contact — most of it far more recent than this — treats the problem as physics, biology and philosophy, and treats the search as something that simply happens, as though telescopes pointed themselves. Lem asked who would pay, and then asked the harder version: if listening is cheaper than transmitting, everybody will listen, and a universe of listeners is silent for reasons that have nothing to do with whether anyone is there. That argument is now the axis of the METI dispute, and it was three sentences in a 1966 book. He also had the discipline to state the information problem in its strong form — that the better the message, the more it looks like nothing, so the thing you can detect is by construction the thing somebody wasted effort to make detectable. Both points are engineering, not prophecy, and both survived because they were about constraints rather than about aliens.

Where he was worst

He assumed expense, and expense was the one thing that did not bind. His estimate — more than nuclear-energy research — was out by roughly two orders of magnitude, and it mattered, because it framed the search as something only a state could attempt, which framed the problem as political will. The actual constraint was neither money nor will but attention: the sums involved were small enough that a single donor could cover them, and small enough that no government felt obliged to. A programme costing 0.1 percent of NASA's budget is not killed because it is expensive. It is killed because it is cheap enough to be an easy line to cut and strange enough to be an easy vote.

There is a second cost to his error, and it lands on the chapter's main argument. Thirty pages treat the silence as data — as something the theory of biogenesis must answer to. But a null result is only as strong as the search that produced it, and this search has been funded at hobby scale for sixty years, with one array going dark for eight months because a state budget moved. Lem himself supplies the standard, at p. 80: the absence of data grows in significance "the longer such attempts take and the more sensitive the instruments used in them are". By his own condition, we are not yet entitled to the conclusion the chapter reaches for. The silence may be the universe's answer. It may also be what $10 million a year sounds like.

Still open

  • What the null result is worth. Nobody has established the search's coverage well enough to say what fraction of the plausible signal space has been excluded. What would close it: a stated, quantified survey completeness — this fraction of stars, bands, sensitivities and durations searched — against which the silence could actually be scored.
  • Whether anyone will ever transmit. His asymmetry predicts permanent mutual listening. What would close it: a sustained transmission programme with international sanction — or a formal international prohibition, which would settle it the other way and confirm the trap.
  • Whether the conversations are already in the archives. His strongest claim is that efficient signals are indistinguishable from noise. What would close it: a method that discriminates structured high-entropy transmission from natural broadband emission — or a proof that no such method can exist, which would make the search permanently blind to any civilisation not deliberately shouting.

Reading

Lem: "Future Prospects" (pp. 87–92), and within it the two pages from p. 89 that carry this essay. "A Metatheory of Miracles" (pp. 70–73) is the chapter's best-known argument and belongs to a different essay; "Votum Separatum" (pp. 83–86) is where he gives his own answer to the silence.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. The record of NASA's High Resolution Microwave Survey and its cancellation, for what happened to public funding; the Breakthrough Listen announcement of 2015 and the SETI Institute's 2023 bequest, for who paid instead; and the 2015 statement on messaging extraterrestrial intelligence, for why nobody transmits. The rest is in the claims register.
