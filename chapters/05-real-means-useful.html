Essay 5 · Lem ch. 5, "Prolegomena to Omnipotence" (2013 tr., pp. 164–196), with ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–141)

Real means useful

There is a figure in the middle of this chapter that anyone who has worked near machine learning half recognises. Lem calls him the Designer — Zylinska's rendering of Konstruktor, a word she notes carries engineering in it (p. 21). He declines every question no experiment could settle, and for him "'real' means the same as 'useful'." Half of that describes 2026 exactly. The other half does not, and Lem wrote the difference himself: "The designer is not a narrow pragmatist, like a builder who is constructing his house from bricks, uninterested in where these bricks came from and what they are, as long as the house gets built" (p. 176). His Designer drops the metaphysics and keeps the materials science; the builders who arrived dropped both. He was describing a working style, not forecasting one, so most of this chapter cannot be scored the way this book scores a prediction — which is itself the finding, and the ledger below says so.

What Lem said

The chapter asks how one would go about building anything at all, and starts by widening the word: "A machine is thus a system that manifests some kind of regularity of behavior: statistical, probabilistic, or deterministic" (p. 165) — an atom is one, an apple tree is one. Lem then warns himself about the two ways this talk fails. On one side, Scylla: "there are also periods like the current one, when practically no inviolable scientific propositions exist" (p. 175). On the other, Charybdis, "a whirlwind of cosmic garrulousness straight from science fiction" (p. 175) full of "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175). His compass is deliberately narrow: "we need some restraint—but on what level? The level of design. It is because we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176).

Then comes the Designer. A working engineer with a theory of knowledge he refuses to defend — and the refusal is narrower than it looks. "The designer is not a narrow pragmatist, like a builder who is constructing his house from bricks, uninterested in where these bricks came from and what they are, as long as the house gets built. The designer knows everything about his bricks—except for what they look like when no one is looking at them. He knows that properties belong to situations, not things" (p. 176). What he gives up is the observer-independent residue, not the engineering. Asked whether a substance is "really" bitter when only some taste it so, he says the question makes no sense; asked what the world looks like with nobody in it, he asks which physics you mean, this century's or the next. He is not a sceptic: "He believes in the existence of one reality—which can be interpreted in an infinite number of ways… He is thus indeed a pragmatist, and 'real' means the same as 'useful' to him" (p. 177). His rule for closing arguments: "The Designer suggests that everything should be resolved by means of an experiment, and if an experiment is not possible, and never will be, the issue disappears for him" (p. 179). He leaves "How does mathematics exist?" and "Why does the world exist?" unanswered "not because of his fondness for ignorance but because he knows the consequences of considering such questions" (p. 179).

The next section is the chapter's best-known. A mad tailor sews clothes for nobody: spherical garments with no openings, sleeves in arbitrary numbers, all consistent, all stacked in a warehouse. "Mathematics works in the same way. It builds structures but it is not clear of what" (p. 180) — Russell is quoted, "the subject in which we never know what we are talking about, nor whether what we are saying is true" (p. 180) — and then the point that matters: "Since the sixteenth century, physicists have been searching through the warehouses of 'empty clothes' created by mathematics. Matrix calculus was an 'empty structure' until Heisenberg found a 'piece of the world' that matched" (p. 181). Mathematics is therefore not a picture of the world but "more like a ladder that can be used to climb a mountain, even though it does not itself resemble a mountain in any way" (p. 182). And then: "Yet the question as to which parts of this mountain correspond to the ranks of the ladder makes no sense… we must not ask whether the ladder is 'true.' It can only be better or worse—as a tool for achieving a goal" (p. 183). He allows the traffic to reverse one day — "Mathematics will be a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184) — but not yet.

"Models and Reality" turns the same knife on modelling. Simplification is the point — "we have to defend ourselves against the excess of information" (p. 186) — and adding variables does not converge the model on its object: "The closer this model moves toward the original one within a range of certain imitated variables, the further away it moves from that original model within a range of others" (p. 187). Follow that to the end and you get the famous line: "The most perfect model of an apple is offered by another apple, of the Universe by another Universe" (p. 188). He labels it at once — "This may sound like a reductio ad absurdum of imitological practice" — and refuses it, because something exists that is not a copy and holds more than the copied thing: "a scientific theory. It covers a whole class of phenomena" (p. 188). Which raises a question he cannot answer and says so: a theory yields predictions nobody put in it, so where is the surplus from? "This would be a true informational perpetuum mobile!" (p. 189), and two lines later, "Unfortunately, this issue cannot be resolved on the basis of current information theory" (p. 189).

"On Imitology" (pp. 193–196) closes the chapter with its one ordered forecast. In the age of advanced imitology, "a 'better' theory will be the kind of theory thanks to which we will manage to control evolution, change the speed and range of an organism's regenerative potential, or orchestrate genetic traits in fetuses. All this will turn out to be possible long before, say, we gain the ability to create the chromosome apparatus of a nucleus in an artificial manner" (p. 193). An ordering claim, with a slow item and a fast one. He also states there what design is for: it is "an amplifier of the states of low probability" (p. 194), and where Nature is "in its element" as a designer — self-organisation at scale — "we are at our weakest" (p. 196).

One more piece belongs here, from ch. 4. Induction "is an attempt to transform incomplete into complete information. It thus constitutes a transgression against the law of information theory" (p. 124), and every regulator commits it: "every homeostat, that is, regulator, which aims to maintain its significant variables within the parameters whose transgression threatens its existence, must show 'belief' or act on the basis of incomplete and uncertain information as if this information was both complete and certain" (p. 125). Refusing to act "would mean stopping life processes" (p. 125). He then proposes an experiment nobody has run: build "believing machines" (p. 138) with "the variability of goals—which is a cybernetic equivalent of 'free will'" (p. 138), leave the brain unprogrammed, vary body and world, and read off the metaphysics each grows. A machine raised among religious people, he predicts, will adopt their model and "will demand equal rights with the practitioners of the religion with which it will identify from then on" (p. 140). The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain, Gödel three decades old, Rosenblatt's perceptron six years old, and the Bohr–Einstein and Brouwer–Hilbert disputes — both long over, both cited by Lem as evidence that foundations are unsettled. One caveat on dating: Zylinska translates the fourth edition of 1974, so "1964" here means the book's argument, not a guarantee that every sentence stood in the first edition.

What happened

Half the Designer arrived, and nobody had to argue for him. Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson" (2019) says seventy years of the field show that general methods leveraging computation beat methods built out of human understanding. That is the pragmatism with Lem's clause struck out — the method is to know less about the bricks — and it is a comparison, not a forecast he made. The situation underneath is stranger still. In 2017 a paper showed that standard networks fit random labels perfectly, so the textbook account of why they generalise cannot be true. The field carried on, and the carrying on worked: model loss falls along a smooth power law nobody derived from first principles. Frontier labs state plainly that they do not understand the insides of what they ship. Lem's builder knew his bricks; this one does not.

His proposals about method fared better. The "side entrance" (p. 192) comes with a literal example: write an organism's chromosomes down "on a piece of paper, in the symbolic language of chemistry", synthesise them, and let the "laboratory egg" obtained that way go into development (p. 192). Half of that happened in 2010 — a bacterial genome assembled from digitised sequence and transplanted into a recipient cell, which then replicated under the synthetic chromosome alone. Not an egg, no embryogenesis, a bacterium — but the step from notation back to a running organism was taken. Zylinska records that Lem, revisiting the book in the 1980s and 1990s, updated its examples with synthetic biology (p. 21), so the later Lem had already claimed this ground; those afterwords are not in this edition and are not scored. The looser version of the move — arrive at Nature's answer without retracing its road — is what structure prediction did: AlphaFold2 does not solve the physics of folding, and its authors shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for protein-structure prediction.

Two neighbouring proposals went nowhere. He suggests starting an egg's development, removing what is not wanted, and growing the rest into "something like an 'artificial brain,' created from natural tissue" (p. 191). It has not been tried, so far as this essay can establish. The nearest object is a cousin with a different history: cultured neurons on electrode arrays trained in closed loop, a Melbourne group reporting in 2022 that a dish of some 800,000 cells learned to play Pong. No embryo is pruned to get there. He also expects comprehensive knowledge of the genetic code to let us "mass-produce 'talent matrices'" and "'embed' traits chosen by parents (musical talent, mathematical talent, etc.) into the genotype of any egg" (p. 192) — conditional on knowledge nobody has, and so not gradeable either way.

Two things sit outside the ledger because the transposition is mine, not his, and both are worth stating anyway. First, his rule for closing arguments — resolve it by experiment, and what no experiment can settle "disappears" — assumes the experiment stays honest, and in 2026 the measurement decays once it becomes the target: benchmarks saturate, test items leak into training sets, small rewordings move scores meant to measure capability. That is not a refutation of a rule about metaphysical questions; it is the bill for living by it. Second, the ladder. He argues, about mathematics and physical reality, that it is meaningless to ask which rung corresponds to which part of the mountain. Carried across to the machines we build, the rule does not hold: a transformer trained only on legal Othello moves carries an internal board representation that can be read out and edited to steer its play, and sparse autoencoders pull a feature dictionary from a production model whose identified features name recognisable things and change behaviour when clamped. Lem's own Designer would have wanted this: it is how you get to know your bricks.

The ordering came out backwards. The artificial chromosome apparatus of a nucleus — the item he put last — is done: an international consortium has synthesised and characterised all sixteen nuclear chromosomes of brewer's yeast, plus a designed seventeenth carrying its transfer-RNA genes. The item he put first, orchestrating genetic traits in fetuses, is not: what exists is selection among embryos already made and a call from the professional societies for a ten-year moratorium on heritable editing. Writing a chromosome turned out to be a manufacturing problem; orchestrating a trait is not.

The belief material came back in a form he would have enjoyed. His homeostat acts on incomplete information as if it were complete, because refusing to act is not available. In 2025 OpenAI published an account of hallucination that reaches a formally similar place by another road: models are graded by tests where an abstention scores zero and a guess sometimes scores one. Lem's regulator believes because it must; ours because we score it like a student. His experiment, though, was never run — nobody has raised populations of homeostats in varied worlds to see which metaphysics they grow, and this essay found no attempt. The nearest thing is an argument: a 2024 paper arguing AI welfare should be taken seriously, and a lab opening a model-welfare programme in April 2025. He expected the demand for rights from the machine; so far it comes from us.

Where Lem was better than us

He knew it was a bargain and priced both halves. "The Silence of the Designer" is a list of things given up — the definitive question, the demand that a model resemble its object, the hope that usefulness and truth are one word — and a list of things kept, starting with the bricks. He calls the posture "active silence" (p. 176): not having nothing to say, but deciding which sentences are not worth finishing. The 2026 field practises the giving-up and mostly does not know it is a choice; it treats "we don't know why it works" as an embarrassment rather than a term of a contract. Lem also says what the silence is for, which the bitter lesson does not: the Designer drops questions because he is trying to change something — "we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176). Strip that clause and the method has no stopping rule, which is roughly where a benchmark culture without a theory of what it measures has ended up.

Where he was worst

He got the order of difficulty backwards, in the one place in this chapter where he committed to an order. Orchestrating traits in a fetus was to come long before building a nucleus's chromosomes from notation; the chromosomes came first, and the orchestration is not in sight. The mistake has a shape worth naming, because it is the same one as the talent matrices two pages earlier: he treated "knowing the code" as the hard part and the intervention as the easy consequence. It was the other way round. Writing a chromosome turned out to be a manufacturing problem, solvable by people who never had a theory of what the sequence means; arranging a trait in a developing organism requires exactly the comprehensive causal knowledge he assumed would arrive first, and does not have.

The second failure is the chapter's, not a claim's. Most of what Lem writes here is a working style, offered as a way to think rather than a forecast with a date, and the ledger above records that honestly: six of eight rows are OPEN. That is not a weakness of the grading — it is the finding. This is where the book's method runs out, and essay 0 will have to account for it: a prophet who mostly describes how to hold a problem cannot be scored the way one who names devices and years can, and the temptation to score him anyway — by carrying his rules across to objects he never described — is exactly the failure this essay had to be rebuilt twice to avoid.

Still open

  • Whether mathematics ever gives the orders — the warehouse becoming "a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184). What would close it: a physical system built to instantiate a formal structure with no natural counterpart, and something useful done with it.
  • Whether the surplus is real. Can a system end with more structural information than went in? The 2026 evidence pulls both ways without settling it: superhuman play bootstrapped from rules alone, models degrading when trained on their own output. What would close it: an accounting that distinguishes new structural information from re-encoded rules — or a proof that the accounting cannot be done.
  • Whether "useful" can be measured once the measurement is contaminated. What would close it: an evaluation regime whose scores survive the systems trained against them — held-out, adversarial, audited — or a decade of capability claims nobody can check.

Ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–131) is the same argument in another key, and "The Beliefs of Electric Brains" (pp. 138–141) is the experiment nobody has run.
