Essay 5 · Lem ch. 5, "Prolegomena to Omnipotence" (2013 tr., pp. 164–196), with ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–141)

Real means useful

There is a figure in the middle of this chapter that anyone who has worked near machine learning will recognise. Lem calls him the Designer. He refuses to be drawn into philosophy, declines every question no experiment could settle, holds that "properties belong to situations, not things", and for him "'real' means the same as 'useful'." In 1964 that was a position to be argued for. In 2026 it is not a position at all — it is the default method of a field that builds things it cannot explain, and the question worth asking is what the method costs.

What Lem said

The chapter is Lem's attempt to say how one would go about building anything at all, and he starts by widening the word. "A machine is thus a system that manifests some kind of regularity of behavior: statistical, probabilistic, or deterministic" (p. 165) — an atom is one, an apple tree is one. He also warns himself about the two ways this kind of talk fails. On one side is Scylla: "there are also periods like the current one, when practically no inviolable scientific propositions exist" (p. 175). On the other is Charybdis, "a whirlwind of cosmic garrulousness straight from science fiction" (p. 175) full of "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175). His compass between them is deliberately narrow: "we need some restraint—but on what level? The level of design. It is because we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176).

Then comes the Designer. A working engineer with a theory of knowledge he refuses to defend. "The designer knows everything about his bricks—except for what they look like when no one is looking at them. He knows that properties belong to situations, not things" (p. 176). Asked whether a substance is "really" bitter when only some people taste it so, he says the question makes no sense; asked what the world looks like with nobody in it, he answers with another — which physics do you mean, this century's or the next? He is not a sceptic: "He believes in the existence of one reality—which can be interpreted in an infinite number of ways… He is thus indeed a pragmatist, and 'real' means the same as 'useful' to him" (p. 177). And he has a rule for closing arguments: "The Designer suggests that everything should be resolved by means of an experiment, and if an experiment is not possible, and never will be, the issue disappears for him" (p. 179). He leaves "How does mathematics exist?" and "Why does the world exist?" unanswered "not because of his fondness for ignorance but because he knows the consequences of considering such questions" (p. 179).

The next section is the best-known thing in the chapter. A mad tailor sews clothes for nobody: spherical garments with no openings, tubes he calls sleeves in arbitrary numbers, all consistent, all stacked in a warehouse. "Mathematics works in the same way. It builds structures but it is not clear of what" (p. 180). Lem quotes Russell — "mathematics may be defined as the subject in which we never know what we are talking about, nor whether what we are saying is true" (p. 180) — and then makes the point that matters: "Since the sixteenth century, physicists have been searching through the warehouses of 'empty clothes' created by mathematics. Matrix calculus was an 'empty structure' until Heisenberg found a 'piece of the world' that matched" (p. 181). From this he draws a conclusion about what a theory is: mathematics is not a picture of the world, it "becomes more like a ladder that can be used to climb a mountain, even though it does not itself resemble a mountain in any way" (p. 182). And then the sentence this essay will have to grade: "Yet the question as to which parts of this mountain correspond to the ranks of the ladder makes no sense. The ranks are used for climbing to the top. In the same way, we must not ask whether the ladder is 'true.' It can only be better or worse—as a tool for achieving a goal" (p. 183). He allows the traffic to reverse one day — "Mathematics will be a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184) — but not yet.

"Models and Reality" turns the same knife on modelling. "Modeling is an imitation of Nature that takes into account few of its characteristics" (p. 186), and the simplification is the point: "we have to defend ourselves against the excess of information" (p. 186). Add variables and the model does not converge on its object, it diverges elsewhere — "The closer this model moves toward the original one within a range of certain imitated variables, the further away it moves from that original model within a range of others" (p. 187). Follow that to the end and you get the line everyone quotes: "The most perfect model of an apple is offered by another apple, of the Universe by another Universe" (p. 188). He labels it at once — "This may sound like a reductio ad absurdum of imitological practice" — and refuses it, because something exists that is not a copy and holds more than the thing copied: "a scientific theory. It covers a whole class of phenomena" (p. 188). Which raises a question he cannot answer and says so: a theory yields predictions nobody put into it, so where did the surplus come from? "This would be a true informational perpetuum mobile!" (p. 189) — and, two lines later, "Unfortunately, this issue cannot be resolved on the basis of current information theory" (p. 189).

One more piece belongs here, from the previous chapter. Induction "is an attempt to transform incomplete into complete information. It thus constitutes a transgression against the law of information theory" (p. 124), and every regulator commits it: "every homeostat, that is, regulator, which aims to maintain its significant variables within the parameters whose transgression threatens its existence, must show 'belief' or act on the basis of incomplete and uncertain information as if this information was both complete and certain" (p. 125). "Every action starts from a position of knowledge that contains gaps" (p. 125); refusing to act "would mean stopping life processes" (p. 125). He then proposes an experiment nobody has run: build "believing machines" (p. 138) with "the variability of goals—which is a cybernetic equivalent of 'free will'" (p. 138), leave the brain unprogrammed, vary the body and the world, and see which metaphysics each grows. A machine raised among religious people, he predicts, will adopt their model and "will demand equal rights with the practitioners of the religion with which it will identify from then on" (p. 140). The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain, Gödel's incompleteness results three decades old, Rosenblatt's perceptron six years old, and the Bohr–Einstein and Brouwer–Hilbert disputes — both long over, both cited by Lem as evidence that the foundations are still unsettled.

What happened

The Designer won, and nobody had to argue for him. The most-quoted position statement in modern machine learning is Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson" (2019): seventy years of the field say that general methods leveraging computation beat methods built out of human understanding, which help in the short term and then plateau. That is restraint at the level of design, restated by someone who had never needed to read Lem. The situation underneath it is stranger than either says. In 2017 a paper showed that standard networks fit random labels perfectly — the architectures that generalise well on real data will as happily memorise noise — so the textbook account of why they generalise cannot be the true one. The field noted this and carried on, and the carrying on worked: capability tracks data, parameters and compute along a smooth curve nobody derived from first principles. Frontier labs state plainly that they do not understand the insides of what they ship.

His concrete predictions about method fared well too. The "side entrance" (pp. 190–191) is stated in this chapter with a literal example: write an organism's chromosomes down "on a piece of paper, in the symbolic language of chemistry," synthesise them, and start development from the result (p. 191). That was done in 2010 — a bacterial genome assembled from digitised sequence and transplanted into a recipient cell, which then replicated on the synthetic chromosome alone. The looser version of the move — arrive at Nature's answer without retracing its road — is what structure prediction did: AlphaFold2 does not solve the physics of folding, and its authors took a Nobel Prize in 2024 for results rather than mechanism. His claim that a model earns its keep by not tracking the variables now has a measurement behind it in the most conservative modelling discipline there is: a learned weather model, trained on reanalysis data and carrying no equations of motion, outperformed the leading physics-based forecast system on about 90 percent of 1,380 verification targets, in under a minute per forecast. The simplification is not the price of the model; it is the model.

Two things went the other way. The first is his rule for closing arguments. "Resolve it by experiment" assumes the experiment stays honest, and the 2026 experience is that the measurement decays as soon as it becomes the target: benchmarks saturate, test items leak into training sets, and small rewordings move scores that were supposed to measure capability — a situation researchers now describe as an evaluation crisis. The pragmatist's criterion is not self-maintaining; "useful" has to be measured by somebody, on something, and that apparatus is now the weakest joint in the field. The second is the ladder. Lem says it is meaningless to ask which rung corresponds to which part of the mountain. In 2026 that question has a research programme, a budget and results: a small transformer trained only on legal Othello moves turns out to carry an internal representation of the board, which can be read out and edited to steer its play; sparse autoencoders pull millions of features out of a production model that correspond to nameable things — cities, people, code bugs — and turning them up or down changes behaviour accordingly. The rungs do map. Not perfectly, not everywhere, but the question he declared malformed turned out to be the productive one.

Two more literal proposals from the same pages split. He suggests starting an egg's development, discarding what is not wanted, and growing the rest into "something like an 'artificial brain,' created from natural tissue" (p. 191). Cultured neurons are now grown on electrode arrays and trained in closed loop — a Melbourne group reported in 2022 that a dish of some 800,000 cells learned to play Pong — his apparatus at a fraction of his ambition. The other failed. He expects that comprehensive knowledge of the genetic code will let us "mass-produce 'talent matrices'" and "'embed' traits chosen by parents (musical talent, mathematical talent, etc.) into the genotype of any egg" (p. 192). No one can embed a trait. What exists is selection among embryos already made — companies scoring IVF embryos on polygenic predictions, including for intelligence — while heritable editing sits under a moratorium the field's own societies reaffirmed in 2025. Choosing among what chance produced is not writing to the genotype.

The belief material came back in a form he would have enjoyed. His homeostat must act on incomplete information as if it were complete, because refusing to act is not available. In 2025 OpenAI published an account of hallucination that says something formally similar: models are graded by tests where an abstention scores zero and a guess sometimes scores one, so the pressure is to answer rather than to say "I don't know". The machine believes because we score it like a student. What was not built is his experiment — nobody has raised populations of homeostats in varied worlds to see which metaphysics they grow. The nearest thing is an argument, not an apparatus: a 2024 paper arguing AI welfare should be taken seriously now, and a lab opening a model-welfare programme in April 2025. Lem expected the demand for rights to come from the machine. So far it comes from us.

Where Lem was better than us

He knew it was a bargain, and he priced it. "The Silence of the Designer" is a list of things given up: the definitive question, the demand that a model resemble its object, the hope that usefulness and truth are the same word. He calls the posture "active silence" (p. 176) — not having nothing to say, but deciding which sentences are not worth the cost of finishing. The 2026 field practises exactly this and mostly does not know it is a choice; it treats "we don't know why it works" as a temporary embarrassment rather than as a term of the contract. Lem also does what the bitter lesson does not: he says what the silence is for. The Designer drops questions because he is trying to change something — "we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176). Strip that clause and the method has no stopping rule, which is roughly where a benchmark culture without a theory of what it measures has ended up. And the other whirlwind still lands: he spent a page mocking "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175) as thinking that costs nothing, and the machines that compose poems arrived without settling one of the questions those banalities skipped.

Where he was worst

He closed the ladder question too early. Ruling that it makes no sense to ask which rung corresponds to which part of the mountain is a tidy way to end an argument with a philosopher, and 2026 has falsified it in the most direct way available: by paying people to ask and getting answers. A network trained only on move sequences builds something that behaves like a board; a production model carries features that name cities and bugs and can be turned like a dial. If the rungs corresponded to nothing, none of that could work. What he got right underneath the error is that the correspondence is partial and licenses no metaphysics: the features are not a translation of the model into English, and interpretability has not made a frontier system explicable. But "partial and hard" is a different verdict from "malformed", and the difference matters, because the 2026 argument about whether these systems understand anything is being fought on ground he declared empty. Two smaller failures sit beside it. He could imagine writing a genome but not the limits of writing to one — the talent matrices are the chapter's flat miss. And he gave the believing machines several pages and the question of how you would check a machine's usefulness almost none, which is the part that turned out to be hard.

Still open

  • Whether mathematics ever gives the orders. His forecast is that the warehouse becomes "a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184) — structures actualised because they are consistent, not because they fit. What would close it: a physical system built to instantiate a formal structure with no natural counterpart, and something useful done with it.
  • Whether the surplus is real. Can a system end with more structural information than went in? What would close it: an account of self-play and synthetic-data results that distinguishes new information from re-encoded rules — or a proof that the accounting cannot be done.
  • Whether "useful" can be measured after the measurement is contaminated. What would close it: an evaluation regime whose scores survive the systems being trained against them — held-out, adversarial, audited — or a decade of capability claims nobody can check.

Reading

Lem: "The Silence of the Designer" (pp. 176–179) is the chapter in miniature; "Methodological Madness" (pp. 179–184) is the mad tailor and the ladder; "Models and Reality" (pp. 186–190) is the apple. Read ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–131) first — it is the same argument in a different key, and "The Beliefs of Electric Brains" (pp. 138–141) is the experiment nobody has run.
