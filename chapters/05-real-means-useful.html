Essay 5 · Lem ch. 5, "Prolegomena to Omnipotence" (2013 tr., pp. 164–196), with ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–141)

Real means useful

There is a figure in the middle of this chapter that anyone who has worked near machine learning half recognises. Lem calls him the Designer — Zylinska's rendering of Konstruktor, a word she notes carries engineering in it (p. 21). He declines every question no experiment could settle, and for him "'real' means the same as 'useful'." That much arrived. But Lem attaches a clause: "The designer is not a narrow pragmatist, like a builder who is constructing his house from bricks, uninterested in where these bricks came from and what they are, as long as the house gets built" (p. 176). His Designer drops the metaphysics and keeps the materials science. The builders who arrived dropped both, and the whole of interpretability is an attempt to buy the second half back.

What Lem said

The chapter asks how one would go about building anything at all, and starts by widening the word: "A machine is thus a system that manifests some kind of regularity of behavior: statistical, probabilistic, or deterministic" (p. 165) — an atom is one, an apple tree is one. Lem then warns himself about the two ways this talk fails. On one side, Scylla: "there are also periods like the current one, when practically no inviolable scientific propositions exist" (p. 175). On the other, Charybdis, "a whirlwind of cosmic garrulousness straight from science fiction" (p. 175) full of "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175). His compass is deliberately narrow: "we need some restraint—but on what level? The level of design. It is because we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176).

Then comes the Designer. A working engineer with a theory of knowledge he refuses to defend — and the refusal is narrower than it looks. "The designer is not a narrow pragmatist, like a builder who is constructing his house from bricks, uninterested in where these bricks came from and what they are, as long as the house gets built. The designer knows everything about his bricks—except for what they look like when no one is looking at them. He knows that properties belong to situations, not things" (p. 176). What he gives up is the observer-independent residue, not the engineering. Asked whether a substance is "really" bitter when only some taste it so, he says the question makes no sense; asked what the world looks like with nobody in it, he asks which physics you mean, this century's or the next. He is not a sceptic: "He believes in the existence of one reality—which can be interpreted in an infinite number of ways… He is thus indeed a pragmatist, and 'real' means the same as 'useful' to him" (p. 177). And he has a rule for closing arguments: "The Designer suggests that everything should be resolved by means of an experiment, and if an experiment is not possible, and never will be, the issue disappears for him" (p. 179). He leaves "How does mathematics exist?" and "Why does the world exist?" unanswered "not because of his fondness for ignorance but because he knows the consequences of considering such questions" (p. 179).

The next section is the best-known thing in the chapter. A mad tailor sews clothes for nobody: spherical garments with no openings, tubes he calls sleeves in arbitrary numbers, all consistent, all stacked in a warehouse. "Mathematics works in the same way. It builds structures but it is not clear of what" (p. 180). Russell is quoted — "mathematics may be defined as the subject in which we never know what we are talking about, nor whether what we are saying is true" (p. 180) — and then comes the point that matters: "Since the sixteenth century, physicists have been searching through the warehouses of 'empty clothes' created by mathematics. Matrix calculus was an 'empty structure' until Heisenberg found a 'piece of the world' that matched" (p. 181). From this he draws a conclusion about what a theory is: mathematics is not a picture of the world, it "becomes more like a ladder that can be used to climb a mountain, even though it does not itself resemble a mountain in any way" (p. 182). And then the sentence this essay will have to grade: "Yet the question as to which parts of this mountain correspond to the ranks of the ladder makes no sense. The ranks are used for climbing to the top. In the same way, we must not ask whether the ladder is 'true.' It can only be better or worse—as a tool for achieving a goal" (p. 183). He allows the traffic to reverse one day — "Mathematics will be a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184) — but not yet.

"Models and Reality" turns the same knife on modelling. Simplification is the point — "we have to defend ourselves against the excess of information" (p. 186) — and adding variables does not converge the model on its object: "The closer this model moves toward the original one within a range of certain imitated variables, the further away it moves from that original model within a range of others" (p. 187). Follow that to the end and you get the famous line: "The most perfect model of an apple is offered by another apple, of the Universe by another Universe" (p. 188). He labels it at once — "This may sound like a reductio ad absurdum of imitological practice" — and refuses it, because something exists that is not a copy and holds more than the copied thing: "a scientific theory. It covers a whole class of phenomena" (p. 188). Which raises a question he cannot answer and says so: a theory yields predictions nobody put in it, so where is the surplus from? "This would be a true informational perpetuum mobile!" (p. 189), and two lines later, "Unfortunately, this issue cannot be resolved on the basis of current information theory" (p. 189).

One more piece belongs here, from ch. 4. Induction "is an attempt to transform incomplete into complete information. It thus constitutes a transgression against the law of information theory" (p. 124), and every regulator commits it: "every homeostat, that is, regulator, which aims to maintain its significant variables within the parameters whose transgression threatens its existence, must show 'belief' or act on the basis of incomplete and uncertain information as if this information was both complete and certain" (p. 125). Refusing to act "would mean stopping life processes" (p. 125). He then proposes an experiment nobody has run: build "believing machines" (p. 138) with "the variability of goals—which is a cybernetic equivalent of 'free will'" (p. 138), leave the brain unprogrammed, vary body and world, and read off the metaphysics each grows. A machine raised among religious people, he predicts, will adopt their model and "will demand equal rights with the practitioners of the religion with which it will identify from then on" (p. 140). The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain, Gödel three decades old, Rosenblatt's perceptron six years old, and the Bohr–Einstein and Brouwer–Hilbert disputes — both long over, both cited by Lem as evidence that foundations are unsettled.

What happened

Half the Designer arrived, and nobody had to argue for him. Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson" (2019) says seventy years of the field show that general methods leveraging computation beat methods built out of human understanding, which help briefly and then plateau. That is Lem's pragmatism with his clause struck out — the method is to know less about the bricks and let computation find the structure. The situation underneath is stranger still. In 2017 a paper showed that standard networks fit random labels perfectly — architectures that generalise well on real data will as happily memorise noise — so the textbook account of why they generalise cannot be true. The field noted it and carried on, and the carrying on worked: model loss falls along a smooth power law in data, parameters and compute that nobody derived from first principles. Frontier labs state plainly that they do not understand the insides of what they ship.

His proposals about method fared better. The "side entrance" (p. 192) comes with a literal example: write an organism's chromosomes down "on a piece of paper, in the symbolic language of chemistry", synthesise them, and let the "laboratory egg" obtained that way go into development (p. 192). Half of that happened in 2010 — a bacterial genome assembled from digitised sequence and transplanted into a recipient cell, which then replicated under the synthetic chromosome alone. Not an egg, no embryogenesis, a bacterium — but the step he pointed at, from notation back to a running organism, was taken. Zylinska records that Lem, revisiting the book in the 1980s and 1990s, updated its examples with synthetic biology among others (p. 21), so the later Lem had already claimed this ground; those afterwords are not in this edition and are not scored. The looser version of the move — arrive at Nature's answer without retracing its road — is what structure prediction did: AlphaFold2 does not solve the physics of folding, and its authors took a Nobel Prize in 2024 for results rather than mechanism.

Two neighbouring proposals went nowhere. He suggests starting an egg's development, removing what is not wanted, and growing the rest into "something like an 'artificial brain,' created from natural tissue" (p. 191). Nobody does this. What exists is a cousin with a different history: cultured neurons on electrode arrays trained in closed loop, a Melbourne group reporting in 2022 that a dish of some 800,000 cells learned to play Pong. No embryo is pruned to get there. And he expects comprehensive knowledge of the genetic code to let us "mass-produce 'talent matrices'" and "'embed' traits chosen by parents (musical talent, mathematical talent, etc.) into the genotype of any egg" (p. 192). Nothing of the kind is done. What is sold is selection among embryos already made — companies scoring IVF embryos on polygenic predictions, including for intelligence — while the professional societies called in 2025 for a ten-year moratorium on heritable editing. Choosing among what chance produced is not writing to the genotype; whether the writing ever becomes possible is left open by his own condition, "comprehensive knowledge".

Two things went the other way. Lem's rule for closing arguments — resolve it by experiment, and what no experiment can settle "disappears" — assumes the experiment stays honest, and the measurement decays once it becomes the target: benchmarks saturate, test items leak into training sets, small rewordings move scores meant to measure capability. That is not a refutation of his rule, which was about metaphysical questions; it is the bill for living by it. And the ladder. He says it is meaningless to ask which rung corresponds to which part of the mountain — his targets are mathematics and a photograph, not a machine. Apply the rule anyway to the machines we build, as this essay does, and it fails: a transformer trained only on legal Othello moves carries an internal representation of the board that can be read out and edited to steer its play, and sparse autoencoders pull a feature dictionary out of a production model in which identified features name recognisable things and change behaviour when clamped. Lem's own Designer would have wanted this: it is how you get to know your bricks.

The belief material came back in a form he would have enjoyed. His homeostat acts on incomplete information as if it were complete, because refusing to act is not available. In 2025 OpenAI published an account of hallucination that reaches a formally similar place by another road: models are graded by tests where an abstention scores zero and a guess sometimes scores one, so the pressure is to answer rather than say "I don't know". Lem's regulator believes because it must; ours because we score it like a student. What was never built is his experiment — nobody has raised populations of homeostats in varied worlds to see which metaphysics they grow. The nearest thing is an argument, not an apparatus: a 2024 paper arguing AI welfare should be taken seriously now, and a lab opening a model-welfare programme in April 2025. He expected the demand for rights from the machine; so far it comes from us — and the question his experiment was to answer, how a regulator comes by its picture of the world, is pursued instead by taking apart systems trained on ours.

Where Lem was better than us

He knew it was a bargain and priced both halves. "The Silence of the Designer" is a list of things given up — the definitive question, the demand that a model resemble its object, the hope that usefulness and truth are one word — and a list of things kept, starting with the bricks. He calls the posture "active silence" (p. 176): not having nothing to say, but deciding which sentences are not worth finishing. The 2026 field practises the giving-up and mostly does not know it is a choice; it treats "we don't know why it works" as an embarrassment rather than a term of a contract. Lem also says what the silence is for, which the bitter lesson does not: the Designer drops questions because he is trying to change something — "we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176). Strip that clause and the method has no stopping rule, which is roughly where a benchmark culture without a theory of what it measures has ended up. And the other whirlwind lands: he spent a page mocking "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175) as thinking that costs nothing, and those machines arrived without settling one question the banalities skipped.

Where he was worst

He could not imagine the pragmatism arriving without the knowledge. What makes his Designer sympathetic is the clause separating him from the builder who does not care what bricks are: this one knows his materials completely and declines only the question of what they are unobserved. That figure did not arrive. What arrived declines the metaphysics and the metallurgy, and calls the second a research problem. With the bricks unknown, "useful" carries the whole weight of "real", and the apparatus that measures usefulness is the joint under most strain. Lem never asks what maintains an experiment, because his Designer had a laboratory and a materials list; ours has a leaderboard.

His flat over-reach is in the biology. "Talent matrices" — traits chosen by parents and written into any egg's genotype — is offered as a consequence of knowing the code, and sixty years of knowing a great deal of the code have produced selection among embryos, not writing to them. He also priced an artificial brain as a matter of starting an embryo and discarding the surplus. Both share the pattern of his ladder ruling: he was quickest where a question could be settled by design, and what resisted him was where the material would not do what the notation said.

Still open

  • Whether mathematics ever gives the orders. His forecast: the warehouse becomes "a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184) — structures actualised because they are consistent, not because they fit. What would close it: a physical system built to instantiate a formal structure with no natural counterpart, and something useful done with it.
  • Whether the surplus is real. Can a system end with more structural information than went in? The 2026 evidence pulls both ways without settling it: superhuman play bootstrapped from rules alone, models degrading when trained on their own output. What would close it: an accounting that distinguishes new structural information from re-encoded rules — or a proof that the accounting cannot be done.
  • Whether "useful" can be measured once the measurement is contaminated. What would close it: an evaluation regime whose scores survive the systems trained against them — held-out, adversarial, audited — or a decade of capability claims nobody can check.
  • Whether the ladder rule survives its own domain. Lem denies that the rungs of a mathematical model correspond part for part to the mountain; in a learned model some do. What would close it for mathematics: a physical theory whose symbols are shown to correspond individually to structures in the world — or a demonstration that the interpretability correspondences are an artefact of training.
