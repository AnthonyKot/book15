Essay 5 · Lem ch. 5, "Prolegomena to Omnipotence" (2013 tr., pp. 164–196), with ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–141)

Real means useful

There is a figure in the middle of this chapter that anyone who has worked near machine learning will recognise immediately. Lem calls him the Designer. He is not a philosopher and refuses to be drawn into philosophy; he declines every question that no experiment could settle; he holds that "properties belong to situations, not things"; and for him "'real' means the same as 'useful'." In 1964 that was a position to be argued for. In 2026 it is not a position at all — it is the default working method of a field that builds things it cannot explain, and the interesting question is what the method costs.

What Lem said

The chapter is Lem's attempt to say how one would go about building anything at all, and he starts by widening the word. "A machine is thus a system that manifests some kind of regularity of behavior: statistical, probabilistic, or deterministic" (p. 165) — an atom is one, an apple tree is one. He also warns himself, at length, about the two ways this kind of talk fails. On one side is Scylla: the foundations of physics are in open dispute, and "there are also periods like the current one, when practically no inviolable scientific propositions exist" (p. 175). On the other is Charybdis, "a whirlwind of cosmic garrulousness straight from science fiction" (p. 175) full of "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175). His compass between them is deliberately narrow: "we need some restraint—but on what level? The level of design. It is because we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176).

Then comes the Designer. He is a working engineer with a theory of knowledge he refuses to defend. "The designer knows everything about his bricks—except for what they look like when no one is looking at them. He knows that properties belong to situations, not things" (p. 176). Asked whether a substance is "really" bitter when only some people taste bitterness, he says the question makes no sense. Asked what the world looks like with nobody in it, he answers with another question — which physics do you mean, this century's or the next? He is not a sceptic: "He believes in the existence of one reality—which can be interpreted in an infinite number of ways. Some of these interpretations allow him to achieve the desired goals. He turns them into his tool. He is thus indeed a pragmatist, and 'real' means the same as 'useful' to him" (p. 177). And he has a rule for closing arguments: "The Designer suggests that everything should be resolved by means of an experiment, and if an experiment is not possible, and never will be, the issue disappears for him" (p. 179). He leaves "How does mathematics exist?" and "Why does the world exist?" unanswered "not because of his fondness for ignorance but because he knows the consequences of considering such questions" (p. 179).

The section that follows is the best-known thing in the chapter. A mad tailor sews clothes for nobody: spherical garments with no openings, tubes he calls sleeves in arbitrary numbers, all perfectly consistent, all stacked in a warehouse. "Mathematics works in the same way. It builds structures but it is not clear of what" (p. 180). Lem quotes Russell — "mathematics may be defined as the subject in which we never know what we are talking about, nor whether what we are saying is true" (p. 180) — and then makes the point that matters: "Since the sixteenth century, physicists have been searching through the warehouses of 'empty clothes' created by mathematics. Matrix calculus was an 'empty structure' until Heisenberg found a 'piece of the world' that matched that empty structure" (p. 181). From this he draws a conclusion about what a theory is. Mathematics is not a picture of the world; it "becomes more like a ladder that can be used to climb a mountain, even though it does not itself resemble a mountain in any way" (p. 182). And then the sentence this essay will have to grade: "Yet the question as to which parts of this mountain correspond to the ranks of the ladder makes no sense. The ranks are used for climbing to the top. In the same way, we must not ask whether the ladder is 'true.' It can only be better or worse—as a tool for achieving a goal" (p. 183). He allows the traffic to reverse one day — "Mathematics will be a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 183) — but not yet.

"Models and Reality" turns the same knife on modelling. "Modeling is an imitation of Nature that takes into account few of its characteristics" (p. 186), and the simplification is not a regrettable necessity but the whole point: "we have to defend ourselves against the excess of information" (p. 186). Add variables and the model does not converge on its object, it diverges elsewhere — "The closer this model moves toward the original one within a range of certain imitated variables, the further away it moves from that original model within a range of others" (p. 187). Follow that to the end and you get the line everyone quotes: "The most perfect model of an apple is offered by another apple, of the Universe by another Universe" (p. 188). He immediately labels it — "This may sound like a reductio ad absurdum of imitological practice" — and refuses it, because there exists something that is not a copy and holds more than the thing copied: "a scientific theory. It covers a whole class of phenomena" (p. 188). Which raises a question he cannot answer and says so. A theory yields predictions nobody put into it; where did the surplus come from? "This would be a true informational perpetuum mobile!" (p. 189) — and, two lines later, "Unfortunately, this issue cannot be resolved on the basis of current information theory" (p. 189).

One more piece belongs to this essay, from the previous chapter. Lem argues that induction "is an attempt to transform incomplete into complete information. It thus constitutes a transgression against the law of information theory" (p. 124), and that every regulator commits it: "every homeostat, that is, regulator, which aims to maintain its significant variables within the parameters whose transgression threatens its existence, must show 'belief' or act on the basis of incomplete and uncertain information as if this information was both complete and certain" (p. 125). "Every action starts from a position of knowledge that contains gaps" (p. 125); refusing to act "would mean stopping life processes" (p. 125). He then proposes an experiment that has never been run: build "believing machines" (p. 138), homeostats with "the variability of goals—which is a cybernetic equivalent of 'free will'" (p. 138), leave the brain unprogrammed, vary the body and the world, and see which metaphysics each one grows. He predicts, among other things, that a machine raised among religious people will adopt their model and "will demand equal rights with the practitioners of the religion with which it will identify from then on" (p. 140). The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain, Wiener, Gödel's incompleteness results three decades old, Rosenblatt's perceptron six years old, and physics in the middle of the Bohr–Einstein argument he keeps citing as evidence that foundations are not settled.

What happened

The Designer won, and nobody had to argue for him. The most-quoted position statement in modern machine learning is Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson" (2019): seventy years of the field say that "general methods that leverage computation" beat methods built out of human understanding, which "always helps in the short term" and then plateaus. That is Lem's restraint-at-the-level-of-design, restated by someone who had never needed to read him. The empirical situation underneath it is stranger than either of them says. In 2017 a paper showed that the standard networks fit random labels perfectly — the same architectures that generalise well on real data will happily memorise noise — which means the textbook account of why they generalise cannot be the true one. The field noted this and carried on, and the carrying on worked: capability tracks data, parameters and compute along a smooth curve nobody derived from first principles. Frontier labs state plainly that they do not understand the insides of what they ship. An engineer from 1964 who declined to ask the definitive question would recognise every step of this.

His concrete predictions about method fared well too. The "side entrance" — don't retrace Nature's road, arrive at its endpoint from somewhere else (pp. 190–191) — is what structure prediction did: AlphaFold2 does not solve the physics of folding, it learns correlations across sequence and structure databases, and its authors took a Nobel Prize in 2024 for the results rather than for a mechanism. His stranger claim, that a model which ignores variables can beat one that tracks them, is now a measured fact in the most conservative modelling discipline there is: a learned weather model trained on reanalysis data beat the leading physics-based forecast system on about 90 percent of 1,380 verification targets, in under a minute per forecast. The apple that is not an apple wins.

Two things went the other way. The first is his rule for closing arguments. "Resolve it by experiment" assumes the experiment stays honest, and the 2026 experience is that the measurement decays as soon as it becomes the target: benchmarks saturate, test items leak into training sets, and small rewordings move scores that were supposed to measure capability — a situation researchers now describe as an evaluation crisis. The pragmatist's criterion is not self-maintaining; "useful" has to be measured by somebody, on something, and that apparatus is now the weakest joint in the field. The second is the ladder. Lem says it is meaningless to ask which rung corresponds to which part of the mountain. In 2026 that question has a research programme, a budget and results: a small transformer trained only on legal Othello moves turns out to carry an internal representation of the board, which can be read out and edited to steer its play; sparse autoencoders pull millions of features out of a production model that correspond to nameable things — cities, people, code bugs — and turning them up or down changes behaviour accordingly. The rungs do map. Not perfectly, not everywhere, but the question he declared malformed turned out to be the productive one.

And the belief material has come back in a form he would have enjoyed. His homeostat must act on incomplete information as if it were complete, because refusing to act is not available. In 2025 OpenAI published an account of hallucination which says something formally similar about language models: they are graded by tests where an abstention scores zero and a guess sometimes scores one, so the training pressure is to answer rather than to say "I don't know". The machine believes because we score it like a student. What has not been built is his actual experiment — nobody has raised populations of homeostats in varied worlds to see which metaphysics they grow. The nearest thing is an argument, not an apparatus: a 2024 paper by philosophers and lab researchers arguing that AI welfare should be taken seriously now, and a frontier lab opening a model-welfare research programme in April 2025. Lem expected the demand for rights to come from the machine. So far it comes from us.

Where Lem was better than us

He knew it was a bargain, and he priced it. Everything in "The Silence of the Designer" is written as a series of things given up: the definitive question, the demand that the model resemble its object, the hope that usefulness and truth are the same word. He calls the resulting posture "active silence" (p. 176) — not the silence of someone who has nothing to say, but of someone who has decided which sentences are not worth the cost of finishing. The 2026 field practises exactly this and mostly does not know it is a choice; it experiences "we don't know why it works" as a temporary embarrassment rather than as the term of a contract signed at the outset. Lem also does the thing the bitter lesson does not: he names what the silence is for. The Designer drops questions because he is trying to change something, and "we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176). Strip that clause out and the method has no stopping rule — which is roughly where a benchmark culture without a theory of what it is measuring has ended up. And his warning about the other whirlwind still lands: he wrote a page mocking "banalities about 'machines that compose poems the way Shakespeare did'" (p. 175) as the mark of thinking that costs nothing, and the machines that compose poems arrived without settling a single one of the questions the banalities skipped.

Where he was worst

He closed the ladder question too early. Ruling that it makes no sense to ask which rung corresponds to which part of the mountain is a tidy way to end an argument with a philosopher, and it is the one move in this chapter that 2026 has falsified in the most direct way available: by paying people to ask it and getting answers. A network trained only on move sequences builds something that behaves like a board; a production model carries features that name cities and bugs and can be turned up like a dial. If the rungs did not correspond to anything, none of that could work. What Lem got right underneath the error is that the correspondence is partial and does not license the metaphysics — the features are not a translation of the model into English, and interpretability has not made a frontier system explicable. But "partial and hard" is a different verdict from "malformed", and the difference matters, because the whole 2026 argument about whether these systems understand anything is being fought on the ground he declared empty. The second, smaller failure is a matter of proportion: he gave the believing machines several pages and the question of how you would check a machine's usefulness almost none, and it is the checking that has turned out to be hard.

Still open

  • Whether mathematics ever gives the orders. His forecast is that the warehouse stops being a warehouse and becomes "a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 183) — structures actualised because they are consistent, not because they fit. What would close it: a physical system built to instantiate a formal structure with no natural counterpart, and something useful done with it.
  • Whether the surplus is real. Lem's perpetuum-mobile question is still open: can a system end with more structural information than went in? What would close it: a settled account of self-play and synthetic-data results that distinguishes new information from re-encoded rules — or a proof that the accounting cannot be done.
  • Whether "useful" can be measured after the measurement is contaminated. What would close it: an evaluation regime whose scores survive the systems being trained against them — held-out, adversarial, audited — or a decade of capability claims nobody can check.

Reading

Lem: "The Silence of the Designer" (pp. 176–179) is the chapter in miniature; "Methodological Madness" (pp. 179–184) is the mad tailor and the ladder; "Models and Reality" (pp. 186–190) is the apple. Read ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–131) first — it is the same argument in a different key, and "The Beliefs of Electric Brains" (pp. 138–141) is the experiment nobody has run.

2026 side: Sutton, "The Bitter Lesson" (2019); Zhang et al., "Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization" (ICLR 2017); Lam et al. on GraphCast (Science, 2023); Li et al., "Emergent World Representations" (ICLR 2023) and Anthropic's "Scaling Monosemanticity" (2024); Kalai et al., "Why Language Models Hallucinate" (2025); Long, Sebo et al., "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" (2024).
