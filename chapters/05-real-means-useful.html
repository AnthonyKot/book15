Essay 5 · Lem ch. 5, "Prolegomena to Omnipotence" (2013 tr., pp. 164–196), with ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–141)

Real means useful

There is a figure in the middle of this chapter that anyone who has worked near machine learning half recognises. Lem calls him the Designer. He declines every question no experiment could settle, and for him "'real' means the same as 'useful'" (p. 177). Half of that describes 2026 exactly. The other half does not, and Lem wrote the difference himself: the Designer "is not a narrow pragmatist, like a builder who is constructing his house from bricks, uninterested in where these bricks came from and what they are" (p. 176). He drops the metaphysics and keeps the materials science. The builders who arrived dropped both.

What Lem said

The chapter asks how one would build anything at all, and widens the word first: "A machine is thus a system that manifests some kind of regularity of behavior" (p. 165) — an atom is one, an apple tree is one. Lem then warns himself about the two ways such talk fails: Scylla, that "there are also periods like the current one, when practically no inviolable scientific propositions exist" (p. 175), and Charybdis, "a whirlwind of cosmic garrulousness straight from science fiction" (p. 175). His compass is narrow by choice: "We thus need some restraint—but on what level? The level of design" (p. 176).

Then comes the Designer — a working engineer with a theory of knowledge he refuses to defend, and the refusal is narrower than it looks. He "is not a narrow pragmatist, like a builder who is constructing his house from bricks, uninterested in where these bricks came from and what they are, as long as the house gets built. The designer knows everything about his bricks—except for what they look like when no one is looking at them" (p. 176). What he gives up is the observer-independent residue, not the engineering. He is no sceptic: "He believes in the existence of one reality… He is thus indeed a pragmatist, and 'real' means the same as 'useful' to him" (p. 177). And his rule for closing arguments: "everything should be resolved by means of an experiment, and if an experiment is not possible, and never will be, the issue disappears for him" (p. 179).

The next section is the chapter's best known. A mad tailor sews clothes for nobody, all stacked in a warehouse. "Mathematics works in the same way. It builds structures but it is not clear of what" (p. 180); "physicists have been searching through the warehouses of 'empty clothes'… Matrix calculus was an 'empty structure' until Heisenberg found a 'piece of the world' that matched" (p. 181). Mathematics is not a picture but "more like a ladder that can be used to climb a mountain, even though it does not itself resemble a mountain in any way" (p. 182), and the rung-by-rung question is ruled out: "we must not ask whether the ladder is 'true.' It can only be better or worse—as a tool for achieving a goal" (p. 183). One day the traffic may reverse — "Mathematics will be a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184) — but not yet.

"Models and Reality" turns the same knife on modelling. Simplification is the point — "we have to defend ourselves against the excess of information" (p. 186) — and adding variables does not converge a model on its object: "The closer this model moves toward the original one within a range of certain imitated variables, the further away it moves from that original model within a range of others" (p. 187). Hence "The most perfect model of an apple is offered by another apple, of the Universe by another Universe" (p. 188). He labels it a possible "reductio ad absurdum of imitological practice" and refuses it, because a scientific theory is not a copy and holds more than the thing copied: "It covers a whole class of phenomena" (p. 188). Then the question that follows — where does the surplus come from, when a theory predicts phenomena nobody put into it? — and, this matters, his answer: "there exists a continuity of transformations in the world. It came from their feedback. We have 'guessed' one thing, and this one thing has subsequently 'led' to the others" (p. 188). What he cannot settle is the bookkeeping: x bits in, x + n out, "a true informational perpetuum mobile!" that "cannot be resolved on the basis of current information theory" (p. 189).

"On Imitology" closes the chapter with its one ordered forecast: "a 'better' theory will be the kind of theory thanks to which we will manage to control evolution, change the speed and range of an organism's regenerative potential, or orchestrate genetic traits in fetuses. All this will turn out to be possible long before, say, we gain the ability to create the chromosome apparatus of a nucleus in an artificial manner" (p. 193) — a bundle of three, ordered against one. Design itself is "an amplifier of the states of low probability" (p. 194).

One piece belongs here from ch. 4, which this essay's contract carries. Every regulator commits induction: a homeostat "must show 'belief' or act on the basis of incomplete and uncertain information as if this information was both complete and certain" (p. 125), because refusing "would mean stopping life processes" (p. 125). He then proposes an experiment nobody has run: build "believing machines" (p. 138) with "the variability of goals—which is a cybernetic equivalent of 'free will'" (p. 138), leave the brain unprogrammed, vary body and world, and read off the metaphysics each grows; one raised among religious people "will demand equal rights with the practitioners of the religion with which it will identify" (p. 140). The baseline he wrote from — the mid-1960s, as revised for the 1974 fourth edition this translation follows: Ashby's Design for a Brain, Gödel three decades old, Rosenblatt's perceptron six years old, and the Bohr–Einstein and Brouwer–Hilbert disputes cited as proof that foundations are unsettled. Dating caveat: Zylinska translates the fourth edition of 1974, so "1964" here means the book's argument, not a guarantee about the first edition.

What happened

Half the Designer arrived, and nobody had to argue for him. Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson" (2019) says seventy years of the field show that general methods leveraging computation beat methods built out of human understanding. That is the pragmatism with Lem's clause struck out: the method is to know less about the bricks. The situation underneath is stranger. In 2017 a paper showed that standard networks fit random labels perfectly, so the textbook account of why they generalise cannot be true; the field carried on, and the carrying on worked, loss falling along a smooth empirical power law. Anthropic states that the internals of the models it ships are not understood. Lem's builder knew his bricks; this one does not, and says so.

His proposals about method fared better. The "side entrance" (p. 192) comes with a literal example: write an organism's chromosomes down "on a piece of paper, in the symbolic language of chemistry", synthesise them, and let the "laboratory egg" go into development. The first half happened in 2010 — a bacterial genome assembled from digitised sequence and transplanted into a recipient cell, which replicated under the synthetic chromosome alone. Not an egg, no embryogenesis, a bacterium. Zylinska records that Lem, revisiting the book, updated its examples with synthetic biology while trying to demonstrate successful predictions (p. 21); those afterwords are not in this edition and are not scored here. The looser version of the move — arrive at Nature's answer without retracing its road — is what structure prediction did: AlphaFold2 does not solve the physics of folding, and its authors shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The ordered forecast cannot be settled, which is not the same as his being wrong. All sixteen nuclear chromosomes of brewer's yeast have been synthesised and validated individually, and a designed seventeenth built — but consolidating them into a single organism is still the project's stated final phase, so there is as yet no artificial chromosome apparatus of a nucleus. Nor is any trait orchestrated in a fetus: what exists is selection among embryos already made and a call from the professional societies for a ten-year moratorium on heritable editing. Both sides of his comparison are unfinished, so the ordering stays open. What can be said is that the synthesis half proved more tractable, and that the yeast work needed design as well as manufacture — recoded stop codons, relocated tRNAs, inserted recombination sites, a long debugging cycle.

Two things sit outside the ledger because the transposition is this essay's, not his. First, his rule for closing arguments assumes the experiment stays honest, and in 2026 the measurement decays once it becomes the target: benchmarks saturate, test items leak into training sets, rewordings move scores. That is not a refutation of a rule about metaphysical questions; it is the bill for living by it. Second, an analogy about the ladder — offered as an analogy, since Lem is talking about mathematics and physical reality and this is about trained artifacts. A transformer trained only on legal Othello moves carries an internal board representation that can be read out and edited to steer play, and sparse autoencoders pull a feature dictionary from a production model whose features change behaviour when clamped. Inside a machine we built, the rung-by-rung question has answers. That says nothing about whether it has answers for physics.

The belief material came back in a form he would have enjoyed, and it is also not his claim. His homeostat believes because refusing to act is not available to a living thing. In 2025 OpenAI published an account of hallucination that arrives somewhere formally similar by another road: models are graded by tests where abstention scores zero and a guess sometimes scores one. Lem's regulator believes on pain of death; ours because we mark it like a student. The mechanisms differ and the ledger does not score them together. His actual experiment was never run. The nearest thing is an argument: a 2024 paper that AI welfare should be taken seriously, and a lab opening a model-welfare programme in April 2025. He expected the demand for rights from the machine; so far it comes from us.

Where Lem was better than us

He knew it was a bargain and priced both halves. "The Silence of the Designer" is a list of things given up — the definitive question, the demand that a model resemble its object, the hope that usefulness and truth are one word — and a list of things kept, starting with the bricks. He calls the posture "active silence" (p. 176): not having nothing to say, but deciding which sentences are not worth finishing. What the bitter lesson states as a lesson, Lem states as a choice with a price attached. Lem also says what the silence is for, which the bitter lesson does not: the Designer drops questions because he is trying to change something — "we only want to get to know the world to the extent that we can improve on it" (p. 176). Strip that clause and the method has no stopping rule at all.

Where he was worst

He was wrong about what a designer keeps. The whole force of "The Silence of the Designer" is that giving up the definitive question is not the same as giving up knowledge of the material: his builder "knows everything about his bricks", and drops only what no experiment could settle. Sixty years on, the field that most resembles his Designer took the first half of that bargain and not the second. The stated method is to build with less understanding, not more. The textbook account of why the material works was falsified in 2017 and has not been replaced. The builders say in public that they do not know what is inside what they ship. Lem asked exactly the right question — what does a designer give up, and what must he refuse to give up? — and got the second half wrong, which is the one that mattered.

The rest of this chapter mostly cannot be graded, and the ledger says so: seven of its eight rows are OPEN. That is not a failure of the grading, it is the finding. Lem is describing a working style here, not naming devices with dates, and a working style does not close. The one place he does commit to a dated order — traits orchestrated in fetuses long before an artificial chromosome apparatus — turns out to be unsettleable in a different way: neither side has happened, so the order cannot be scored at all. This is where the book's method runs out, and essay 0 will have to account for it. The temptation to score him anyway, by carrying his rules across to objects he never described, is exactly the failure this essay was rebuilt three times to avoid; the reader who thinks that is an excuse should attack this paragraph first.

Still open

  • Whether mathematics ever gives the orders — the warehouse becoming "a phantomological generator, a creator of worlds" (p. 184). What would close it: a physical system built to instantiate a formal structure with no natural counterpart, and something useful done with it.
  • Whether the surplus is real. Can a system end with more structural information than went in? Two modern cases motivate the question without touching his accounting: superhuman play bootstrapped from rules alone, and models degrading when trained on their own output. Neither is framed as a structural-information balance. What would close it: an accounting that distinguishes new structural information from re-encoded rules — or a proof that the accounting cannot be done.
  • Whether "useful" can be measured once the measurement is contaminated. This is the essay's transposition of his experiment rule, not a question he left open. What would close it: an evaluation regime whose scores demonstrably survive the systems trained against them — or a demonstration that every proposed regime degrades under optimisation, which would settle it the other way.

Reading

Lem: "The Silence of the Designer" (pp. 176–179) is the chapter in miniature; "Methodological Madness" (pp. 179–184) is the mad tailor and the ladder; "Models and Reality" (pp. 186–190) is the apple; "Plagiarism and Creation" (pp. 190–192) is the side entrance. Ch. 4's "Belief and Information" (pp. 124–131) is the same argument in another key, and "The Beliefs of Electric Brains" (pp. 138–141) is the experiment nobody has run.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. Sutton, "The Bitter Lesson" (2019), for the half of the Designer that arrived; Zhang et al. (ICLR 2017), for the falsified account of why the material works; and Gibson et al. (Science, 2010), for the side entrance actually taken. The rest is in the claims register.
