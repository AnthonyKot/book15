Essay 1 · Lem ch. 1, "Dilemmas" (2013 tr., pp. 24–30)

The three ways to be wrong

Seven pages before he begins forecasting, Lem explains why forecasting fails. He names three of the mistakes that irritate him most, grades a working futurist by them, and supplies a test anyone can run: put the believable image of tomorrow in a drawer for a few years. Sixty years later there is a discipline that does exactly that, with scoring rules and tournaments and a literature. It measures something else than what he wanted measured.

What Lem said

The chapter states the book's subject before it states its problem. What Lem proposes to study is not devices but "technologies, i.e., means of bringing about certain collectively determined goals that have been conditioned by the state of our knowledge and our social aptitude—and also those goals that no one has identified at the outset" (p. 24). The mechanism of any particular technology "does not interest me much" (p. 24); what interests him is that goals split and get replaced, so that "man hardly ever knows what he is actually doing—at least he does not know for sure" — illustrated at the limit, since "the destruction of Life on Earth, which is entirely possible today, was not actually the goal of any of the discoverers of atomic energy" (p. 25). That is the subject: consequences nobody chose.

His remedy is not moral. "The only way to deal with technology is with another technology. Man knows more about his dangerous tendencies than he did a hundred years ago, and in the next hundred years, his knowledge will be even more advanced. Then he will make use of it" (p. 26). He is explicit that he does not trust "any promises" or "any assurances built on so-called humanism" (p. 26).

Then he removes the ground from under his own project. The rate of change "also reduces the viability of any prophecies" (p. 27), and the fault lies with professionals. P. M. S. Blackett, physicist and founder of operational research, "and thus a kind of professional foreteller", predicted atomic weapons through 1960 "as inadequately as one could only imagine" (p. 27): he thought warheads would not exceed kilotons, because megatons "would not have any targets that would be worth destroying" (p. 27). In the same paragraph Lem notes the opposite error — that "around 1955, it was believed that the fusion of hydrogen into helium observed in the stars would generate industrial energy in the near future" — and corrects it with a reported consensus rather than a claim of his own: "Current estimates situate the production of the microfusion cell in the 1990s, or even later" (pp. 27–28). The standing warning is Bellamy, who wrote the year 2000 from the 1880s and "consciously ignored all the possible inventions that were still not known to his contemporaries" (p. 27). Lem's test is cruel and good: "Anyone can have some fun by just putting in a drawer for a few years what is currently being described as a believable image of tomorrow" (p. 27).

Only then does he list mistakes, and he is careful about the status of the list. There are, he says, "an infinite variety of reasons that turn making predictions into a thankless task", from which "I shall list several such mistakes that are particularly displeasing to the artist" (p. 28). Three follow. The sudden turn: transformations "sometimes burst forth like Athena from Zeus's head—to the surprise of everyone, including the experts", cybernetics being his example (p. 28). The straight line: "we are always inclined to extend the course of new technologies into the future by means of straight lines, hence 'the world full of balloons' or 'the total steam world'" (p. 28), because History "does not show us any straight paths of development but rather uses the curvilinear zigzags of nonlinear evolution" (p. 28). And the closed schema: "a literary work has a beginning, a middle, and an end… Generally speaking, we have a tendency to place any phenomenon within a closed schema" (p. 29). Ask a thinker of the 1930s what happens when the world splits into two halves able to annihilate each other and the answer is "total annihilation or total disarmament" (p. 29).

His image for the missing third option is qualified, and the qualification matters: "To a certain degree, the world is like a sick man who believes that either he is going to get better soon or die very quickly and to whom it does not even occur that he can, moaning as he does, and going through some short-term ups and downs, go on living until old age" (p. 29). He then calls the comparison "rather shortsighted" and adds what it leaves out — that a cure brings its own bill. "Liberation from oppression… has its price, while the amount of expected repayment and its period, as well as the method of its delivery, usually come as a surprise" (p. 29), his example being radioactive waste, "which we still do not know what we should do about" (p. 29). The chapter ends by refusing endings: history "does not know, and I hope will not know, such definitive closures or 'final ends'" (p. 30). The baseline: Blackett of 1948, Thirring of 1946, Bellamy from the 1880s, a balance of fear about fifteen years old. This translation follows the altered fourth edition of 1974.

What happened

Forecasting did become a discipline, and the first thing it established was that Lem's contempt for professional foretellers was justified. Tetlock collected political and economic predictions from 284 experts starting in 1984 and followed them for two decades; their accuracy was barely distinguishable from chance, which is the finding everyone remembers as the dart-throwing chimpanzee. The second thing it established is that the problem is fixable. In a tournament run by IARPA between 2011 and 2015, forecasters selected and trained for calibration were about 30 percent more accurate than intelligence analysts with access to classified material. Lem's drawer test was made into an institution: register the forecast, wait, score it.

But it measures a different thing than the one he cared about. The tournaments score probabilistic judgment on questions that resolve — an election, a border, a price, within months or a couple of years. Lem's subject was the goals that split and the consequences nobody chose, which do not resolve on a schedule and cannot be scored at all. Where the two do meet, on the technology closest to his own subject, the record is not reassuring: a survey of 2,778 researchers publishing in top AI venues put a 50 percent chance of high-level machine intelligence by 2047, thirteen years earlier than the same series had found from a survey conducted one year before. A thirteen-year move in twelve months is not the behaviour of a discipline; it is the behaviour of a mood.

The taxonomy itself holds up where it can be tested against this book's own scoring, though each mode needs a specific case rather than a mood of its own. The straight line is the best evidenced: essay 2 records the scenario in which the car is replaced by an air-cushion vehicle — the impressive machine of its moment, extended forward — and air-cushion vehicles retreated to a marginal role while what is displacing the combustion engine kept the wheels, the roads and the shape. The sudden turn is evidenced by third options: essay 7 finds that what automated science was neither of the two routes Lem posed but a formalism that executes, and essay 8 finds that selection on heredity, whose partner-screening form he described correctly, also acquired a second and unforeseen venue once the decision could be moved past conception. The closed schema is evidenced by an outcome that refused both endings: essay 6 records headsets that neither replaced reality nor disappeared, but sold, peaked and settled.

His premise about unchosen consequences is the recurring finding of this book. Essay 4's ledger is largely about systems that optimise the objective they were given rather than the one intended; essay 7 found an automated laboratory whose claims of novelty had to be narrowed after the fact, because novelty was not the property anyone had wired it to establish. Sixty years on, "man hardly ever knows what he is actually doing" is not a lament but a design constraint.

Two of his own examples went the way he warned about. The fusion consensus he reported — the 1990s "or even later" — did not arrive, and it still has not: the flagship experiment has passed $20 billion and is decades from operation. He was repeating a contemporary estimate rather than making a forecast, which is exactly the failure mode he had described one sentence earlier, and the sentence remains open rather than wrong, because "or even later" has no terminal bound. His radioactive waste, meanwhile, is still where he left it: as of August 2026 no spent fuel has been permanently disposed of anywhere, with the world's first deep repository still awaiting its operating licence.

Where Lem was better than us

He wrote the disclaimer first and did the work anyway, which is rarer than it sounds. The usual forms are to forecast without admitting a failure rate, or to admit it and use the admission as cover. Lem names mistakes, grades a named contemporary by them, supplies a test anyone can run, and then commits three hundred pages of predictions specific enough to be scored — which is why this book exists and why several of its verdicts go against him. And his third mistake is the one still least noticed. Everyone knows that surprises happen and curves bend; almost nobody notices that the endings we find plausible are the endings that make a story. The two endings on offer for every frightening technology are still two, and the sick man is still the option nobody argues for.

He was also right about what would be hard to measure, and the discipline that arrived proves it by avoiding it. Forecasting tournaments work because they take questions that resolve. Lem's subject — the goal that splits, the consequence nobody chose — was chosen precisely because it does not resolve, and sixty years of methodological progress has not touched it.

Where he was worst

He asked for a better discipline and got one that answers a different question, and this book cannot show that he saw the difference coming. The drawer test is a scoring rule, and scoring rules select for questions with answers. What Lem wanted studied was consequences that arrive late, unbidden and unresolvable — the atomic energy nobody meant to point at life on Earth — and no tournament can score that, because there is nothing to resolve and no date to resolve it on. The gap between "forecasting became rigorous" and "we understand what our technologies are doing to us" is the whole distance between the discipline he asked for and the one that exists, and he did not mark it.

The chapter is also gentler on itself than this essay first claimed. Reporting the contemporary fusion consensus was not a self-inflicted failure of his taxonomy — the sentence is unbounded and not his own estimate — and his list of mistakes was never offered as complete: he says explicitly that there are "an infinite variety of reasons" and that he will name "several such mistakes that are particularly displeasing to the artist" (p. 28). A book that grades a prophet has to be careful not to grade him for a completeness he disclaimed, and the first draft of this essay was not.

Still open

  • Whether knowing the modes helps at all. He named three and this book's ledgers record him being caught by two of them. What would close it: forecasters trained on failure taxonomies beating untrained ones on scored, dated questions — as against training for calibration, which demonstrably works and is a different intervention.
  • Whether the consequences he cared about can be scored at all. Tournaments need resolution dates; goals that split do not have them. What would close it: a scoring method for unintended consequence that survives contact with a real technology — or an argument that the demand is incoherent, which would make his chapter a counsel of despair rather than a programme.
  • Whether another technology is the only answer. He refused humanist assurances outright (p. 26). What would close it: a dangerous capability durably contained by agreement rather than counter-technology — noting that verification and enforcement are themselves technologies, which is why this has stayed open.

Reading

Lem: seven pages, and worth reading before any other chapter. The subject and method are set out at pp. 24–25; the graded forecasters and the drawer test at p. 27; the three mistakes at pp. 28–29, with the disclaimer about "an infinite variety of reasons" immediately before them; the sick man at p. 29, together with the sequel calling the comparison shortsighted.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. Tetlock's Expert Political Judgment (2005), for the state of professional foretelling he suspected; the IARPA tournament results, for what became measurable; and Grace et al. (2024) on AI timelines, for what did not.
