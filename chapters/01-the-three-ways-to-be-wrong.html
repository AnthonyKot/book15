Essay 1 · Lem ch. 1, "Dilemmas" (2013 tr., pp. 24–30)

The three ways to be wrong

Seven pages before he begins forecasting, Lem explains why forecasting fails. He names three mechanisms, illustrates each, and grades two working futurists as they deserve. Then, in the same seven pages, he commits one of the errors himself, in a sentence about fusion power. This essay grades the taxonomy against the one dataset qualified to test it: the ledgers of the seven other essays in this book, which record what actually went wrong when a careful man predicted sixty years forward.

What Lem said

He starts by removing the ground from under his own project. The rate of change, he says, "also reduces the viability of any prophecies" (p. 27), and the fault lies with the professionals rather than the popularisers. His first example is P. M. S. Blackett, physicist and founder of operational research, "and thus a kind of professional foreteller", whose 1948 book predicted atomic weapons through 1960 "as inadequately as one could only imagine" (p. 27) — Blackett thought warheads would not exceed kilotons, because megatons "would not have any targets that would be worth destroying" (p. 27). The standing warning is Bellamy, who wrote the year 2000 from the 1880s and "consciously ignored all the possible inventions that were still not known to his contemporaries", believing "that changes caused by technoevolution were not significant either for the functioning of societies or for an individual psyche" (p. 27). Lem's test for this kind of writing is cruel and good: "Anyone can have some fun by just putting in a drawer for a few years what is currently being described as a believable image of tomorrow" (p. 27).

Then the taxonomy, offered as three ways the future defeats the person describing it.

The first is the sudden turn. "transformations that lead to a sudden turn in existing technologies sometimes burst forth like Athena from Zeus's head—to the surprise of everyone, including the experts. The twentieth century has already been taken aback several times by the newly emergent hegemonies, such as, for example, cybernetics" (p. 28). He notes that a novelist hates this, because it is a deus ex machina, "But what shall we do when History turns out to be so easy to please?" (p. 28).

The second is the straight line. "we are always inclined to extend the course of new technologies into the future by means of straight lines, hence 'the world full of balloons' or 'the total steam world' of the nineteenth-century utopians and draftsmen—both of which seem most amusing today" (p. 28). His contemporary example is science fiction crewing spacecraft with "'watch officers,' 'helmsmen,'" (p. 28), which he calls "a form of 'reverse' nineteenth-century historical novel" (p. 28). History "does not show us any straight paths of development but rather uses the curvilinear zigzags of nonlinear evolution" (p. 28).

The third is the closed schema, and it is the one he thinks nobody notices. "a literary work has a beginning, a middle, and an end… Generally speaking, we have a tendency to place any phenomenon within a closed schema" (p. 29). His illustration is a thought experiment: ask a thinker of the 1930s what happens when the world divides into two halves each able to annihilate the other, and the answer will be "total annihilation or total disarmament" — and he "would also most certainly add that our idea is weak because it is so melodramatic and unbelievable" (p. 29). Then the image the chapter is worth reading for: "the world is like a sick man who believes that either he is going to get better soon or die very quickly and to whom it does not even occur that he can, moaning as he does, and going through some short-term ups and downs, go on living until old age" (pp. 29–30).

Two further pieces belong to the same argument. The premise: "man hardly ever knows what he is actually doing—at least he does not know for sure", illustrated at the limit — "the destruction of Life on Earth, which is entirely possible today, was not actually the goal of any of the discoverers of atomic energy" (p. 25). And the cost that arrives late: "Liberation from oppression… has its price, while the amount of expected repayment and its period, as well as the method of its delivery, usually come as a surprise" (p. 29), his example being radioactive waste, "which we still do not know what we should do about" (p. 29). His remedy is not moral: "The only way to deal with technology is with another technology. Man knows more about his dangerous tendencies than he did a hundred years ago… Then he will make use of it" (p. 26), and he is explicit that he does not "believe in any assurances built on so-called humanism" (p. 26).

And then, four paragraphs after mocking Blackett, he does it himself. Correcting the optimists of 1955 who expected stellar fusion to power industry soon, he writes: "Current estimates situate the production of the microfusion cell in the 1990s, or even later" (p. 28). The baseline he wrote from: Blackett of 1948, Thirring of 1946, Bellamy from the 1880s, a balance of fear about fifteen years old, and controlled fusion widely expected within decades — bearing in mind that this translation follows the altered fourth edition of 1974, so the text is mid-1960s as revised.

What happened

Take the self-inflicted one first, because it is the cleanest. Fusion did not produce industrial power in the 1990s or in the thirty years after. ITER, the flagship, has passed $20 billion and is not expected to be fully operational until 2039; the US fusion programme alone spent $790 million in a single year. The forecast fails in the exact manner he had just diagnosed on the same page, which is the most useful thing in the chapter: he knew the failure modes, named them, and was caught anyway, in writing, within four paragraphs.

Now the taxonomy, tested against what this book actually found. The straight line caught him, and caught him where he was most concrete. Essay 2 records the scenario in which the car is replaced by an air-cushion vehicle: the impressive machine of his moment, extended forward. Air-cushion vehicles retreated to a single scheduled route, while what displaced the combustion engine kept the wheels, the roads and the shape. Essay 3 records the same shape in a different register — that contact would need investment beyond nuclear-energy research, an extrapolation from the cost curve of big physics that was wrong by two orders of magnitude.

The sudden turn caught him harder, and it is the mode this book keeps recording. He did not see that competence would be grown from written language at scale; essay 4 files that as the omission the whole book turns on, and essay 7 finds him betting explicitly against the formalism that produced it. He did not see that the unit of selection in human heredity would move from the couple to the embryo, which is essay 8's central finding: his machine screens partners, and what arrived screens embryos. Both are Athena from the head of Zeus. Neither was available to be extrapolated from anything on his desk.

The closed schema is the one he escaped, and the ledgers show why it mattered. Essay 6 grades a technology that was promised as either the successor to reality or a fad, and became neither: headsets sold, plateaued, and settled into a modest niche. Essay 3 grades a search that was supposed to end in contact or in proof of absence, and instead simply continues, underfunded, for sixty years. In both cases the two dramatic endings on offer were narrative artifacts, and the sick man went on living. That is his third mode, working exactly as advertised, on evidence he never saw.

His premise held at the object level too. "Man hardly ever knows what he is actually doing" is the recurring finding of essay 4's ledger, where systems optimise the objective they were given and not the one intended, and of essay 7's, where an automated laboratory was mostly right about what it had been wired to check and wrong about what nobody had wired it to check. And the late-arriving price is still arriving: sixty years on, the waste he named is still not permanently disposed of anywhere at scale.

What the ledgers add is a fourth mode he does not name, and it is the one that caught him most often. Twice — the air-cushion car, the polymer information farm of essay 7 — he chose the successor that abolished the most, over the one that changed the least. Evolution is a tinkerer, as his own second chapter argues at length, and technology under pressure substitutes the cheapest component: the motor, not the wheel; the representation, not the substrate. He knew the rule. He applied it to lineages and not to his own forecasts. Call it the impressive-successor error; this book found it three times and named it once.

Where Lem was better than us

He wrote the disclaimer first and then did the work anyway, which is rarer than it sounds. The standard forms are to forecast without admitting the failure rate, or to admit it and use the admission as cover. Lem does neither: he names the mechanisms, grades a named contemporary by them, supplies a test anyone can run — put the believable image of tomorrow in a drawer for a few years — and then commits three hundred pages of predictions that this book has been able to score precisely because they are specific enough to fail. The third mode is his real contribution. Everyone knows that surprises happen and that curves bend; almost nobody notices that the endings we find plausible are the endings that make a story. Sixty years later the same two endings are on offer for every technology anyone is frightened of, and the sick man is still the least discussed possibility and usually the right one.

Where he was worst

He put a date on fusion. It is a small sentence and it is the most instructive thing in the chapter, because it demonstrates that the taxonomy is not prophylactic: you can hold all three failure modes clearly in mind, in writing, on the page, and still extend a curve because the extension is what everybody around you believes. What makes it worse — or better, for a book like this — is that it is the same error he had just convicted the 1955 optimists of, corrected only in its date. He did not repeat their mistake; he repeated its shape.

And his list is short by at least one. The impressive-successor error is not a straight line, because it does not extrapolate an existing curve; it is not a sudden turn, because the thing that actually happens is the boring option that was already available; and it is not a closed schema, because it has no ending in it. It is a failure of taste: the assumption that the future will be reached by whatever currently looks most like the future. His own second chapter contains the antidote — evolution changes what is cheap to change — and he did not turn it on himself.

Still open

  • Whether knowing the modes helps at all. This chapter is the strongest available test and it comes out negative: he named them and was caught by two of them. What would close it: forecasters trained on failure taxonomies beating untrained ones on scored, dated predictions — or a demonstration that the modes are only ever visible in hindsight.
  • Whether the fourth mode is really separate. This essay names it; it may be a special case of the straight line, with the extrapolated quantity being impressiveness rather than performance. What would close it: cases where a forecaster picked the conservative successor and was wrong for that reason.
  • Whether another technology is the only answer. He refused humanist assurances outright (p. 26). What would close it: a dangerous capability durably contained by agreement rather than by counter-technology — or a case where the agreement failed and only the counter-technology held.

Reading

Lem: the whole chapter is seven pages and repays reading before any other. The taxonomy is at pp. 28–29, the sick man at pp. 29–30, and the fusion sentence — the chapter's own best exhibit — at p. 28, four paragraphs after the demolition of Blackett.

2026 side: this essay's evidence is mostly the other essays in this book, whose receipts are in the claims register. The one external source it leans on directly is the Congressional Research Service on ITER, for what happened to the fusion timetable.
