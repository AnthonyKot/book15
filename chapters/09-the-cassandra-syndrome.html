The Cassandra syndrome

The chapter this essay was meant to grade is not in the book. What arrived instead is better: a case against the whole enterprise, made by the man who translated Lem and interviewed him at length. Peter Swirski's answer to the question of whether Lem should be read as a futurologist is No. This book has eighty-five graded rows bearing on that question. Most of them agree with him, in a way he does not argue for — and one of them shows that half his case is wrong.

What the case says

Swirski opens on cloned polo horses, because nobody forecast them. Fifty pedigree mounts copied for half a million dollars each is a future nobody wrote, and no library of forecasts contains "a single word about the cutthroat world of professional polo, the lifestyles of moneyed elites, and a ground-breaking Latin American business enterprise". The lesson he draws is general: that "futurology in general and science fiction in particular are not the best places to look for enlightenment about the future", and that this applies to Lem like anyone else.

His central charge is structural rather than personal, and it is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to overstate. He does not say Summa is unfalsifiable. He writes a deliberately absurd forecast of his own — open-ended wonders on a millennial timeline — and names the trick it exploits: "Heads I win, tails you lose." The charge against Lem is different and narrower: that scientific futurology would require unequivocal predictions on reasonable timelines, and that Lem's hits are byproducts of a different intellectual project.

He is not dismissive of the book. "This is not to deny that Summa is remarkably savvy in some of its deliberations", he writes, crediting Lem with something close to punctuated equilibrium eight years before Eldredge and Gould, and with anticipating "artificial emotion, virtual reality, search engines, nanobots, bionics, and not least the failure of Frank Drake's SETI". He also notes, drily, that this book has a genre: "Some fans even keep score cards, with check marks next to the fulfilled prognoses and question marks next to the perceived flops."

The sharper observation is about the author. Lem graded himself, repeatedly — in The Mystery of the Chinese Room (1996), The Megabit Bomb (1999) and The Blink of an Eye (2000) — and among the successes he claimed were "the adaptive morphology of the human brain, biocomputing, and autonomous search engines", the last of which he called "ariadnology: the science of threads". Swirski's verdict on that habit is unsentimental: "he is not above cherry-picking his fiction for predictive hits."

The conclusion is flat. "Should Lem be read, not to say revered, as a futurologist?" — "in any proper—which is to say scientific—understanding of the term, the answer has to be No." The sentence that follows matters more, though it must be quoted as what it is: Swirski's report of what Lem told him in 1992, in the third person and not in Lem's words — "more than in predicting the future he has always been more interested in mapping the conceptual limits of science and its technological offshoots". The interviews are collected in A Stanislaw Lem Reader, which is not held here, so this book cannot check the wording. Against that, "his specific forecasts—whether in Summa or in his science-driven fiction—could never amount to more than provisional signposts".

And the same verdict arrives independently, six years earlier and from a scientist rather than a critic. Peter Butko, closing his chapter-by-chapter reading of Summa, notes the same habit — "Some read the book as a list of predictions and keep little score cards, where they note the fulfilled prophecies as well as the perceived failures" — and rejects it in one word: "Nevertheless, this is a misunderstanding." Summa, he writes, "is not a prognostication almanac, and its author is not Nostradamus or Jules Verne", because "The aim of Summa was not to predict particular gadgets but rather to explore the conceptual foundations of the two evolutions and their relations to society and civilization" (pp. 98–99). His conclusion about the hits is the sharpest sentence either scholar writes: "Individual predictions that have come true are rather byproducts of Lem's creative thinking." Two people who knew the book and the man, arriving separately at the same objection to the exercise this book performs.

One more witness, from the same editor's earlier volume. Peter Butko, a biophysicist, went through Summa chapter by chapter in 2006, before any English translation existed, and described it as a book Lem "corrected and updated" on reader feedback — the judgement Zylinska cites when she explains why she translated the fourth edition of 1974 rather than the first of 1964.

What this book found

The ledgers agree with Swirski, and they agree with him more precisely than his own argument does. Across eight essays there are eighty-five graded rows. Split by layer — the separation this book insisted on from the start — the object-level rows, which grade devices, dates and quantities, return 11 plain HITs from 50, a rate of 22 percent, and carry every MISS in the book. The problem-level rows, which grade what will be hard, what it will cost and who will decide, return 15 from 35, a rate of 43 percent, and carry no MISSes at all.

That is Swirski's thesis in numbers, and it is stronger evidence than the argument he offers for it. He infers from failed forecasts and an author's interview that Lem's real subject was conceptual limits. The ledgers show it: Lem is roughly twice as reliable about the shape of a difficulty as about the thing that will meet it, and when he fails outright it is always a device, a date or a number.

But half the case does not survive contact with the same evidence. The unfalsifiability charge — heads I win, tails you lose — does not describe Summa's object-level claims, and this book is the demonstration. He said the car would be replaced by an air-cushion vehicle; it was not. He said technical evolution was producing "only narrowly specialized devices"; the direction reversed. Those rows were falsified because they were specific enough to falsify, and the reason there are only four MISSes is not that his claims were slippery but that most of them are not yet decidable, which is a different failing and shows up as thirty-seven OPEN rows. Loose forecasting is the sin Swirski names; the sin this book actually found is a man being specific about the wrong things.

The two scorecards collide once, and the collision is instructive. Lem claimed autonomous search engines as a success and named them with his own coinage, ariadnology. Essay 7 graded the passage that coinage comes from and scored it a MISS — because in the same note he rules out precisely the machine that worked, insisting that a machine librarian "cannot be a device that lacks 'comprehension' and that is based on frequency analysis", and that "algorithmic methods still turn out to be worthless when it comes to making selections from among the 'avalanche of information'" (p. 367). Counting citations was the method he called worthless; it launched that same year as the Science Citation Index and became PageRank. He named the need and got the mechanism backwards, then claimed the need as a hit.

Which is what an author's scorecard does, and why the disagreement matters more than either verdict. Swirski's charge of cherry-picking is not a charge about honesty; it is a charge about what self-assessment is for. This book's totals — four MISSes, eleven half-failures, thirty-seven questions it cannot settle — are not the shape a self-assessment produces, and they are not flattering enough to be worth writing if flattery were the point.

The enterprise
The implied charge in "Some fans even keep score cards" — that grading a thinker like this is a category error Unresolved, and this book cannot judge it about itself. What it can report is that scoring produced a result neither camp states: the questions hold rather better than the answers, and the failure rates are the same OPEN

Where Lem was better than us

He said it first, and about himself. The interview sentence Swirski quotes from 1992 is not a defence produced under pressure; it is the same position the book takes in its own Conclusion, where Lem grounds his warrant not in prophecy but in the claim that the objects he describes already exist in nature and "have passed, rather well, an empirical test that is billions of years old" (p. 351). Sixty years of receipts say he was right about which half of his work would last. And the fact that he then spent three books marking his own homework, and marking it generously, does not damage the point. It illustrates it: the man was better at knowing what kind of thinker he was than at scoring what kind of predictions he had made.

Where he was worst

The self-grading is the worst thing in his record, and it is worse than Swirski's word for it. Cherry-picking implies choosing the good cases. What Lem did with ariadnology was claim as a success a passage whose operative sentence is a refusal — he identified the need for a guide to assembled knowledge, specified that no machine lacking comprehension could provide it, and lived to see machines lacking comprehension provide it. Counting the coinage as a hit while the specification failed is not selection bias; it is scoring the question and ignoring the answer, which is precisely the error this book's two-layer rule exists to prevent, committed by the author against himself.

And a caution that cuts against this essay's own argument. The problem-level rows are not the unscoreable ones: 21 of 35 carry a verdict, a higher settled rate than the object rows, where 23 of 49 stand OPEN. Nor is "no MISSes at problem level" the evidence it looks like, since MISS requires no live path and a problem-level row grades a live dilemma, which routes to RIGHT QUESTION, WRONG ANSWER by construction. Counted properly the layers fail at almost the same rate, 16 percent against 17. What survives is narrower: Lem earns twice as many outright credits on problems as on devices. That supports Swirski and Butko. It does not support the grander version of their claim, that the forecasts were never the point, because the forecasts were specific enough to be graded and eleven of them are HITs.

Still open

  • Whether the author's scorecard is generous throughout. One comparison was possible here, and it went against him. What would close it: the three later collections — The Mystery of the Chinese Room, The Megabit Bomb, The Blink of an Eye — none of which is held here, so every claim in them reaches this book through Swirski's summary
  • Whether the missing chapter would have changed the shape. Chapter 9 is Lem on art and technology, the one domain where 2026 moved most visibly and the one this corpus lacks. Essay 0 notes that no row in the book earned DISSOLVED; that verdict may simply have been waiting in the chapter this edition omits. What would close it: the Polish original or the Russian translation, both of which exist.
  • Whether scoring a thinker like this is worth doing. Swirski implies not, and this book cannot be a fair judge of the question. What would close it: someone using these ledgers to defeat a verdict on the corpus — which is what they are written for, and why every row carries its page.

Reading

Lem: the Conclusion (pp. 351–352), where he states his own warrant, and note 8 to chapter 7 (pp. 365–367), which contains both the coinage he later claimed and the specification that failed.

The case against: Peter Swirski, "The Cassandra Syndrome, or How Not to Be a Prophet", Filozofia i Nauka 10 (2022) — short, and the best argument against this book that exists. Peter Butko, "Summa technologiae – Looking Back and Ahead", in Swirski (ed.), The Art and Science of Stanisław Lem (2006), is the earlier scorecard, written by a biophysicist before the English translation existed.
