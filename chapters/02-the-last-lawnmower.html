Essay 2 · Lem ch. 2, "Two Evolutions" (2013 tr., pp. 31–58)

The last lawnmower

Lem spends five pages describing the life cycle of a species — arriving small and primitive, copying its predecessor, radiating into niches, swelling into giants before the end, leaving relics in the margins — and then tells you he has been describing two things at once. It is the best sustained analogy in the book, and he was rash enough to date it: when the car is replaced by an air-cushion vehicle, he writes, a small petrol lawnmower will be its last surviving descendant. The air-cushion vehicle is gone. The lawnmower is being banned. And the disanalogy that actually matters is in his footnote.

What Lem said

The chapter opens on a question about direction: "Who causes whom? Does technology cause us, or do we cause it?" (p. 32). To answer it he borrows de Latil's ladder of effectors — simple tools, feedback machines, self-programming systems — and places technical evolution near the top, as a system with "freedom of choice with regard to its building material (since technology has at its disposal everything the Universe contains)" (p. 33).

Then the set piece. He describes, in the present tense and without saying of what, the arc of a species: "A new species appears in the world unnoticed. Its appearance seems to come from what already exists, and this borrowing seems to testify to the inventive inertia of the Designer" (p. 33). The first specimens are small and carry "a number of primitive features, as if their birth had been hurried"; they vegetate, barely competing; then an environmental shift lets them expand, and in empty space the lineage "bursts into evolutionary radiation" (p. 33). Decline is gradual and has a signature: "the specimens are getting bigger, as if gigantism was to provide protection against the threat" (p. 34), while lateral branches escape into weaker competition and persist as "the last proof of the primeval abundance and power of a species" (p. 34). Only then does he admit the trick: "I have been talking about two kinds of evolution at the same time: biological and technical" (p. 34).

The examples are specific and checkable. "The first automobile was a spitting image of the coach with a guillotined shaft; the airplane had been 'copied' from the kite (or even the bird), the radio from the already existing telephone" (p. 35). Prototypes are poor: "Ancient mechanical vehicles moved more slowly than horse-driven ones; the first airplane could barely manage to lift itself off the ground" (p. 35), just as "The feathered lizard—the archaeopteryx—did not as much fly as flutter" (p. 35). Then radiation: the car "'gave birth' to the bus, the truck, the bulldozer, the fire engine, the tank" (p. 35). Then a live prediction about an arms race in progress: "just as the predator's strategy affects the strategy of its prey, so the classical airplane defends itself against the helicopter's invasion. It does this by creating a prototype of the winged plane, which—owing to the change of direction of the thrust—is capable of taking off and landing vertically" (p. 35). And the gigantism signature, applied: "The last zeppelins of the 1930s can be easily compared with the atlantosauruses and brontosauruses of the Cretaceous period" (pp. 35–36). Radio, squeezed by television, answers with "a sudden 'radiation of variations'… and to their appearance in new 'ecological niches'" (p. 36).

Then the sentence this essay is named for. "One can also imagine further development of mechanical vehicles, which will make wheel drive obsolete. When the current automobile is eventually replaced by an 'air-cushion vehicle,' it is quite likely that, say, a small combustion-driven lawnmower will be the last descendant of the classical car—still vegetating in a 'lateral branch.' Its design will be a remote reflection of the automobile period, just as certain specimens of lizards from the archipelagos of the Indian Ocean are the last remaining descendants of the large Mesozoic reptiles" (p. 36).

He extends the analogy past shape. Non-functional traits are driven, in biology, by sexual selection and, in technology, by fashion: the car's "chrome-plated embellishments, fantastically shaped cooling pipes, and head and rear lights" answer to "mating colors, crests, unusual outgrowths" (p. 37). There is technological mimicry too — nineteenth-century ironwork pretending to be botanical, and everyday objects given "aerodynamic shapes… masquerading as forms designed in the aerospace industry" (p. 37) — though he notes this version "lacks the profound justification that is evident in its biological counterpart" (p. 37). The claim underneath all of it is stated once, plainly: both are "material processes with almost the same number of degrees of freedom and with similar dynamic laws" (p. 38).

And then, in a footnote, he takes a good deal of it back. Note 1 (p. 356) names a disanalogy the chapter never mentions: "the current increase in knowledge is caused by interspecies hybridization in technology but not in biology. Any randomly chosen areas of human activity are capable of cross-fertilization… yet biological species, having become fixed, cannot reproduce through crossbreeding." Three consequences follow. Technoevolution shows "constant acceleration, which is much higher than that of biological evolution". Its long-range forecasts are hard "owing to the occurrence of sudden turns—which are totally unexpected and unpredictable (one could not predict the emergence of cybernetics before it happened)". And "The number of 'technological species' emerging at any given time is a function of all the existing species, which is something we cannot say about bioevolution." The mid-1960s baseline he wrote from: de Latil's classification; Lévi-Strauss on why technogenesis happened where it did; Apollo under way; the cross-Channel hovercraft entering service; and the first vectored-thrust jet hovering since 1960.

What happened

The car was replaced, and not by what he said. Air-cushion vehicles did not take the roads or anything else: the car-carrying cross-Channel service closed on 1 October 2000, beaten by a tunnel, and large commercial hovercraft effectively vanished, leaving one short scheduled route as a curiosity. Wheel drive survives untouched. What is displacing the internal combustion engine is a different motor under the same wheels: electric cars reached a quarter of global new-car sales in 2025, and more than half in China.

The relic prediction is stranger, because it is being falsified from the wrong end. Lem's small petrol engine is supposed to outlive the car, vegetating in a lateral branch. Instead California required most newly manufactured small off-road engines — petrol engines under 25 horsepower, which is exactly the lawnmower — to be zero-emission from January 2024, while three-quarters of new cars sold worldwide still burn petrol. The relic is being killed before the main line, and not by a competitor. It is being killed by a regulation, which is a selection pressure his analogy has no term for.

Where he was watching an arms race in progress he was right. The aeroplane did answer the helicopter by vectoring its thrust, and the answer stuck: the Harrier entered service in 1969, the V-22 tiltrotor in 2007, the F-35B in 2015. None of them displaced the helicopter, which is what an evolutionist would expect of a lineage defending a niche rather than conquering one. The civil version is still not born: no American electric vertical-take-off aircraft holds a type certificate, with the leading firms at stage four and phase three of the FAA's processes and certification targeted for late 2026 or 2027.

Radio's radiation continued past anything he could have watched, and its current niche war is the one his own example set up. Manufacturers began dropping AM radio from electric cars around 2023, because the high-voltage drivetrain interferes with the signal — the new species making the old one's habitat uninhabitable. What happened next has no biological counterpart at all: the older technology got a lobby. The AM Radio for Every Vehicle Act was reintroduced in January 2025 with more than sixty Senate cosponsors and, by September, 296 in the House, and would compel manufacturers to carry AM reception from model year 2028. Species do not petition the legislature against their competitors.

The pattern he described holds well for machines that occupy a physical niche, and collapses for the machines that now matter most. Software does not go extinct. COBOL, written for mainframes contemporary with this book, is still in production at a scale estimated between roughly 220 and 800 billion lines, carrying a large share of core banking and, by widely quoted figures, most American ATM transactions. That is not a relic in a lateral branch. It is load-bearing, in the centre, sixty years after the environment that produced it disappeared — because in software the cost of replacement is paid by whoever switches, and nothing forces the switch. Biological relics survive in the margins by escaping competition. Software relics survive in the core by making competition unaffordable.

His footnote, meanwhile, has aged better than his chapter. The dominant technology of 2026 is a hybrid of the kind he said biology cannot make: the result that opened the deep-learning era was trained on two consumer graphics cards built for rendering video games. Statistics crossed with entertainment hardware, and neither parent was a plausible ancestor of the offspring. Note 1's other prediction — that this hybridising makes long-range forecasting hard, because of "sudden turns" nobody could have anticipated — is the reason a book like this one has anything to score.

Where Lem was better than us

He treated the analogy as a claim to be tested rather than a figure of speech, and he tested it where it could fail — on dated, specific artifacts, with the mechanism named each time. Sixty years of technology writing has since made "ecosystem", "DNA", "species" and "evolution" into decoration, applied to firms and products with nothing at stake and nothing checkable. Lem's version says what the pattern predicts: that prototypes will be bad, that decline will be preceded by gigantism, that the loser leaves relics in the margins. That is why it can be graded at all, and why the one clean MISS in this ledger is available. He also did the harder thing in his own footnote — named the mechanism that breaks his analogy, said what follows from it, and did not soften the chapter to match. Note 1 is where he tells you that the technology of the future will come from crossings nobody can anticipate, which is both the best forecast in the chapter and a warning about the rest of it.

Where he was worst

He picked the wrong successor, and picked it for a reason worth naming. The air-cushion vehicle was, in 1964, the impressive machine — it did the thing that looked hard, abolishing the wheel, and it was in the newspapers. What actually replaced the combustion engine was a substitution so conservative it kept the wheels, the roads, the shape and the name. His own framework predicts this better than he did: evolution is a tinkerer, it changes what is cheap to change, and a lineage under pressure does not reinvent its body plan. He applied that rule to the archaeopteryx and forgot it for the car.

The deeper limit is that his frame has only one selection pressure. Everything in the chapter happens by competition in a niche. But the small petrol engine is not being outcompeted, it is being prohibited; and AM radio is not being saved by fitness, it is being saved by a bill with 296 cosponsors. Technologies have a legislature, and it can preserve the unfit and kill the fit. Nothing in the two-evolutions model has a place to put that, which is why the chapter's most datable prediction is going wrong in a way the chapter cannot describe.

Still open

  • Whether the classical car leaves a relic at all. His pattern predicts one; regulation is removing the obvious candidate first. What would close it: the combustion engine surviving in some protected niche after passenger cars have gone electric — or its disappearance everywhere at once, which would be an extinction with no biological analogue.
  • Whether software ever goes extinct. Sixty years in, the oldest production code is still in the centre of the financial system. What would close it: a large legacy stack actually retired rather than wrapped — or an argument that "extinction" is simply the wrong category for something that costs nothing to keep running.
  • Whether the hybridising accelerates or exhausts. Note 1 says the number of technological species is a function of all existing species, which predicts acceleration without limit. What would close it: a measured slowdown in the rate of genuinely novel cross-domain technologies — or a demonstration that the combinatorial supply is not the binding constraint.

Reading

Lem: "Similarities" (pp. 33–38) is the set piece and the part worth reading aloud; the lawnmower is at p. 36. Read note 1 (pp. 355–357) immediately afterwards — it is where he names the disanalogy that limits everything he has just argued, and it is the most useful page in the chapter.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. The IEA's Global EV Outlook 2026, for what actually replaced the combustion engine; the California Air Resources Board's small off-road engine rule, for the relic being killed first; and the industry estimates of COBOL still in production, for the case the analogy cannot hold. The rest is in the claims register.
