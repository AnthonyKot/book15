Essay 2 · Lem ch. 2, "Two Evolutions" (2013 tr., pp. 31–58)

The last lawnmower

Lem spends five pages describing the life cycle of a species — arriving small and primitive, copying its predecessor, radiating into niches, swelling into giants before the end, leaving relics in the margins — and then tells you he has been describing two things at once. It is the best sustained analogy in the book. He also, more cautiously than he is usually given credit for, offers a scenario in which the car is replaced by an air-cushion vehicle and a small petrol lawnmower survives as its last descendant. The air-cushion vehicle did not happen. What did happen to the lawnmower is something his model has no term for.

What Lem said

The chapter opens on a question about direction: "Who causes whom? Does technology cause us, or do we cause it?" (p. 32). To answer it he borrows de Latil's ladder of effectors and puts technical evolution near the top, as a system with "freedom of choice with regard to its building material" (p. 33).

Then the set piece. He describes, without saying of what, the arc of a species: "A new species appears in the world unnoticed. Its appearance seems to come from what already exists, and this borrowing seems to testify to the inventive inertia of the Designer" (p. 33). The first specimens are small, carrying "a number of primitive features, as if their birth had been hurried" (p. 34); then an environmental shift lets them expand, and in empty space the lineage "bursts into evolutionary radiation" (p. 34). Decline has a signature: "the specimens are getting bigger, as if gigantism was to provide protection against the threat" (p. 34), while lateral branches persist as "the last proof of the primeval abundance and power of a species" (p. 34). Only then does he admit the trick: "I have been talking about two kinds of evolution at the same time: biological and technical" (p. 34).

The examples are specific. "The first automobile was a spitting image of the coach with a guillotined shaft; the airplane had been 'copied' from the kite (or even the bird), the radio from the already existing telephone" (p. 35). Prototypes are poor, just as "The feathered lizard—the archaeopteryx—did not as much fly as flutter" (p. 35). Then radiation: the car "'gave birth' to the bus, the truck, the bulldozer, the fire engine, the tank" (p. 35). Then a mechanism, stated in the present tense about a machine that already existed: "just as the predator's strategy affects the strategy of its prey, so the classical airplane defends itself against the helicopter's invasion. It does this by creating a prototype of the winged plane, which—owing to the change of direction of the thrust—is capable of taking off and landing vertically" (p. 35). And the gigantism signature: "The last zeppelins of the 1930s can be easily compared with the atlantosauruses and brontosauruses of the Cretaceous period" (pp. 35–36).

Then the passage this essay is named for, with its hedges intact. "One can also imagine further development of mechanical vehicles, which will make wheel drive obsolete. When the current automobile is eventually replaced by an 'air-cushion vehicle,' it is quite likely that, say, a small combustion-driven lawnmower will be the last descendant of the classical car—still vegetating in a 'lateral branch'" (p. 36). It is a scenario, not a dated forecast: he says "one can also imagine", and "it is quite likely that, say".

The claim underneath the analogy is stated once: both are "material processes with almost the same number of degrees of freedom and with similar dynamic laws" (p. 38). He is not claiming that technology runs itself. The same page says technical evolution "is not, for the time being at least, people-free", and that "It is here perhaps that the biggest difference lies: bioevolution is beyond all doubt an amoral process, which is something we cannot say about technical evolution" (p. 38).

Two further claims belong to this essay. On universality: "the universality of our technologies is minimal. Technical evolution has so far been moving in a kind of reverse direction to biological evolution in that it has only been creating narrowly specialized devices" (p. 43), and the way out is named — "The potential for universality seems to lie in a further development of the theory of self-organizing systems—which would be capable of adaptive self-programming" (pp. 43–44). And in "Several Naïve Questions" he refuses the consolation that creative work is safe: "Apparently it will only be possible to automatize noncreative mental labor. But where is the evidence? Let us be clear about it: there is none, and what is more, there can be none" (p. 56). The displacement "will not happen in the narrow sense of laborers and technicians being replaced by programmers of digital machines because future generations and future types of these machines will not need programmers anymore" (p. 57). And he separates capability from motive: "'cleverer' does not yet mean 'rebellious'" (p. 57).

Note 6 (pp. 355–357), which hangs not from the parallel but from the end of the theory-versus-empiricism discussion at p. 47, qualifies the analogy where the chapter's own "Differences" section does not. "The difficulty is that the current increase in knowledge is caused by interspecies hybridization in technology but not in biology. Any randomly chosen areas of human activity are capable of cross-fertilization… yet biological species, having become fixed, cannot reproduce through crossbreeding." Hence technoevolution shows "constant acceleration", its forecasts are hard "owing to the occurrence of sudden turns—which are totally unexpected and unpredictable", and "The number of 'technological species' emerging at any given time is a function of all the existing species, which is something we cannot say about bioevolution."

The baseline needs a caveat this book applies elsewhere: Zylinska translates the altered fourth edition of 1974, so the text's state of the art is mid-1970s, not 1964. That matters here. The Harrier entered service in 1969 and the car-carrying cross-Channel hovercraft in 1968 — both before this edition — so neither can be treated as something the text predicted.

What happened

The scenario's successor never arrived. Air-cushion vehicles did not take the roads, and at sea they retreated: the car-carrying cross-Channel service closed in October 2000, beaten by a tunnel, and large commercial hovercraft effectively disappeared, leaving one short scheduled route. Wheel drive is untouched. What is displacing the internal combustion engine is a different motor under the same wheels: electric cars reached a quarter of global new-car sales in 2025, more than half in China, so at least three-quarters of new cars still burn fuel.

The relic half is being decided by something outside the model. Lem's small petrol engine is supposed to outlive the car in a lateral branch. Instead California required most newly manufactured small off-road engines — petrol engines at or below 25 horsepower, which covers lawnmowers — to be zero-emission from January 2024, while existing machines stay legal to own and resell. The relic is not being outcompeted; the niche is being closed to new entrants by regulation. Lem is clear that technical evolution is "not… people-free" and, unlike bioevolution, not amoral (p. 38) — but the chapter models human agency as choice about direction, not as a body that can prohibit a fit technology and protect an unfit one.

The same pressure runs the other way too. Manufacturers began dropping AM radio from electric cars around 2023, because the high-voltage drivetrain interferes with reception — habitat loss, and the reverse of the diversification under television pressure Lem describes at p. 36. What followed has no biological counterpart: the older technology acquired a lobby. The AM Radio for Every Vehicle Act, reintroduced in January 2025 and advanced by the Senate Commerce Committee within a week, would compel manufacturers to carry AM reception from model year 2028. Species do not petition a legislature against their competitors.

Where the analogy earns its keep is the mechanism, not the artifacts. The vertically launching aeroplane already existed when he wrote — the P.1127 hovered in 1960, the Harrier entered service in 1969 — so the forecast is not that it would appear. The testable part is his account of why: a lineage defending a niche rather than conquering one. The V-22 entered service in 2007 and the F-35B in 2015, and neither displaced the helicopter, which is what niche defence predicts and displacement does not. The civil version, which he did not describe, remains uncertified.

The pattern holds where machines compete for a physical niche, and something else happens where they do not. Software relics do not retreat to the margins; they stay in the middle. COBOL, written for mainframes contemporary with this book, is still load-bearing in core banking, on industry estimates that put it somewhere between roughly 220 and 800 billion lines in production — figures that are trade estimates rather than audited counts. The direction is not in doubt even if the number is: this is not a lateral branch vegetating in a peripheral niche, it is the centre of the financial system sixty years after the environment that produced it disappeared. In software the cost of replacement falls on whoever switches, and nothing forces the switch, so a relic can occupy the core indefinitely.

His p. 43 claim is a description rather than a forecast — technology "has so far been" specialising, which was true when he wrote it — and the interesting part is the route he named next — universality via "self-organizing systems… capable of adaptive self-programming" — and what arrived is self-programmed in a loose sense, learned rather than written, but by gradient descent on large corpora rather than the theory he meant. His refusal at p. 56 to reserve creative work for people has held: what automated first was writing, drawing and coding. Programmers have not been dispensed with.

Note 6's mechanism — cross-fertilisation between technical domains, which biology cannot perform — is visible in the origin of that technology. The founding result of deep learning was trained on two consumer graphics cards built for rendering video games. That is one instance of cross-domain recombination, offered as this essay's illustration rather than as proof of the note's larger claims about acceleration or unpredictability, which it cannot carry alone.

Where Lem was better than us

He treated the analogy as a claim with consequences rather than a figure of speech, and he named mechanisms — niche defence, radiation into empty space, relics in lateral branches — instead of gesturing at resemblance. Sixty years of technology writing has since made "ecosystem", "DNA" and "evolution" into decoration, applied to firms and products with nothing at stake and nothing checkable. His version can be graded, which is why there is a MISS in this ledger at all. He was also better than most of his successors on two specific points. He refused, in 1966, the consolation that creative work is safe from automation, and refused it on the right ground: not that machines would soon write poems, but that nobody had any evidence for the exemption and no such evidence could exist. And in the same paragraph he kept capability apart from motive — "'cleverer' does not yet mean 'rebellious'" (p. 57) — which is precisely the inference most current argument about intelligent machines runs together.

Where he was worst

He picked the wrong successor for the car, and picked it for a reason worth naming. The air-cushion vehicle was, when he wrote, the impressive machine: it did the thing that looked hard, abolishing the wheel. What actually displaced the combustion engine was a substitution so conservative it kept the wheels, the roads, the shape and the name. His own framework handles that better than his example did — evolution changes what is cheap to change, and a lineage under pressure does not redesign its body plan — but the framework is in the chapter and the hovercraft is in the prediction.

The larger gap is a pressure the model does not contain. Lem is explicit that technical evolution is not people-free and not amoral, so this is not a chapter that thinks technology runs itself. But the agency he models is directional: societies choosing what to build and how fast. What decided the fate of the small petrol engine was neither competition nor choice of direction but a rule about what may be manufactured, and what is keeping AM radio alive in cars is a bill. A regulator can close a niche to a technology that is winning it and compel the carriage of one that is losing. Between "competition" and "human choice" there is a third thing — enforceable prohibition — and the two-evolutions frame has no slot for it, which is why the chapter's most concrete illustration is being settled in a way the chapter cannot describe.

Still open

  • Whether the classical car leaves a relic at all. His pattern predicts one; regulation is closing the obvious candidate to new manufacture first, in one jurisdiction. What would close it: the combustion engine surviving in some protected niche after passenger cars have gone electric — or its disappearance everywhere at once, which would be an extinction with no biological analogue.
  • Whether software ever goes extinct. Sixty years in, code from this book's decade is still in the centre of the financial system. What would close it: a large legacy stack actually retired rather than wrapped — or an argument that extinction is the wrong category for something that costs nothing to keep running.
  • Whether cleverer becomes rebellious. He separated the two and said the intermediaries would be "in some sense 'cleverer' than their human designer" without that implying revolt (p. 57). What would close it: a capable system that pursues ends its designers did not give it and will not accept — or a demonstration that capability and motive are separable by construction.

Reading

Lem: "Similarities" (pp. 33–38) is the set piece and the part worth reading aloud; the lawnmower scenario is at p. 36, with its hedges. Then p. 43 for the specialisation claim and "Several Naïve Questions" (pp. 55–58) for the automation of creative work. Note 6 (pp. 355–357) hangs from p. 47, not from the parallel, and qualifies it. Bear in mind that this translation follows the altered fourth edition of 1974.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. The IEA's Global EV Outlook 2026, for what actually replaced the combustion engine; the California Air Resources Board's small off-road engine rule, for the niche closed by regulation rather than competition; and the industry estimates of COBOL still in production, for where the analogy stops.
