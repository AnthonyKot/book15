Essay 0 · Translator's introduction and Conclusion (2013 tr., pp. 11–23, 351–352)

How to grade a prophet

In 1991 Lem said he would gladly publish "a new critical edition of Summa, much enlarged to include—in the margins, footnotes, or in some other way—my commentary on the things I wrote in the 1960s" (p. 20). He never made it. This is that edition, with the marginal commentary supplied by the sixty years instead of by the author. It is written last, because a method chapter written first is a promise, and the only interesting thing about a method is what it cost to keep and what it turned out to show.

What Lem said

Twice in this book Lem grades his own procedure, and both times he is more careful than his reputation. The Conclusion answers the obvious charge — that he has "shifted responsibility for the future Gnosis of our species onto the dead shoulders of nonexistent machines" (p. 351), reviving Lull's Ars Magna, which Swift had already mocked. His defence is a constraint rather than a boast: "This book differs from pure fantasy in that it tries to provide a relatively firm basis for its hypotheses, while also considering that which exists to be the most solid thing. This is why it constantly refers to Nature, since this is where the 'autonomous apsychic predictors' and the 'intelligent device' are located" (p. 351). The book's warrant is that its objects already exist somewhere — in chromosomes, in brains — and "have passed, rather well, an empirical test that is billions of years old" (p. 351).

The first chapter had already supplied the failure modes, and essay 1 grades them. What matters here is a quieter admission Zylinska records: "I do not see myself as subjectively infallible" (p. 18), and her judgement that Lem "is arguably more interested in signaling certain problems and posing questions about them than in offering any determined visions of either the present or the future" (p. 18). That sentence is an objection to this whole book. If he was posing problems rather than predicting, then scoring him against 2026 is a category error dressed as rigour.

Two facts about the text govern everything else. First, the editions. Summa appeared in Polish in 1964 and was reissued that year, with altered editions in 1967 and 1974; this translation follows the fourth edition of 1974, chosen as "the most mature and up-to-date", and Butko's description is quoted approvingly: "Summa is essentially a work in progress: Lem corrected mistakes and updated his thinking, based in part on feedback from readers who are often scientists and experts in their respective fields" (p. 20). The book being graded is therefore a revised book. Second, Lem graded himself, and graded generously. His later essays "Twenty Years Later" and "Thirty Years Later" update the examples "with their later scientific equivalents (e.g., synthetic biology and virtual reality devices such as Eye Phone and Data Glove)", and Zylinska is explicit about their purpose: "Those latter efforts were aimed at demonstrating how many of the inventions and discoveries Lem did manage to predict correctly in Summa" (p. 21). Neither essay is in this English edition.

One passage shows his method better than anything he says about method, and no ledger can score it. At pp. 347–350 he rejects extrasensory perception, and not on the statistics: "If telepathic phenomena were real, if they formed a unique channel of information transfer… then biological evolution would no doubt have used such phenomena" (p. 349), and "no matter what environment we look into, we can see the great potential usefulness of telepathic phenomena, coupled with their complete absence" (p. 350). Then note 10 explains why the experimental literature looked encouraging anyway. Imagine five hundred psychologists testing telepathy; chance leaves a hundred with striking results, half of whom get nothing on retesting and stop, and "Ultimately, there will be five or six people left on the battlefield—those who have obtained positive results several times in a row. They are already 'lost'" (p. 371). That is the file-drawer problem, described in a footnote fifteen years before it was named. It is not a forecast and has no row in any ledger. It is the clearest thing in the book about how he thought.

He was not deceived about the odds. "Nothing ages as fast as the future" (p. 21), he said. And of the book itself, to Bereś: "from my discursive books Summa Technologiae is the only one I am happy with… It has survived and is still very much alive" (p. 20).

What happened

Eight essays grade Lem, in eighty-four rows. The pooled distribution is unflattering to the enterprise — 37 OPEN, 26 HIT, 11 RIGHT QUESTION WRONG ANSWER, 4 EARLY, 3 MISS, 3 RIGHT ANSWER WRONG REASON — and the modal verdict, by a distance, is that the question is not settled. Those OPEN rows are not where one would guess: 23 of the 37 sit at object level, on devices and dates. But the pooled number is the wrong number, and the rule that made it wrong is the one this book kept most strictly: object level and problem level are scored in separate rows. Split them and the finding appears.

At object level — devices, dates, quantities — there are 49 rows and 11 plain HITs, a rate of 22 percent, with all three MISSes and all four EARLYs. At problem level — the shape of a difficulty, what a thing will cost, who will decide — there are 35 rows and 15 HITs, 43 percent, and no MISSes at all. Lem is roughly twice as likely to be plainly right about what will be hard as about what will be built, which is the result the two-layer method exists to produce.

But the striking half of that — no MISSes at problem level — is partly an artefact of the vocabulary, and this chapter would be doing what it accuses others of if it failed to say so. MISS is defined as a forecast that did not happen and has no live path; a problem-level row grades a live dilemma, so a wrong answer there routes to RIGHT QUESTION, WRONG ANSWER by construction, and the category is close to unreachable at that layer. Count negatives properly, MISS and RIGHT QUESTION WRONG ANSWER together, and the layers are nearly identical: 8 of 49 object rows against 6 of 35 problem rows, 16 percent against 17. The finding is narrower than it first looked and still worth having. Lem earns twice as many outright credits on problems as on things, and fails at about the same rate on both.

That vindicates the objection this book was built against. Zylinska reads Lem as "more interested in signaling certain problems and posing questions about them than in offering any determined visions" (p. 18), which sounds like a defence against scoring. The ledgers say she is describing his strength, though on credits rather than on failures: he is right about problems twice as often, and wrong about them just as often.

The vocabulary had seven words and one was never used. Not a single row — not one of the ninety-three — earned DISSOLVED, the verdict for a question that stopped being a question. That is a fact about this corpus rather than a law: eight chapters were graded and the ninth, on art, is missing, so the sample is the part of Lem that was already about engineering. Within it, nothing he framed became meaningless.

The negatives were expensive, and they were not all the same mistake. Three MISSes survive; eight were written, defended and withdrawn under review. Three were transposition — testing the essay's own analogy as though it were Lem's claim: essay 8 tried one-generation embryo selection against a claim about partner selection over millennia, essay 7 did the equivalent twice. Two were category errors about what kind of sentence he had written: essay 1 graded a consensus he was reporting as a forecast, then debited him for a completeness he expressly disclaimed. One graded an order before either side had happened (essay 5); one compared unlike quantities (essay 3). The common thread is not a technique but a pressure: a rule requiring a negative, and an essay without one.

The endnotes decided four essays. Note 8 to chapter 7 supplied the second selector its chapter lacks and reversed the essay's thesis; note 3 to chapter 3 states the hypothesis Lem actually chose, which his chapter never says; note 6 to chapter 2 names the disanalogy that limits its central parallel. Note 2 to chapter 8 sharpened the argument rather than reversing it. In every case the material sat in small type at the back, and in every case it was found late. The rule now standing — read the chapter's endnotes before drafting — was written after the first and did not prevent the third.

Two failures of this book's own machinery are worth recording. Two misquotes reached the published site, both introduced while cutting for length rather than while drafting, and both passed the gate because the gate only checks quotations that have been registered. And the claims gate is weaker than it looks: it verifies that a marker has a row, not that the row supports the sentence — a fact demonstrated in this chapter's first draft, which receipted a claim about review rounds with a row about genetic carrier screening. Neither gate can tell a true sentence from a well-formed one.

Where Lem was better than us

He asked for this book and nobody made it. The 1991 remark about a critical edition "much enlarged to include… my commentary on the things I wrote in the 1960s" is the correct instinct about what a forecast is for: not to be right, but to be checkable later by someone with the answers. What he actually produced instead — "Twenty Years Later" and "Thirty Years Later" — were, on his translator's account, aimed at showing how much he had got right. That is the ordinary thing to do and it is why authors should not grade themselves. But he knew the better procedure and named it, which is more than most forecasters manage, and he wrote the first chapter of this book for us: the three failure modes in chapter 1 are a scoring rubric its author supplied against himself.

The other thing he did better is the one this book has been least able to match. He was specific enough to fail. Two pages of chapter 8 name the exact menu a genotype screen would offer; chapter 7 gives the sieve a chemistry; chapter 3 names the cost of the search in units. Nothing in the current futures literature is this exposed. It is why there are only three MISSes here and why they were so hard to find — the specificity that makes him gradeable is the same specificity that makes him worth grading.

Where he was worst

Not a chapter, a habit, and essay 1 named it: he reached for the successor that abolished the most. The air-cushion vehicle rather than a different motor in the same wheels; polymer carriers rather than a formalism that runs; partner selection rather than a decision moved past conception. His own second chapter contains the antidote — evolution changes what is cheap to change — and he did not turn it on his own forecasts. Where he was wrong, he was usually wrong in the direction of the more impressive machine.

Where this book was worst is easier to say, because a panel said it repeatedly. It wanted results. Every manufactured negative came from the same impulse: a rule requiring a negative, an essay without one, and a transposition close enough to Lem's words to look like a reading of them. The rule stays, because a book of only credits would be worthless. But the rule needs its companion, which took four rounds to learn and is now written down: if no honest negative exists, say that the chapter is different and invite attack on that conclusion. Essay 5 ended up there and is stronger for it.

Still open

  • Chapter 9 and the afterwords. "Art and Technology" is absent from this translation, and so are "Twenty Years Later" and "Thirty Years Later" — the two texts in which Lem scores himself, and therefore the only direct check on whether this book's verdicts are harsher than the author's for good reasons. What would close it: the Polish or Russian text of chapter 9, and either afterword.
  • Whether the verdicts survive their own method. Three MISSes stand; eight were withdrawn under review, and no essay went unblocked. What would close it: a reader who defeats a surviving MISS on the corpus — the ledger rows are written to make that possible, and the invitation is meant.
  • Whether naming a failure mode helps anyone. Essay 1 finds that Lem named three mistakes and this book's ledgers catch him inside two of them; it also finds that the discipline which did become rigorous trains for calibration, not for failure taxonomies. This book, meanwhile, wrote its own characteristic error into its rules and committed it anyway, in essay after essay. What would close it: evidence that studying failure taxonomies improves scored forecasts, as training for calibration demonstrably does.

Reading

Lem: the Conclusion (pp. 351–352) is his own defence of the book's method and takes five minutes; chapter 1 (pp. 24–30) is the failure taxonomy, and essay 1 grades it. Zylinska's introduction is worth reading for pp. 20–21, on the four editions and on what Lem's later self-assessments were for.

2026 side: this chapter's evidence is the book itself — the ledger rows, the review reports in drafts/reviews/, and the claims register. The verdict counts were computed from the published essays rather than typed, and they moved twice while this chapter was being written, because two essays were rebuilt under review after it first quoted them. They are current as of 18 August 2026.
