Essay 7 · Lem ch. 7, "The Creation of Worlds" (2013 tr., pp. 237–293)

The two sieves

Halfway through this chapter Lem stops forecasting and files a design, one of the most detailed in the book: a machine to produce scientific theories without a scientist — not by symbol-pushing, which he thinks cannot represent what needs representing, but by growing them in vats of synthetic polymers. The vats do not exist, and the automation arrived from inside the formalism he was escaping. The more interesting failure is elsewhere. In his own endnote he describes a second selector the chapter never mentions — wired not to the phenomenon but to whoever wants the answer — and stakes the proposal on it. That is where his aim is worst and his diagnosis best, in the same three pages.

What Lem said

The chapter opens on a civilisation with a good engine and no rudder: science wins its game with Nature every time and is dragged along by the winnings, so that "instead of developing a strategy, it ends up just practicing tactics" (p. 237). The remedy is an automated science, and he begins where automation stops. "Many cyberneticists are currently working on the possibility of automating hypothesis formation" (p. 239); the wall they hit is combinatorial, six variables needing "thirty thousand such machines… over a period of several dozen years" (p. 239) — though only for variables "at least for us, i.e., as long as we cannot see any link between them" (p. 239), an escape clause that does a great deal of work.

Then the specification: "We are to invent a device that will gather information, generalize it in the same way the scientist does, and present the results of this inquiry to experts. The device collects facts, generalizes them, and checks the validity of this generalization by applying it to a new set of facts" (p. 243). And immediately: "Our device thus produces theories" (p. 243) — theories in the strict sense he then spells out, structural equivalents of a phenomenon whose transformations track its trajectory.

The design follows. A fertilised egg computes an organism without writing anything down: "Could we not take up 'information farming,' cross-breed bits of information with one another, and initiate their 'growth' so that we eventually obtain a 'mature organism' in the form of scientific theory?" (pp. 251–252). The carriers are "large molecules of synthetic polymers" (p. 263) whose development is isodynamic with the phenomenon studied — not a picture of it, a process running alongside it. They pass through sieves, each "connected (e.g., by means of automatic manipulators, perceptrons, etc.) to a real phenomenon" (p. 264) whose actual state is encoded as molecular specks and crashed against the carriers. Those that predicted it survive; repeat, and you climb from "a theory of the examined class" toward "a theory of the class of classes" (p. 265). The law installed in place of biological fitness is the crux: "that which expresses the environment most adequately survives" (p. 259).

He states Ashby's position fairly first — "new theories count for nothing… while the only things that really matter are elimination and selection" (pp. 260–261) — and does not dispute it; what he rejects is the source of the variants: "Ashby thinks that one should start from the greatest diversity and then 'filter' it gradually. We, in turn, want to start from significant yet not enormous diversity" (p. 261). He is explicit that formal generation and empirical selection already work as a pair: "Mathematicians are thus a generator of diversity, whereas empiricists are the selector postulated by Ashby" (p. 261). His bet against formalism is a bet about representation: write fetal development out in chemical notation and the formulae run "in the realm of quadrillions. There would not be enough surface across all the oceans and the entire mainland for it" (p. 251). Hence the choice between mathematics "encoded in the binary elements of large electron machines" and "the kind that actualizes the fertilized egg without any formalism whatsoever" (p. 251), and hence the rule at p. 263: "We need to represent processes with some other processes, not with formal signs."

And then there is note 8 — three pages of small type at the back of the book (pp. 365–367), and it takes most of the design apart. A farm, he writes, "can actually exacerbate, rather than diminish, a crisis resulting from the excess of information" (p. 365), because it "only offers alternative ways of proceeding; it reveals what can be achieved but not whether we should proceed at all", and "Such a decision-making process must not be mechanized" (p. 365). Worse, one running too far ahead of the civilisation that started it produces information that "would not only be useless but also incomprehensible," at which point "the criteria for eliminating irrelevant information would disappear" and it becomes "a 'bomb'—if not a megabyte then at least a gigabyte one" (p. 366). The condition on the apparatus is exact: "As long as the selectors at the information farm are under the active influence of intelligent beings, they are capable of selecting information effectively" (p. 366). So there are two selectors in Lem: one wired to the phenomenon, deciding what is true, one wired to the addressee, deciding what is worth saying.

He then applies that second sieve to 1964's own literature and gets his sharpest dated forecasts. Libraries grow exponentially and abstracts cannot keep up, so methodology "should give way to 'ariadnology'—which would stand for a guide to the labyrinth of the already assembled knowledge" (p. 367). He rules out the obvious machine: "A machine librarian, sending the right kinds of information to the right kinds of people, cannot be a device that lacks 'comprehension' and that is based on frequency analysis." He knows the attempts exist — "counting how often they get mentioned by experts in the bibliographies of their publications… are already under way" — and dismisses them: "algorithmic methods still turn out to be worthless when it comes to making selections from among the 'avalanche of information'" (p. 367). The correction, he expects, comes from publishing, hedged: "It seems that the crisis of information distribution will lead to the strengthening of publication criteria in the future so that their original selection prevents the flooding of the professional market with insignificant works" (p. 367). And the priority is unambiguous: "the automatization of cognitive processes, at least within the field of librarianship and publishing, is a more urgent task" (p. 367).

The chapter's second half turns to language, where two forecasts must be kept apart. On p. 276 he predicts, without conditions, "a still remote state when it will be easier to control phenomena than to understand all the conditions that enable such control". The sentence after it is conditional: "If we manage to crossbreed algorithms produced by finite machines with nonalgorithmic streams of information emerging from phenomena supervised by self-organizing gradients, causal language will stop being comprehending, while science will be producing predictions stripped of 'explanations'" (p. 277). On p. 280, stronger again: natural language, using "tools that have become autonomized on an informational level through being located in extracerebral material systems," will develop a causal language "of the next kind" crossing "the threshold of 'understanding'" — causal in his strict sense, the language of heredity, where "word literally becomes flesh" (p. 273). The last two sections he labels speculation and this book takes them at that value: "The Engineering of Transcendence" builds an afterlife by machine and argues itself out of it — "to prove belief is to destroy it" — closing socially: "It is not a renaissance of transcendence that is needed but rather a renaissance of society" (p. 286). "Cosmogonic Engineering" designs worlds whose inhabitants must not find the exit (pp. 289–290); essay 6 scored the inhabitants' side. The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain, Amarel's 1962 paper cited in his own note 1, and lab automation that measured — the AutoAnalyzer of 1957, the "Robot Chemist" of 1959 — but formed no hypotheses.

What happened

Parts of the p. 243 device got built, well short of the sentence. In 2009 a robot called Adam proposed functions for orphan genes in yeast, designed the experiments, ran them on its own liquid handlers and confirmed them. In 2023 Coscientist, a language-model system, planned and executed palladium-catalysed cross-couplings on a robotic platform. In 2026 Nature published a human-authored report on an end-to-end system; three of its papers entered blind review at a machine-learning workshop, one cleared the bar and was withdrawn under a protocol agreed in advance. Gather, generalise, test, hand to experts: the list is ticked. But none of it produces theories in the sense he defines two sentences later — Adam found gene functions, not a theory of gene function.

None of it is his farm. There are no polymer carriers, no isodynamic development, no vats. Every system here represents processes with formal signs — executable ones, which is the move he did not consider: not a written score but a formalism that runs. FunSearch is the cleanest case: a language model proposes programs, an evaluator runs and scores them, survivors are fed back as prompts. That is not his sieve — its evaluator computes a formal criterion exactly, it is not "a real phenomenon" (p. 264) reporting its own state — but it is not off his map either. It is the pairing he named at p. 261, mathematicians generating and empiricists selecting, with both halves mechanised.

Where a selector is really coupled to the phenomenon, his rule holds: Adam's hypotheses lived or died on yeast growth curves, Coscientist's routes in the flask. Where a proxy stands between, results move. Berkeley's autonomous lab reported 41 novel compounds from 58 targets in seventeen days unattended, phase identification automated from diffraction data; crystallographers reanalysed all of it and argued that no new materials had been made. In January 2026 the authors corrected the paper: 36 compounds from 57 targets, "novel" dropped from the title, novelty redefined as new to the prediction platform rather than to science, 36 of 40 claimed successes confirmed on manual re-analysis. That is not the failure the critique described, and it is more useful to Lem than a failure would be: the selector was right about what it was wired to — did the synthesis work — and wrong about what nobody had wired it to check. The same shape appears in a graph network's 380,000 predicted-stable crystals, in a database that by 2025 had duplicates and calls for correction.

On the second sieve the record splits, and not as the chapter expects. The deluge arrived — from ordinary human production, with no farm anywhere near it. Publication doubles every nine years; more than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023, a record, mostly from compromised review and paper mills; and a 2025 analysis found the brokerages selling authorship in mass-produced papers large, resilient and growing faster than the legitimate literature — his "works that are only produced to obtain an academic degree or to satisfy someone's ambition", industrialised. He expected that pressure to strengthen selection at the gate; in the venues these papers reach, it did not. Meanwhile the addressing technology he ruled out was built first and worked: Garfield's Science Citation Index appeared in 1964, the year of this book, indexing 613 journals by counting citations — the "frequency analysis" he called worthless. In 1998 PageRank extended citation analysis to hyperlinks, citing Garfield. What was never built is the machine he specified: one "sending the right kinds of information to the right kinds of people" (p. 367).

His p. 276 forecast, by contrast, describes 2026. AlphaFold2 predicts protein structure by learning correlations across databases rather than simulating the folding physics, and took a Nobel for it; a learned weather model beat the leading operational ensemble on 97.2% of its targets without solving that ensemble's equations. Control ran ahead of understanding, and the results were accepted as knowledge. The p. 277 consequent is another matter: conditional on a crossbreed that has not occurred, and science has not stopped explaining. Nor has p. 280 happened — nothing built in extracerebral material systems has crossed a threshold of understanding. The nearest approach is to his first causal language: generative models that write protein sequences which, synthesised, fold and bind as designed. There the word does literally become flesh, but that is engineering in causal language, not a successor to it.

Where Lem was better than us

He required two couplings where the design of automated discovery usually gets one. The sieve must be wired to the phenomenon — every case in this essay where automation held up obeyed that rule, and every contested one substituted a proxy — and the apparatus must stay wired to an addressee: "all information assumes the existence of an addressee. There is no 'information in general'" (p. 243). Note 8 states the failure condition precisely: "As long as the selectors at the information farm are under the active influence of intelligent beings, they are capable of selecting information effectively" (p. 366). He drew the practical conclusion too — a farm "will be particularly useful if it is presented with specific tasks, although outlined in a general manner" (p. 366) — the configuration that works in the systems surveyed here. And he ranked the jobs: addressing is "a more urgent task" (p. 367) than production.

Where he was worst

The single wrong call is the machine librarian. He looked straight at citation counting in 1964, called algorithmic selection worthless, and it became the standard apparatus for finding scientific work — then, extended to hyperlinks, the ranking method behind web search. The error has a shape worth naming: he assumed selection on relevance required comprehension. The cheap statistical proxy he refused was good enough, and he missed it because he was looking for a sieve wired to a phenomenon, while the citation graph is wired to an addressee — to what other scientists had already found worth using.

The substrate bet was the expensive part. "We need to represent processes with some other processes, not with formal signs" (p. 263) is the load-bearing premise of the design, and it forced a carrier that had to be invented before anything could be tried — he half-admits it: "we do not know how to build an 'information carrier' or where to look for the building material" (p. 266). The premise was too strong. He set the choice up as written formalism against developing chemistry, and what worked was neither: a formalism that executes. His quadrillions-of-formulae argument is about writing things down, and stops applying once the signs are made to run.

And neither half asks what happens when running the phenomenon is itself the expensive step, so that a cheap proxy replaces an expensive world by economics rather than carelessness — that the cheap-to-evaluate problems and the important ones may be different sets is this essay's framing, not his.

Still open

  • Whether anything grows rather than searches. An egg is not a search. What would close it: a discovery system whose internal state develops with the phenomenon instead of proposing and testing against it — or a demonstration that the distinction dissolves once you look at how learning updates.
  • Whether the librarian he actually specified gets built. Counting found what is central; it never sent the right work to the right person. What would close it: a system that measurably gets cross-disciplinary work to the researcher who needed it — his "ariadnology" (p. 367).
  • Whether choosing what to work on can be mechanised. He says flatly it must not be (p. 365), and no system has been handed that choice. What would close it: a research programme whose agenda is set by machine and defended as such — or an argument that the prohibition is incoherent.

Reading

Lem: "Information Farming" (pp. 239–266) is the design; p. 243 the specification, p. 251 the bet against formalism, pp. 263–265 the apparatus. Read note 8 (pp. 365–367) first: it is where he grades his own proposal, and it is better than the proposal. "Linguistic Engineering" (pp. 267–281) holds two separate forecasts, pp. 276–277 and p. 280.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. Szymanski et al. on the A-Lab (Nature, 2023), the Leeman et al. reanalysis (PRX Energy, 2024) and the author correction (Nature, Jan 2026) are the proxy-selector story in one chain; Garfield, "'Science Citation Index' — A New Dimension in Indexing" (Science, 1964) is the thing Lem was looking at when he called it worthless; Richardson et al. (PNAS, 2025) is the deluge. The rest are in the claims register.
