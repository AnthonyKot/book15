Essay 7 · Lem ch. 7, "The Creation of Worlds" (2013 tr., pp. 237–293)

The farm and the sieve

Halfway through this chapter Lem does something he does nowhere else in the book: he stops forecasting and files a design. The machine is supposed to produce scientific theories without a scientist — not by searching, which he considers a dead end, but by growing them, generation after generation, in vats of synthetic polymers. Sixty years later the machine does not exist and will not. But he had a rival in the room, W. Ross Ashby, whose plan he explicitly rejected — and Ashby's plan is what got built. What is left over from the argument is the part neither of them thought was hard.

What Lem said

The chapter opens on a civilisation with a good engine and no rudder: "Civilization lacks knowledge that would allow it to choose a path knowingly from the many possible ones, instead of drifting in random tides of discoveries" (p. 237). Science wins its game with Nature every time and is dragged along by the winnings — "instead of developing a strategy, it ends up just practicing tactics" (p. 237). The remedy has to be an automated science, and he begins where the automation stops: "Many cyberneticists are currently working on the possibility of automating hypothesis formation" (p. 239), and the wall they hit is combinatorial. With five variables a machine doing a million operations a second gets through the values in two hours; "with six variables, the same process requires thirty thousand such machines, working at maximum speed over a period of several dozen years" (p. 239). His conclusion carries a qualification that decides how it should be graded: if these are random variables "at least for us, i.e., as long as we cannot see any link between them," then "no system whatsoever, no matter if artificial or natural, will be able to deal with more than several dozen variables, even if it was as large as the Metagalaxy" (p. 239).

So enumeration is out, and modelling has limits too — a model that keeps all the variables stops being a model. "A machine that would copy, with utmost exactitude, every material phenomenon would be a universal plagiarizer. Its full consideration of all the variables of phenomena would in a sense automatically cut it off from any creative activity" (p. 241). Between those walls he places his gardener: "A scientist wants an algorithm, whereas a technologist is more like a gardener who plants a tree, picks apples, and is not bothered about 'how the apple tree did it'. A scientist considers such a narrow, utilitarian, and pragmatic approach a sin against the Laws of Full Knowledge. It seems that those attitudes will change in the future" (p. 241).

The design follows. Since a fertilised egg computes an organism without writing anything down, "Could we not take up 'information farming,' cross-breed bits of information with one another, and initiate their 'growth' so that we eventually obtain a 'mature organism' in the form of scientific theory?" (pp. 251–252). The apparatus is specified in unusual detail for this book. The carriers are "large molecules of synthetic polymers" (p. 263) that grow by taking up "food" from their medium, and whose development is isodynamic with the phenomenon being studied — not a picture of it, a process running alongside it. They pass through containers he calls sieves; a sieve is "connected (e.g., by means of automatic manipulators, perceptrons, etc.) to a real phenomenon" (p. 264), encodes the phenomenon's actual state as molecular specks, and crashes the two populations together like antigens and antibodies. Carriers whose development predicted the real state survive; the rest are gone. Do it again on the survivors and you get "a theory of the examined class" (p. 265); do it on the theories and you climb toward "a theory of the class of classes" (p. 265). The law he installs in place of biological fitness is the crux of the whole thing: "that which expresses the environment most adequately survives" (p. 259).

Before the design he does something a forecaster rarely does: he states his rival's position fairly and declines it. Ashby's intelligence amplifier is built on a "generator of diversity" — any theorem whatever is already being transmitted, in Morse, by air molecules bumping into each other several hundred thousand times a second in every cubic centimetre, so "new theories count for nothing because they can be produced in great numbers by means of 'noise' processes… while the only things that really matter are elimination and selection" (p. 261). Lem's answer: "Our position on this differs from his. I have referred to Ashby here to demonstrate that it is possible to travel toward a similar goal… via different routes. Ashby thinks that one should start from the greatest diversity and then 'filter' it gradually. We, in turn, want to start from significant yet not enormous diversity—a kind of diversity that a self-organizing process (e.g., fertilized egg) represents—and make sure this process 'develops' into a scientific theory" (p. 261). He notes in passing that the generator already exists in a domestic form: "Mathematicians are thus a generator of diversity, whereas empiricists are the selector postulated by Ashby" (p. 261).

The chapter's second half turns to language, and it is worth reading against what this book's essay 4 records as Lem's big omission. Here he does treat language as a substrate: he sorts languages on a scale from causal — the language of heredity, where "word literally becomes flesh" (p. 273) — to comprehending, which is natural speech; he observes that natural-language utterances are "programs that are full of gaps" (p. 269) that the receiver must fill; and he forecasts a third language, built "in extracerebral material systems," which "almost by accident, will cross the threshold of 'understanding' or 'comprehensibility'" (p. 280) and by which "science will be producing predictions stripped of 'explanations' rather than explanations themselves" (p. 277). The last two sections are speculation and he says so: "The Engineering of Transcendence" builds an afterlife by machine and then argues itself out of it — "to prove belief is to destroy it" (p. 285), and the conclusion is social, not technical: "It is not a renaissance of transcendence that is needed but rather a renaissance of society" (p. 286). "Cosmogonic Engineering" designs worlds whose inhabitants must not be able to find the exit (pp. 289–290); this book scored the inhabitants' side of that in essay 6, and the constructor's side is still where Lem left it. The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain, perceptrons that could not yet read, machines doing a million operations a second, and no automated experiment anywhere.

What happened

The automation arrived, roughly where he pointed it. In 2009 a robot called Adam — hypothesis generator, liquid handler and all — proposed and tested functions for orphan genes in yeast and was described as the first machine to discover new scientific knowledge autonomously. In 2023 a language-model system called Coscientist planned and ran palladium-catalysed cross-couplings on a robotic platform. In the same year an autonomous laboratory in Berkeley reported synthesising 41 novel compounds from 58 targets in seventeen days of unattended operation, with the targets chosen by machine learning over a database of predicted structures. And the loop closed on the paper itself: a system that generates ideas, writes the code, runs the experiments, writes the manuscript and reviews it produced a paper that passed peer review at a machine-learning workshop, reported in Nature in 2026. That is Lem's gardener, planting and picking without asking how the tree did it.

None of it is his farm. There are no polymer carriers, no isodynamic development, no vats. What was built is Ashby's machine, the one Lem said he was departing from: a generator of candidates and a selector that kills most of them. FunSearch is the purest case — a language model proposes programs, an automatic evaluator scores them, the survivors are fed back as prompts, and the loop produced an improved construction for the cap set problem and better bin-packing heuristics. Generate and filter, exactly as described in 1964, with the noise source upgraded from air molecules to a model of human text. The materials pipeline is the same shape at industrial scale: a graph network predicted some 2.2 million candidate crystals, of which about 380,000 were called stable and handed on for synthesis.

And here the essay's argument starts, because the part Lem treated as machinery — the sieve — is the part that broke. His sieve is coupled to the phenomenon: it encodes what the world actually did, and what fails to predict it dies. Where 2026's selectors are like that, the results hold up; FunSearch's evaluator runs the program and counts, so its discoveries are not opinions. Where the selector is a proxy, they do not. The Berkeley lab's 41 compounds were reanalysed by outside crystallographers who concluded that no new materials had been made, and the dispute is about how the automated analysis read its own diffraction data. The 380,000 stable crystals turned out to include duplicates, and by late 2025 the database had a correction problem and a citation count in the thousands. The workshop that accepted the machine-written paper had an acceptance rate near 70 percent. Meanwhile the human selector that all of this ultimately reports to is in visible trouble: more than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023, a record, most of them traceable to compromised peer review and paper mills.

His combinatorial wall, on the other hand, stood — with the qualification he attached to it. Nobody deals with a few dozen genuinely independent variables, then or now; the systems that handle millions of them do it precisely because the variables are not independent, which is the escape clause he wrote into his own sentence. What he did not foresee is how much of the world is covered by that clause. And the language forecast has aged better than the farm: a competence grown outside a brain, from written language, now acts on the world through tools — it is the generator inside FunSearch and the author of the workshop paper — while remaining, exactly as he predicted of a causal language, something whose outputs we can use and cannot follow.

Where Lem was better than us

He knew the output had to be received by someone, and said so in a sentence the automated-science literature could use on its title page: "all information assumes the existence of an addressee. There is no 'information in general'" (p. 243). His whole farm is built backwards from that — the point is not to bury the reader deeper but to hand over what was extracted: in the future, he writes, scientists "will obtain its very essence and build their theories not from facts but from other theories" (p. 243). Compare the shape of 2026's automation, which is uniformly optimised for production: papers, candidate structures, hypotheses, all generated at rates no community of readers can absorb, with the reading left to the same overloaded reviewers who were already failing. He also insisted the sieve be wired to the phenomenon rather than to a judgement about it — the antigen–antibody image is doing real work — and every case in this essay where automated discovery held up is a case where somebody obeyed that rule. And he was right about the bargain the scientist would have to accept. The gardener who does not ask how the apple tree did it is now the normal working scientist in three or four fields, and Lem wrote the sentence in a tone of neither triumph nor alarm sixty years early: "It seems that those attitudes will change in the future" (p. 241).

Where he was worst

He backed the wrong horse in an argument he set up himself, and he backed it for a reason worth naming. Ashby's proposal — generate wildly, then filter — struck him as wasteful and, in the air-molecule form Ashby gave it, silly. His alternative was to keep the diversity small and let structure develop, because that is what biology does. Biology was the wrong analogy: what makes an egg work is that it is not searching, and what makes discovery work in 2026 is that the search is cheap and the test is cheaper. The chemistry made it worse. Committing the design to molecular carriers meant committing to a substrate that has to be invented before anything can be tried, and he half-admits it — "we do not know how to build an 'information carrier' or where to look for the building material" (p. 266) — while a digital version of the same selection loop was already possible in 1964 and is what people actually built. And there is a smaller failure of imagination inside the successful part: his sieve selects for prediction against a phenomenon, which is right, but he never asks what happens when the phenomenon is expensive, slow or contested and the machine gets scored against a proxy instead. That is the failure mode the whole 2026 record turns on, and it is not in his chapter.

Still open

  • Whether anything grows rather than searches. Lem's distinction is real: an egg is not a search. What would close it: a discovery system whose internal state develops with the phenomenon instead of proposing and testing against it — or a demonstration that the distinction dissolves once you look closely at how learning updates.
  • Whether the theory of a class of classes is reachable. His farm climbs from phenomena to theories to theories of theories (p. 265). Nothing in 2026 climbs. What would close it: a system that produces a generalisation over existing theories that working scientists adopt — or an argument that this is what human theorising is, and cannot be automated separately.
  • Cosmogonic engineering, on the constructor's side. Essay 6 graded whether an inhabitant could tell. The constructor's problem — build a world and hide the exit (pp. 289–290) — is not a live path in 2026 in any form; the nearest thing is generative environments built to train agents, which nobody has to hide anything from.

Reading

Lem: "Information Farming" (pp. 239–266) is the design; pp. 260–261 is where he states Ashby's case and declines it, and pp. 263–265 is the apparatus. "Linguistic Engineering" (pp. 267–281) is the chapter's other half and the best answer in the book to the charge that Lem missed language. The last two sections are speculation he labels as such.

2026 side: King et al. on Adam (Science, 2009); Boiko et al., "Autonomous chemical research with large language models" (Nature, 2023); Szymanski et al. on the A-Lab (Nature, 2023) and the Leeman et al. reanalysis (2024); Romera-Paredes et al. on FunSearch (Nature, 2023); Merchant et al. on GNoME (Nature, 2023); Lu et al., "Towards end-to-end automation of AI research" (Nature, 2026); Van Noorden and Singh Chawla on the 2023 retraction record (Nature, 2023).
