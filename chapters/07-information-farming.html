Essay 7 · Lem ch. 7, "The Creation of Worlds" (2013 tr., pp. 237–293)

The two sieves

Halfway through this chapter Lem stops forecasting and files a design, one of the most detailed in the book. The machine is to produce scientific theories without a scientist: not by symbol-pushing, which he thinks ends in an information crisis, but by growing them in vats of synthetic polymers. The vats do not exist, and the automation arrived from inside the formalism he was escaping. The crisis arrived too — but the chapter has the wrong cause for it. The right one is in his own endnote, which describes a second sieve the chapter never mentions and warns that a farm lacking it "would most certainly lead to a paper deluge" making "any further research impossible" (p. 367). 2026 built the first sieve and failed the second.

What Lem said

The chapter opens on a civilisation with a good engine and no rudder: science wins its game with Nature every time and is dragged along by the winnings, so that "instead of developing a strategy, it ends up just practicing tactics" (p. 237). The remedy is an automated science, and he begins where the automation stops. "Many cyberneticists are currently working on the possibility of automating hypothesis formation" (p. 239); the wall they hit is combinatorial: five variables take a machine two hours, six need "thirty thousand such machines… over a period of several dozen years" (p. 239). His conclusion carries the qualification that decides how it is graded — if these are variables "at least for us, i.e., as long as we cannot see any link between them," then "no system whatsoever, no matter if artificial or natural, will be able to deal with more than several dozen variables, even if it was as large as the Metagalaxy" (p. 239).

Then the specification, before any chemistry: "We are to invent a device that will gather information, generalize it in the same way the scientist does, and present the results of this inquiry to experts. The device collects facts, generalizes them, and checks the validity of this generalization by applying it to a new set of facts" (p. 243). And immediately: "Our device thus produces theories" (p. 243) — theories in the strict sense he then spells out, structural equivalents of a phenomenon whose transformations track its trajectory.

The design follows. A fertilised egg computes an organism without writing anything down: "Could we not take up 'information farming,' cross-breed bits of information with one another, and initiate their 'growth' so that we eventually obtain a 'mature organism' in the form of scientific theory?" (pp. 251–252). The carriers are "large molecules of synthetic polymers" (p. 263) whose development is isodynamic with the phenomenon studied — not a picture of it, a process running alongside it. They pass through sieves, each "connected (e.g., by means of automatic manipulators, perceptrons, etc.) to a real phenomenon" (p. 264), which encodes the phenomenon's actual state as molecular specks and crashes the two populations together like antigens and antibodies. Carriers that predicted the real state survive. Repeat and you get "a theory of the examined class"; repeat on the theories and you climb toward "a theory of the class of classes" (p. 265). The law installed in place of biological fitness is the crux: "that which expresses the environment most adequately survives" (p. 259).

He states Ashby's position fairly first — "new theories count for nothing… while the only things that really matter are elimination and selection" (pp. 260–261) — and does not dispute it; what he rejects is the source of the variants: "Ashby thinks that one should start from the greatest diversity and then 'filter' it gradually. We, in turn, want to start from significant yet not enormous diversity" (p. 261). The bet underneath was stated earlier. Which mathematics do we mean — "the kind that uses the formal language of equations and inequalities… encoded in the binary elements of large electron machines, or the kind that actualizes the fertilized egg without any formalism whatsoever? If we are to stay with the first one, we will be facing an information crisis" (p. 251).

And then there is note 8 — three pages of small type at the back of the book (pp. 365–367), and it takes most of the design apart. A farm, he writes, "can actually exacerbate, rather than diminish, a crisis resulting from the excess of information" (p. 365), because it "only offers alternative ways of proceeding; it reveals what can be achieved but not whether we should proceed at all" (p. 365). Worse, one running too far ahead of the civilisation that started it produces information that "would not only be useless but also incomprehensible," at which point "the criteria for eliminating irrelevant information would disappear" and it "would be immediately transformed into a 'bomb'—if not a megabyte then at least a gigabyte one" (p. 366). The condition on the whole apparatus is exact: "As long as the selectors at the information farm are under the active influence of intelligent beings, they are capable of selecting information effectively" (p. 366). Left alone, "it will quickly produce the excess of information in which it will drown, together with its creators," because "it is a 'mindless' farm" (p. 366). So there are two selectors in Lem, not one: one wired to the phenomenon, deciding what is true, and one wired to the addressee, deciding what is worth saying.

He then applies the second sieve to 1964's own literature, and gets the chapter's sharpest dated forecasts. Libraries grow exponentially and abstracts cannot keep up, so methodology "should give way to 'ariadnology'—which would stand for a guide to the labyrinth of the already assembled knowledge" (p. 367). Then he rules out the obvious machine: "A machine librarian, sending the right kinds of information to the right kinds of people, cannot be a device that lacks 'comprehension' and that is based on frequency analysis." He knows the attempts exist — "counting how often they get mentioned by experts in the bibliographies of their publications… are already under way" — and dismisses them: "algorithmic methods still turn out to be worthless when it comes to making selections from among the 'avalanche of information'" (p. 367). He expects the correction from publishing instead: "the crisis of information distribution will lead to the strengthening of publication criteria in the future so that their original selection prevents the flooding of the professional market with insignificant works" — works "produced to obtain an academic degree or to satisfy someone's ambition" (p. 367). And the priority is unambiguous: "the automatization of cognitive processes, at least within the field of librarianship and publishing, is a more urgent task" (p. 367).

The chapter's second half turns to language, where two forecasts must be kept apart. On pp. 276–277, a bifurcation: "a still remote state when it will be easier to control phenomena than to understand all the conditions that enable such control" (p. 276), after which "causal language will stop being comprehending, while science will be producing predictions stripped of 'explanations' rather than explanations themselves," the working unit becoming the "cognitive man–machine tandem" (p. 277). On p. 280, something stronger: natural language, using "tools that have become autonomized on an informational level through being located in extracerebral material systems," will develop a causal language "of the next kind" which "almost by accident, will cross the threshold of 'understanding'". Causal has his strict sense — the language of heredity, where "word literally becomes flesh" (p. 273). The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain; Amarel's 1962 paper on automatic theory formation, cited in his own note 1; perceptrons that could not "read texts" (p. 153); and lab automation that measured — the AutoAnalyzer of 1957, the "Robot Chemist" of 1959 — but formed no hypotheses.

What happened

Parts of the p. 243 device got built, well short of the sentence. In 2009 a robot called Adam proposed functions for orphan genes in yeast, designed the experiments, ran them on its own liquid handlers and confirmed them — a closed gather–hypothesise–test loop. In 2023 Coscientist, a language-model system, planned and executed palladium-catalysed cross-couplings on a robotic platform. In 2026 Nature published a human-authored report on an end-to-end system that generates ideas, writes code, runs experiments and drafts manuscripts; three of its papers entered blind review at a machine-learning workshop, one cleared the bar and was withdrawn under a protocol agreed in advance. Gather, generalise, test, hand to experts: that list is ticked. But none of it produces theories in the sense he defines two sentences later. Adam found gene functions, not a theory of gene function.

None of it is his farm. There are no polymer carriers, no isodynamic development, no vats. What was built is symbol-pushing on the binary elements of large electron machines — the branch he expected to end in an information crisis. FunSearch is the cleanest example: a language model proposes programs, an evaluator runs and scores them, survivors are fed back as prompts, and the loop produced an improved cap-set construction and better bin-packing heuristics. It is easy to mistake that for Lem's sieve, and it is not one: its evaluator computes a formal criterion exactly; it is not "a real phenomenon" (p. 264) reporting its own state. Generate-and-select inside the formalism is the road neither man drew.

Where a selector is really coupled to the phenomenon, his rule holds: Adam's hypotheses lived or died on yeast growth curves, Coscientist's routes in the flask. Where a proxy stands between, results move. Berkeley's autonomous lab reported 41 novel compounds from 58 targets in seventeen days unattended, phase identification automated from diffraction data. Crystallographers reanalysed all of it and argued that automated Rietveld analysis was not yet reliable and no new materials had been made. In January 2026 the authors corrected the paper: 36 compounds from 57 targets, "novel" dropped from the title, novelty redefined as new to the prediction platform rather than to science, and 36 of 40 claimed successes confirmed on manual re-analysis, four inconclusive. That is not the failure the critique described, and it is more useful to Lem than a failure would be: the selector was mostly right about what it was wired to — did the synthesis work — and wrong about what nobody had wired it to check. The same shape appears in a graph network's 380,000 predicted-stable crystals, deposited in a database that by late 2025 had duplicates and calls for correction.

The second sieve is where the record is worst. Note 8's deluge arrived on schedule. Publication doubles every nine years; more than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023, a record, mostly from compromised review and paper mills; and a 2025 analysis found the brokerages selling authorship in mass-produced papers large, resilient and growing faster than the legitimate literature — his "works that are only produced to obtain an academic degree or to satisfy someone's ambition", industrialised. He expected that pressure to tighten the gate. It loosened. What arrived on time was the addressing technology, by the method he ruled out: Garfield's Science Citation Index appeared in 1964, the year of this book, indexing 613 journals by counting citations — the "frequency analysis" he called worthless. It ran scientific search for fifty years, and in 1998 the same idea, generalised to hyperlinks and crediting Garfield, became PageRank.

His pp. 276–277 forecast, by contrast, describes 2026. AlphaFold2 predicts protein structure by learning correlations across databases rather than simulating the folding physics, and took a Nobel for it; a learned weather model beat the leading operational ensemble on 97.2% of its targets without solving the equations that ensemble is built from. Predictions stripped of explanations, accepted as knowledge, from the tandem he named. The p. 280 forecast has not happened: nothing built in extracerebral material systems has crossed a threshold of understanding. The nearest approach is to his first causal language — generative models that write protein sequences which, synthesised, fold and bind as designed. There the word does literally become flesh, but that is engineering in causal language, not a successor to it.

Where Lem was better than us

He required two couplings where most 2026 discussion of automated discovery has one. The sieve must be wired to the phenomenon — every case here where automation held up obeyed that rule — and the farm must stay wired to an addressee: "all information assumes the existence of an addressee. There is no 'information in general'" (p. 243). Note 8 states the failure condition better than anything written since: "As long as the selectors at the information farm are under the active influence of intelligent beings, they are capable of selecting information effectively" (p. 366). He drew the practical conclusion too — a farm "will be particularly useful if it is presented with specific tasks, although outlined in a general manner" (p. 366), which is the configuration that works in 2026 and the one open-ended proposals skip. And he ranked the jobs correctly: addressing before production. Sixty years of building the second first is the record this essay scores.

Where he was worst

The single wrong call is the machine librarian. He looked straight at citation counting in 1964, called it worthless, and it became the most durable information technology in his own subject area — running scientific retrieval for half a century, then, generalised to the web, the ranking method that made everything findable. The error has a shape: he assumed selection on relevance required comprehension, which is also his objection to Ashby, and the cheap statistical proxy he refused is the one that worked. He missed it because he was looking for a sieve wired to a phenomenon, and the citation graph is wired to an addressee — to what other scientists had already found worth using.

The chemistry was the expensive part: molecular carriers meant a substrate that had to be invented before anything could be tried, and he half-admits it — "we do not know how to build an 'information carrier' or where to look for the building material" (p. 266). And the chapter blames the crisis on the wrong thing. Formal representation did not bury research under paper; production without addressing did — which the endnote says, three pages of small type from a chapter that never revises itself in its light. The two halves of Lem are arguing, and the footnote wins.

Neither half asks what happens when running the phenomenon is itself the expensive step — when a cheap proxy is substituted for an expensive world not by carelessness but by economics. That the cheap-to-evaluate problems and the important ones might be different sets is this essay's framing, not his.

Still open

  • Whether anything grows rather than searches. An egg is not a search. What would close it: a discovery system whose internal state develops with the phenomenon instead of proposing and testing against it — or a demonstration that the distinction dissolves once you look at how learning updates.
  • Whether the climb to theories of theories happens. What would close it: a machine-made generalisation over existing theories that working scientists adopt.
  • Whether an addressing sieve can be built now that comprehension is cheap. His objection to frequency analysis was wrong for fifty years and may come true from the other side. What would close it: a system that measurably gets the right work to the right researcher across disciplines — his "ariadnology" (p. 367) — rather than raising output again.
  • Cosmogonic engineering, on the constructor's side. Essay 6 graded whether an inhabitant could tell; the constructor's problem is to hide the exit (pp. 289–290). No project surveyed here attempts it; the nearest relatives are generative environments built to train agents, which hide nothing from what lives in them by design.
