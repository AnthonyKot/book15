Essay 7 · Lem ch. 7, "The Creation of Worlds" (2013 tr., pp. 237–293)

What the sieve is wired to

Halfway through this chapter Lem does something he does nowhere else in the book: he stops forecasting and files a design. The machine is to produce scientific theories without a scientist — not by symbol-pushing, which he thinks leads to an information crisis, but by growing them, generation after generation, in vats of synthetic polymers. Sixty years later the vats do not exist and the automation arrived from inside the formalism he was trying to escape. What survives the wreck of his design is the rule he put at its centre, which he and Ashby both understood and 2026 keeps breaking: the sieve has to be wired to the phenomenon.

What Lem said

The chapter opens on a civilisation with a good engine and no rudder: "Civilization lacks knowledge that would allow it to choose a path knowingly from the many possible ones, instead of drifting in random tides of discoveries" (p. 237). Science wins its game with Nature every time and is dragged along by the winnings — "instead of developing a strategy, it ends up just practicing tactics" (p. 237). The remedy has to be an automated science, and he begins where the automation stops: "Many cyberneticists are currently working on the possibility of automating hypothesis formation" (p. 239), and the wall they hit is combinatorial. With five variables a machine doing a million operations a second gets through the values in two hours; "with six variables, the same process requires thirty thousand such machines, working at maximum speed over a period of several dozen years" (p. 239). His conclusion carries a qualification that decides how it should be graded: if these are random variables "at least for us, i.e., as long as we cannot see any link between them," then "no system whatsoever, no matter if artificial or natural, will be able to deal with more than several dozen variables, even if it was as large as the Metagalaxy" (p. 239).

So enumeration is out, and modelling has limits too — a model that keeps all the variables stops being a model. "A machine that would copy, with utmost exactitude, every material phenomenon would be a universal plagiarizer. Its full consideration of all the variables of phenomena would in a sense automatically cut it off from any creative activity" (p. 241). Between those walls he places his gardener: "A scientist wants an algorithm, whereas a technologist is more like a gardener who plants a tree, picks apples, and is not bothered about 'how the apple tree did it'. A scientist considers such a narrow, utilitarian, and pragmatic approach a sin against the Laws of Full Knowledge. It seems that those attitudes will change in the future" (p. 241).

The design follows. Since a fertilised egg computes an organism without writing anything down, "Could we not take up 'information farming,' cross-breed bits of information with one another, and initiate their 'growth' so that we eventually obtain a 'mature organism' in the form of scientific theory?" (pp. 251–252). The apparatus is specified in unusual detail for this book. The carriers are "large molecules of synthetic polymers" (p. 263) that grow by taking up "food" from their medium, and whose development is isodynamic with the phenomenon being studied — not a picture of it, a process running alongside it. They pass through containers he calls sieves; a sieve is "connected (e.g., by means of automatic manipulators, perceptrons, etc.) to a real phenomenon" (p. 264), encodes the phenomenon's actual state as molecular specks, and crashes the two populations together like antigens and antibodies. Carriers whose development predicted the real state survive; the rest are gone. Do it again on the survivors and you get "a theory of the examined class" (p. 265); do it on the theories and you climb toward "a theory of the class of classes" (p. 265). The law he installs in place of biological fitness is the crux of the whole thing: "that which expresses the environment most adequately survives" (p. 259).

Before the design he states his rival's position fairly and then narrows the disagreement. Ashby's intelligence amplifier runs on a "generator of diversity" — any theorem whatever is already being transmitted, in Morse, by air molecules bumping into each other several hundred thousand times a second in every cubic centimetre — so "new theories count for nothing because they can be produced in great numbers by means of 'noise' processes… while the only things that really matter are elimination and selection" (pp. 260–261). Lem does not dispute that. His farm also generates variants and kills most of them; what he rejects is the source: "Ashby thinks that one should start from the greatest diversity and then 'filter' it gradually. We, in turn, want to start from significant yet not enormous diversity—a kind of diversity that a self-organizing process (e.g., fertilized egg) represents—and make sure this process 'develops' into a scientific theory" (p. 261). Both men, in other words, put selection at the centre. And he notes that the generator already exists domestically: "Mathematicians are thus a generator of diversity, whereas empiricists are the selector postulated by Ashby" (p. 261).

The bet underneath the design is stated a few pages earlier, and it is the one that can be graded hardest. Which mathematics do we mean, he asks — "the kind that uses the formal language of equations and inequalities, written on a piece of paper or encoded in the binary elements of large electron machines, or the kind that actualizes the fertilized egg without any formalism whatsoever? If we are to stay with the first one, we will be facing an information crisis. However, if we initiate the second one for our purposes, then matters can take a very different course" (p. 251). The vats are his way out of the formalism, and the design he wants is one that arrives at the answer "without math" (p. 251), the way Nature solves the many-body problem "through the sheer activity of those bodies" (p. 251). He is also explicit about what the device must do, in plain terms, before any of the chemistry: "We are to invent a device that will gather information, generalize it in the same way the scientist does, and present the results of this inquiry to experts. The device collects facts, generalizes them, and checks the validity of this generalization by applying it to a new set of facts" (p. 243).

The chapter's second half turns to language, and it is worth reading against what this book's essay 4 records as Lem's big omission. Here he does treat language as a substrate: he sorts languages on a scale from causal — the language of heredity, where "word literally becomes flesh" (p. 273) — to comprehending, which is natural speech; he observes that natural-language utterances are "programs that are full of gaps" (p. 269) that the receiver must fill; and he forecasts a third language, built "in extracerebral material systems," which "almost by accident, will cross the threshold of 'understanding' or 'comprehensibility'" (p. 280) and by which "science will be producing predictions stripped of 'explanations' rather than explanations themselves" (p. 277). The last two sections are speculation and he says so: "The Engineering of Transcendence" builds an afterlife by machine and then argues itself out of it — "to prove belief is to destroy it" (p. 286), and the conclusion is social, not technical: "It is not a renaissance of transcendence that is needed but rather a renaissance of society" (p. 286). "Cosmogonic Engineering" designs worlds whose inhabitants must not be able to find the exit (pp. 289–290); this book scored the inhabitants' side of that in essay 6, and the constructor's side is still where Lem left it. The 1964 baseline: Ashby's Design for a Brain; Amarel's 1962 paper on automatic theory formation, which Lem's own note cites; perceptrons that, in his words, could not "read texts" (p. 153); and laboratory automation that already existed for measurement — a continuous-flow AutoAnalyzer from 1957, a discrete-sample "Robot Chemist" marketed in 1959 — but nothing that formed or tested a hypothesis.

What happened

The device on p. 243 got built, at a smaller scope than the sentence implies. In 2009 a robot called Adam — hypothesis generator, liquid handler and all — proposed and tested functions for orphan genes in yeast and was described as the first machine to discover new scientific knowledge autonomously. In 2023 a language-model system called Coscientist planned and ran palladium-catalysed cross-couplings on a robotic platform. In the same year an autonomous laboratory in Berkeley reported synthesising 41 novel compounds from 58 targets in seventeen days of unattended operation, the targets chosen by machine learning over a database of predicted structures. And the loop closed on the paper itself: a system that generates ideas, writes code, runs experiments, writes the manuscript and reviews it produced a paper that passed peer review at a machine-learning workshop, reported in Nature in 2026. Gather, generalise, test on new facts, hand the result to experts — that is his list.

None of it is his farm, and the reason cuts against the bet he placed. There are no polymer carriers, no isodynamic development, no vats. What was built is symbol-pushing on the binary elements of large electron machines, which is precisely the branch he expected to end in an information crisis. The generators are trained models proposing structured candidates; the selectors are programs that score them. FunSearch is the clearest case — a language model proposes programs, an automatic evaluator runs and scores them, survivors are fed back as prompts, and the loop produced an improved construction for the cap set problem and better bin-packing heuristics. This is not Ashby's air molecules either: the diversity is structured, seeded from a program skeleton, and evolved. It is the middle of the road neither man drew — formal, digital, generate-and-score — and Lem's escape route was never taken.

What did survive is his rule about the sieve, and it survives by being violated. His selector is coupled to the phenomenon: it encodes what the world actually did, and what fails to predict it dies — "that which expresses the environment most adequately survives" (p. 259). Where 2026's selectors are like that, the results hold. FunSearch's evaluator runs the program and counts, so its constructions are not opinions. Where the selector is a proxy, they wobble. The Berkeley lab's 41 compounds were reanalysed by outside crystallographers who concluded that no new materials had been made, the dispute turning on how the automated analysis read its own diffraction data. A graph network's 2.2 million predicted crystals, 380,000 of them called stable, went into a public database that by late 2025 had a duplicate problem and public calls for correction. The workshop that accepted the machine-written paper had an acceptance rate near 70 percent, and the system's own authors present it as a step, not a result. Meanwhile the human selector all of this ultimately reports to is in visible trouble: more than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023, a record, most traceable to compromised peer review and paper mills.

His combinatorial wall stood, with the qualification he attached to it. Nobody enumerates a few dozen genuinely independent variables, then or now; the systems that handle millions do it because the variables are not independent, which is the escape clause he wrote into his own sentence — and the clause turns out to describe the world. Measured directly, standard image datasets sit on manifolds whose intrinsic dimension is two or three orders of magnitude below their pixel count, and the lower it is, the better networks learn them. And his language forecast has aged better than his chemistry: a competence grown outside a brain, from written language, now acts on the world through tools — it is the generator inside the search loop and the author of the workshop paper — while remaining, as he said of a causal language, something whose outputs we can use and whose workings its builders do not claim to follow.

Where Lem was better than us

He insisted that the sieve be wired to the phenomenon rather than to a judgement about it — the antigen–antibody image is doing real work — and every case in this essay where automated discovery held up is a case where something obeyed that rule, while every contested case is one where a proxy stood in for the world. That is a design rule, not a prophecy, and it is the most useful sentence in the chapter. He also knew the output had to be received by someone: "all information assumes the existence of an addressee. There is no 'information in general'" (p. 243). His farm is built backwards from that — the point is not to bury the reader deeper but to hand over what was extracted, so that scientists "will obtain its very essence and build their theories not from facts but from other theories" (p. 243). The automation that exists is aimed almost entirely at production, with the reading left to reviewers who were already failing. And he was right about the bargain the scientist would have to accept, in a tone of neither triumph nor alarm: "It seems that those attitudes will change in the future" (p. 241).

Where he was worst

He bet against the formalism, and the formalism won. The whole apparatus of vats and carriers exists to escape "the binary elements of large electron machines", because he thought that road ended in an information crisis; what actually automated science is that road, scaled. The chemistry was the expensive part of the mistake: committing to molecular carriers meant committing to a substrate that had to be invented before anything could be tried, and he half-admits it — "we do not know how to build an 'information carrier' or where to look for the building material" (p. 266) — while a digital version of the same generate-and-select loop was buildable in 1964 and is what people built.

The subtler failure is inside the part he got right. His sieve selects on what the phenomenon actually did, which is correct and is the rule the 2026 record keeps confirming by breaking it. But he never asks what happens when the phenomenon is slow, costly or contested — when running the world is exactly what you cannot afford — and that is precisely where automation is wanted and where a proxy gets substituted. A crystal that has to be synthesised and characterised is not a bin-packing heuristic you can score in a millisecond. Nothing in his chapter anticipates that the cheap-to-evaluate problems and the important ones might be different sets, which is the shape of the whole automated-discovery record so far.

Still open

  • Whether anything grows rather than searches. His distinction is real: an egg is not a search. What would close it: a discovery system whose internal state develops with the phenomenon instead of proposing and testing against it — or a demonstration that the distinction dissolves once you look closely at how learning updates.
  • Whether the climb from theories to theories of theories happens. What would close it: a machine-made generalisation over existing theories that working scientists adopt — or an argument that this is what human theorising is, and cannot be separated out.
  • Cosmogonic engineering, on the constructor's side. Essay 6 graded whether an inhabitant could tell; the constructor's problem is to build a world and hide the exit (pp. 289–290). Nothing in 2026 attempts it; the nearest relatives are generative environments built to train agents, from which nothing is being hidden. What would close it: an attempt.

Reading

Lem: "Information Farming" (pp. 239–266) is the design; p. 251 is the bet against formalism, pp. 260–261 the exchange with Ashby, pp. 263–265 the apparatus. "Linguistic Engineering" (pp. 267–281) is the chapter's other half and the best answer in the book to the charge that Lem missed language. The last two sections are speculation he labels as such.

2026 side: King et al. on Adam (Science, 2009); Boiko et al., "Autonomous chemical research with large language models" (Nature, 2023); Szymanski et al. on the A-Lab (Nature, 2023) and the Leeman et al. reanalysis (2024); Romera-Paredes et al. on FunSearch (Nature, 2023); Merchant et al. on GNoME (Nature, 2023); Lu et al., "Towards end-to-end automation of AI research" (Nature, 2026); Van Noorden and Singh Chawla on the 2023 retraction record (Nature, 2023).
