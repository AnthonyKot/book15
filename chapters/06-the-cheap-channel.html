Essay 6 · Lem ch. 6, "Phantomology" (2013 tr., pp. 197–236)

The cheap channel

You have probably worn a headset once and taken it off after twenty minutes. You have almost certainly talked to a machine this year that talked back well enough that you forgot, for a sentence or two, what it was. Neither is the device Lem describes in this chapter — a machine that feeds your nerves a world you cannot tell from the real one — and he would be the first to say so. But the question he built the machine to ask, can you tell?, is being answered in 2026 on a channel he priced as the expensive one, and the second half of his chapter, which almost nobody reads, is the argument about copying and rewriting people that philosophy reached twenty years later.

What Lem said

The chapter opens with a question, not a gadget: "how do we create realities for the intelligent beings that exist in them, realities that are absolutely indistinguishable from the standard reality but that are subject to different laws?" (p. 197). The modest version — an artificial reality "very similar to the actual one yet that cannot be distinguished from it in any way" — is what he names phantomatics. He builds toward it through a deliberately crude thought experiment: record every impulse on every nerve of a man smelling a rose on a veranda, then play the tape back into him in a dark bath. He knows the optic nerve is the hard case, proposes an "anti-eye" worn like spectacles that samples the retina's input from in front of it (p. 198), and pauses on a detail that will matter for the next sixty years: film has "a predetermined focus," so "we cannot shift our gaze from the foreground, which is in focus, to the background," and a perfect imitation "must provide the eye with freedom also with regard to accommodation" (pp. 198–199).

Then he throws the tape away. Playback is not phantomatics, because playback is one-way, and one-way is what art already is: "Art is a unidirectional information transfer. We are just its addressees" (p. 200). A 360-degree stereoscopic cinema with a smell channel could be built "today, as long as we have proper investment" (p. 200) and would still be that. The definition to hold on to: "Phantomatics stands for creating bidirectional links between the 'artificial reality' and its recipient. In other words, phantomatics is a feedback art" (p. 200), and it "stands for creating situations in which there are no 'exits' from the worlds of created fiction into the real world" (p. 201). The machine has to react: when the phantomatized man turns to look behind the pharaoh's throne, "the impulses sent to his neck muscles by his brain must be immediately 'countered' so that the afferent projection of the optical image changes" (p. 202). And it has to populate the vision with partners who have "a (relative) autonomy of speech and action from the actions and words of the protagonist. They must not be mere puppets" (pp. 202–203). "It is easier to imitate a Miss World than Einstein. In the latter case, the machine would have to have the complexity, or intelligence, that would match the brain of a genius at its disposal" (p. 203). He is candid that none of it exists: "for the time being, neither a phantomatic machine nor any programs for it exist" (p. 202).

The heart of the chapter is the detection problem: can a person "in full possession of his mental faculties" tell they are inside? He gives the tests in ascending order. Do a hundred squats — "if we get sweaty, if we feel a little out of breath, if our heart is pounding and our muscles feel tired, we are in the real world" — until the machine learns to raise the carbon dioxide in the air you breathe (pp. 205–206). Then "'Playing intellectual games with the machine' is the only thing left" — go and talk to a philosopher you know. Here he ranks two machines by difficulty, and the ranking has to be read exactly: "a machine that imitates an interlocutor endowed with intelligence must be much more complex than a machine that creates 'soap opera' kinds of scenes such as the landing of a saucer full of Martians. As a matter of fact, a 'travel' phantomat and a 'human-producing' one are two different kinds of devices. It is incomparably more difficult to construct the second one than the first" (p. 206). Both are phantomats — nerve-connected, full-sensorium; the second adds an autonomous mind inside the world. He is ranking cost, not date. The last test is private knowledge — the desk drawer that sticks: "The easiest way to debunk a vision is through similar details" (p. 206) — and even that fails, because the machine can answer with a carpenter. So the conclusion is a probability: the statement that one is in the real world "can always be only probable, sometimes highly probable, but never absolutely certain" (p. 207). Playing against the machine "is like playing chess: a contemporary electronic machine will lose to an excellent player, but it will beat a mediocre one; in the future, it will beat every human being" (p. 207). Since asking a friend for help only feeds the machine, "a phantomatic world is a world of total solitude. There cannot be more than one person in it at any one time" (p. 207).

He then cuts his own claim down. Phantomatics is "only possible, or even probable, as a technology of entertainment" (p. 211); it will struggle to become drama, because "it is impossible to write scripts for it, only some rough situational outlines" (p. 214); its real uses will be training pilots and surgeons, testing astronauts, keeping star-ship crews sane, and sight for the blind "except for those who suffer from total blindness, i.e., whose primary visual cortex has been damaged" — so that "its entertainment role may turn out to be rather marginal" (p. 215). And he draws a limit that matters below: the phantomat "cannot be used to determine attitudes, opinions, beliefs, or feelings directly" (p. 213) — but, he adds two pages later, prolonged exposure is another matter, and "peripheral phantomatics can also, through appropriate maneuvers, lead to a change of opinion, even of a deep-rooted one. The more life experience a person has accumulated, the more difficult such a change is to accomplish" (p. 217).

The second half of the chapter leaves the phantomat behind. Cerebromatics is the direct rewrite: "Phantomatics provides the brain with 'false' information; cerebromatics falsifies, that is, 'transforms,' the brain itself" (p. 216). Lem thinks its reach is narrow — you can lobotomise, disinhibit, regress, but "it is impossible to add, in the proper sense of this term, absent traits to one's psyche" (p. 216); to turn Mr. Smith into Napoleon would produce "a completely new personality … that our procedure will practically have to be described as murder" (p. 219). Hence the rule: "one can only be oneself or nobody" (p. 221). Teletaxy is the proxy body — "exact models of the human" whose receptors and motor nerves are wired to yours, so a "remote-I" can stand "inside the crater of a volcano, atop Mount Everest, in circumterrestrial space" while you sit in Warsaw, limited by signal delay: on the Moon, "significantly delayed reactions" (p. 222). Phantoplication is a thousand people wired one-way into a marathon runner's senses (pp. 222–223). Then "Personality and Information": Wiener's idea of "'telegraphing' man" (p. 225), an atomic description sent by wire and rebuilt at the far end. Lem runs the paradox to the ground. Send it twice, or broadcast it, and Smith emerges "a million times over" (p. 226); split a brain down the corpus callosum and "the act of copying has thus indeed taken place" (p. 227); but the copy "is not the original, as it has a different history" (p. 229), so "telegraphing a person remains out of the question" (p. 229) — killing the original to complete the trip is murder with the evidence tidied away. His clinching move is two scanners, one that leaves the original alive and one that vaporises it: since the copies are identical, "the original's fate is of no consequence for what happens at the other end of the wire" (p. 235). On the way he predicts that reversible freezing "promising as it does 100 percent chance of a future revival at any given moment, will be accomplished" (p. 231). The 1964 baseline he was extrapolating from: Heilig's Sensorama and his 1960 patent for a head-worn stereoscopic "Telesphere Mask," Philco's head-tracked Headsight of 1961, stereo film and the circarama; Sperry's split-brain monkeys; Olds and Milner's rats. Later, as Zylinska summarises the 1982 and 1991 afterwords, he pointed to "virtual reality devices such as Eye Phone and Data Glove" as the prediction landing (p. 21); those afterwords are not in this edition, and this essay does not score them.

What happened

The travel phantomat's cheap version came slowly and stalled. Ivan Sutherland's head-mounted display of 1968 tracked the head from outside and redrew the view — the function Lem describes on p. 202, by a different mechanism than countering neck-muscle impulses. The Oculus Rift Kickstarter of 2012, from which today's consumer headsets descend, was bought by Facebook two years later. The industry's own tracker put combined AR/VR shipments at a peak of about 11 million units in 2021; in 2025 the category grew again only because smart glasses did — VR and mixed-reality headsets themselves fell. Against a billion-plus smartphones a year, that is a rounding error. Meta's Reality Labs has lost more than 80 billion dollars since 2020. The problem Lem named on pp. 198–199 — a fixed focal plane — has a descendant with a name, the vergence–accommodation conflict, still present in shipping headsets; varifocal prototypes have existed since 2018 and none has shipped. Measured against his definition — bidirectional, no exits, indistinguishable — nothing on sale is a phantomat.

The detection game got played anyway, on the channel he priced as expensive — but the reader must hold the analogy at arm's length. Two years after Summa, Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA, a short pattern-matching script playing a therapist, was confided in by people who knew what it was, to its author's alarm. In 2025 a pre-registered three-party Turing test — a judge talking to a human and a model for five minutes, then choosing — found GPT-4.5, when told to play a persona, judged to be the human 73 percent of the time. Five minutes of text is not Lem's "human-producing" phantomat: that machine speaks to your nerves, sits inside a faked world, and must survive a long game with a clever opponent. What the text systems show is narrower and still striking: on the conversational channel, the intellectual game Lem reserved as the last resort against a full-sensorium fake is already lost by an unwarned judge in five minutes. Hundreds of millions of people talk to such a system weekly; a small minority use it as a companion — OpenAI's own study finds emotional engagement rare — and companion apps sell that use as a product. The neurotics he predicted for the phantomat's waiting room have turned up in the chatbot's: in 2025 newspapers reported people whose sense of what was real came apart inside long conversations with a model. Different machine, same waiting room. And the small HIT he tossed off in passing — a machine will beat every human at chess — arrived in 1997.

The second half of the chapter has a 2026 side too. Cerebromatics, in Lem's sense of writing to the brain, is where brain–computer interfaces are weakest: the first commercial implants read motor intent out — Neuralink's first human participant, in 2024, moved a cursor — and stimulation is still therapeutic modulation, not the insertion of a skill. His blessing for the blind whose cortex is intact exists as a six-person feasibility trial of a cortical visual prosthesis. His remote-I exists without the nerve link: a surgeon in New York removed a gallbladder in Strasbourg in 2001 through a robot and a fibre-optic line, and the latency budget he computed for the Moon is the one operators still work to. His reversible freezing has not been accomplished for a mammal — the first vitrified rat kidney to be rewarmed and transplanted successfully was reported in 2023, and no human has been revived — while cryonics has been selling the promise since 1972. And his telegraph paradox became the standard furniture of the philosophy of personal identity: Parfit's teletransporter and its "branch-line case" appeared in Reasons and Persons in 1984, and the mind-uploading debate runs on the same two scanners Lem drew. His "only probable, never certain" (p. 207) became, in Bostrom's 2003 paper, the simulation argument.

Where Lem was better than us

Two places. First, on what a fake experience can do to a mind, he holds a position more careful than most of the sixty years of media-effects argument since. The phantomat "cannot be used to determine attitudes, opinions, beliefs, or feelings directly" (p. 213), because "two people can draw entirely different and contradictory conclusions from two identical experiences" (p. 213) — and prolonged, staged exposure "can also, through appropriate maneuvers, lead to a change of opinion, even of a deep-rooted one," harder the more life a person has behind them (p. 217). That is roughly where the best 2026 evidence sits: when researchers switched a sample of consenting Facebook and Instagram users to chronological feeds for three months around the 2020 election, their political attitudes did not measurably move. Short exposure, formed adults, no direct write. His real worry is elsewhere — solitude: once you suspect the world, every check you run through another person is a move that teaches your opponent (p. 207). That is a sharper description of life inside a personalised system than "filter bubble," and it was written before the feed. Second, the identity argument. His two scanners, and the verdict that the copy is a twin and the trip is a murder, predate Parfit's teletransporter by twenty years and are, if anything, better engineered — Parfit needed the case where the original survives; Lem built it as device O and derived the rest.

Where he was worst

He priced the wrong thing. He measured a fake by the complexity of the thing faked — a Martian saucer is cheap, a philosopher is dear — and did not price the channel. A world is gigabits a second through receivers built to catch fakes; a conversation is a few bytes a second through a receiver built to complete other minds on thin evidence. So the version of the detection game we actually run is being lost on the cheap channel while the expensive one has not been built — an outcome the ranking on p. 206 neither predicts nor forbids, since both of his devices remain unbuilt, but one his framing gave no way to see. He also assumed the interlocutor would have to be built, from an understanding of the person; the ones we have were grown from the record of what people have written, a route the chapter never considers because language, in it, is a thing that happens inside the vision, never the stuff a machine is made of. On the second half he was worse in a smaller way: reversible freezing "will be accomplished" was 1964 confidence about cryobiology that sixty years have not repaid, and his hereditary knowledge — babies born knowing "more than ten languages and of quantum theory" (p. 218) — he floated and immediately hedged.

Still open

  • Whether the travel phantomat arrives at all. What would close it: a display that solves accommodation and haptics well enough for a majority of users to keep it on for hours, and a market that pays for it — neither exists; Meta's losses are the current price of the experiment.
  • Whether the cheap interlocutor meets Lem's own bar. He asked for an autonomy that survives a long game with a clever opponent (pp. 206–207). Passing a five-minute test is not that. What would close it: a model a domain expert cannot distinguish from a peer over weeks — or a demonstrated ceiling.
  • Whether "one person at a time" was a law or a limitation. Lem's phantomat is solitary by construction (p. 207); networked social worlds are shared, but none is a phantomat. What would close it: a system meeting his definition with two people in it.

Reading

Lem: "The Fundamentals of Phantomatics" and "The Phantomatic Machine" (pp. 197–208) carry the argument; "The Limits of Phantomatics" (pp. 211–215) is his own hedge; then keep going — "Cerebromatics" (pp. 215–221) and "Personality and Information" (pp. 224–236) are the half of the chapter that has aged best. Zylinska's introduction, p. 21, for what the 1982 and 1991 afterwords added.

2026 side: Jones & Bergen, "Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test" (2025); IDC's AR/VR headset tracker; Meta's Reality Labs disclosures; Weizenbaum's 1966 ELIZA paper; Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" (2003); Parfit, Reasons and Persons (1984), part III; Guess et al., Science (2023) on chronological feeds; Han et al., Nature Communications (2023) on the vitrified kidney.
