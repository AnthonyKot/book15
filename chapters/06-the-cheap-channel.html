Essay 6 · Lem ch. 6, "Phantomology" (2013 tr., pp. 197–236)

The cheap channel

You have probably worn a headset once and taken it off after twenty minutes. You have almost certainly talked to a machine this year that talked back well enough that you forgot, for a sentence, what it was. Neither is the device Lem describes in this chapter — a machine that feeds your nerves a world you cannot tell from the real one — and he would be the first to say so. But the question he built the machine to ask, can you tell?, is being answered in 2026 on a channel he never priced at all — cheap, one-dimensional conversation — and that transposition is this essay's, not his. The second half of his chapter, which almost nobody reads, is the argument about copying and rewriting people whose cases philosophy later made canonical.

What Lem said

The chapter opens with a question, not a gadget: "how do we create realities for the intelligent beings that exist in them, realities that are absolutely indistinguishable from the standard reality but that are subject to different laws?" (p. 197). The modest version — an artificial reality "very similar to the actual one yet that cannot be distinguished from it in any way" — is what he names phantomatics. He builds toward it through a crude thought experiment — record every nerve impulse of a man smelling a rose, play the tape back into him in a dark bath — and pauses on a detail that will matter for sixty years: film has "a predetermined focus," so "we cannot shift our gaze from the foreground, which is in focus, to the background," and a perfect imitation "must provide the eye with freedom also with regard to accommodation" (pp. 198–199).

Then he throws the tape away. Playback is one-way, and one-way is what art already is: "Art is a unidirectional information transfer. We are just its addressees" (p. 200). The definition to hold on to: "Phantomatics stands for creating bidirectional links between the 'artificial reality' and its recipient. In other words, phantomatics is a feedback art" (p. 200), and it "stands for creating situations in which there are no 'exits' from the worlds of created fiction into the real world" (p. 201). The machine has to react: when the man turns to look behind the pharaoh's throne, "the impulses sent to his neck muscles by his brain must be immediately 'countered' so that the afferent projection of the optical image changes" (p. 202). And it has to populate the vision with partners who have "a (relative) autonomy of speech and action from the actions and words of the protagonist. They must not be mere puppets" (pp. 202–203). "It is easier to imitate a Miss World than Einstein. In the latter case, the machine would have to have the complexity, or intelligence, that would match the brain of a genius at its disposal" (p. 203). He is candid that none of it exists: "for the time being, neither a phantomatic machine nor any programs for it exist" (p. 202).

The heart of the chapter is the detection problem: can a person "in full possession of his mental faculties" tell they are inside? He gives the tests in ascending order. Do a hundred squats — "if we get sweaty, if we feel a little out of breath, if our heart is pounding and our muscles feel tired, we are in the real world" — until the machine learns to raise the carbon dioxide you breathe (pp. 205–206). Then "'Playing intellectual games with the machine' is the only thing left" — go and talk to a philosopher you know. Here he ranks two machines by difficulty, and the ranking has to be read exactly: "a machine that imitates an interlocutor endowed with intelligence must be much more complex than a machine that creates 'soap opera' kinds of scenes such as the landing of a saucer full of Martians. As a matter of fact, a 'travel' phantomat and a 'human-producing' one are two different kinds of devices. It is incomparably more difficult to construct the second one than the first" (p. 206). Both are phantomats — nerve-connected, full-sensorium; the second adds an autonomous mind inside the world. He is ranking cost, not date, and he is pricing by the complexity of the thing faked. The last test is private knowledge — the drawer that sticks: "The easiest way to debunk a vision is through similar details" (p. 206) — and even that fails. So the conclusion is a probability: the statement that one is in the real world "can always be only probable, sometimes highly probable, but never absolutely certain" (p. 207). Since asking a friend for help only feeds the machine, "a phantomatic world is a world of total solitude. There cannot be more than one person in it at any one time" (p. 207). The omniplanetary "Superphantomat" that follows is a reductio — a civilisation wired in for good "would only exist for the duration of one generation" (p. 207) — an argument, not a forecast.

He sorts the field: "peripheral" phantomatics feeds the senses; "central phantomatics," direct stimulation of the brain's reward centres after Olds and Milner's rats, would be "a technology of facilitated ecstasy" that "cannot in any case be considered an art" (p. 210). Then he cuts his claim down: phantomatics "can mainly be a form of entertainment" (p. 214), yet beside training pilots and surgeons, keeping star-ship crews sane, and sight for the blind "except for those who suffer from total blindness, i.e., whose primary visual cortex has been damaged," "its entertainment role may turn out to be rather marginal" (p. 215). Two limits matter below. The phantomat "cannot be used to determine attitudes, opinions, beliefs, or feelings directly" (p. 213) — but prolonged staged exposure is another matter, and "peripheral phantomatics can also, through appropriate maneuvers, lead to a change of opinion, even of a deep-rooted one. The more life experience a person has accumulated, the more difficult such a change is to accomplish" (p. 217).

The second half leaves the phantomat. Cerebromatics is the direct rewrite: "Phantomatics provides the brain with 'false' information; cerebromatics falsifies, that is, 'transforms,' the brain itself" (p. 216). Its reach is narrow — you can lobotomise, disinhibit, regress, but "it is impossible to add, in the proper sense of this term, absent traits to one's psyche" through such maneuvers (p. 216), and writing a skill in directly is "not simple even on a theoretical level" (p. 217); to turn Mr. Smith into Napoleon would produce "a completely new personality" so different "that our procedure will practically have to be described as murder" (p. 219). Hence "one can only be oneself or nobody" (p. 221). Teletaxy is the proxy body — "exact models of the human" whose receptors and motor nerves are wired to yours, so a "remote-I" can stand "inside the crater of a volcano" while you sit in Warsaw, limited by signal delay: on the Moon, "significantly delayed reactions" (p. 222). Then "Personality and Information": Wiener's "'telegraphing' man" (p. 225), an atomic description sent by wire and rebuilt. Broadcast it and Smith emerges "a million times over" (p. 226); but the copy "is not the original, as it has a different history" (p. 229), so "telegraphing a person remains out of the question" (p. 229) — killing the original to complete the trip is murder with the evidence tidied away. His clinching move is two scanners, one that leaves the original alive and one that vaporises it: since the copies are identical, "the original's fate is of no consequence for what happens at the other end of the wire" (p. 235). On the way: "We can expect that such a freezing technology, promising as it does 100 percent chance of a future revival at any given moment, will be accomplished" (p. 231). The 1964 baseline: Heilig's Sensorama and his 1960 patent for a head-worn stereoscopic "Telesphere Mask," Philco's head-tracked Headsight of 1961, stereo film and the circarama; split-brain monkeys; Olds and Milner's rats. Later, as Zylinska summarises the 1982 and 1991 afterwords, he pointed to "virtual reality devices such as Eye Phone and Data Glove" as evidence of what he "did manage to predict correctly" (p. 21). The afterwords are not in this edition; the ledger scores that self-grade only as far as her summary carries it.

What happened

The travel phantomat's cheap version came slowly and stalled. Sutherland's head-mounted display of 1968 tracked the head from outside and redrew the view — Lem's p. 202 function, by another mechanism. The Oculus Rift Kickstarter of 2012 was bought by Facebook two years later. The industry's own tracker put combined AR/VR shipments at a peak of about 11 million units in 2021; in 2025 the category grew again only because smart glasses did — VR and mixed-reality headsets themselves fell. Against a billion-plus smartphones a year, a rounding error. Meta's Reality Labs has lost more than 80 billion dollars since 2020. The problem Lem named on pp. 198–199 — a fixed focal plane — has a descendant with a name, the vergence–accommodation conflict, still present in shipping headsets; varifocal prototypes have existed since 2018 and none has shipped. Measured against his definition — bidirectional, no exits, indistinguishable — nothing on sale is a phantomat, which is why the later Lem's pointing at EyePhone and DataGlove reads, from here, as premature.

The detection game got played anyway — and the transposing here is the essay's, not Lem's. Two years after Summa, Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA, a short pattern-matching script playing a therapist, was confided in by people who knew what it was, to its author's alarm. In 2025 a pre-registered three-party Turing test — a judge talking to a human and a model for five minutes, then choosing — found GPT-4.5, when told to play a persona, judged to be the human 73 percent of the time. Five minutes of text is not Lem's "human-producing" phantomat, which speaks to your nerves inside a faked world and must survive a long game with a clever opponent. What the text systems show is narrower and still striking: on the conversational channel, a judge who knows one of two partners is a machine, and has five minutes to pick, picks wrong more often than not. Hundreds of millions talk to such a system weekly; a small minority as a companion — OpenAI's own study finds emotional engagement rare — and companion apps sell that use. The neurotics he predicted for the phantomat's waiting room have been reported in the chatbot's — 2025 newspaper accounts of people whose sense of what was real came apart inside long conversations with a model; reports are what they are. The other cheap fake is the one he classed as art: the one-way image. Generated images made "can you tell?" a mass problem on exactly the channel he set outside phantomatics — in May 2023 a fake photograph of an explosion at the Pentagon spread on social media and briefly moved the stock market.

The second half of the chapter has a 2026 side too. Cerebromatics, writing to the brain, is where brain–computer interfaces are weakest: the implants in human trials read motor intent out — Neuralink's first participant, in 2024, moved a cursor — and the write side is stimulation: sensation restored by intracortical microstimulation, mood and movement modulated by deep-brain electrodes; nothing yet that adds a skill. His blessing for the blind whose cortex is intact exists as a six-person feasibility trial of a cortical visual prosthesis. His remote-I has both halves in trials — a paralysed woman drove a robotic arm from her motor cortex in 2012; tactile feedback was written into a participant's cortex in 2021 — while teleoperation without the nerve link is routine: a New York surgeon removed a gallbladder in Strasbourg in 2001 at a delay of about 155 milliseconds; his lunar second-each-way would still disqualify a surgeon. His reversible freezing has not been accomplished for any mammal — vitrified rat kidneys were rewarmed and transplanted with lasting function in 2023; there is no dated path from an organ to an organism, and no human has been revived — while cryonics has been selling the promise since 1972. His telegraph paradox has the structure of the cases Parfit made canonical in Reasons and Persons (1984) — the teletransporter, the branch line — and the upload debate runs on them still. His "only probable, never certain" (p. 207) is where the skeptical literature still sits; Bostrom's simulation argument (2003) is a cousin, not the same result.

Where Lem was better than us

Two places. First, on what a fake experience can do to a mind, he is more careful than most of the argument since. The phantomat "cannot be used to determine attitudes, opinions, beliefs, or feelings directly" (p. 213), and prolonged, staged exposure "can also, through appropriate maneuvers, lead to a change of opinion, even of a deep-rooted one," harder the more life a person has behind them (p. 217). The feed experiments, which are not his cases, at least do not contradict him: three months of chronological feeds on Facebook and Instagram moved nothing measurable, and seven weeks of X's algorithm switched on shifted issue priorities but not partisanship or affective polarization. His real worry is elsewhere — solitude: once you suspect the world, every check you run through another person is a move that teaches your opponent (p. 207). That is a sharper description of life inside a personalised system than "filter bubble," written before the feed. Second, the identity argument: his two scanners, twenty years before Parfit's teletransporter and branch line, are the same cases with the opposite conclusion, and the argument is still open.

Where he was worst

He priced the wrong thing — for the problem as it actually arrived. He measured a fake by the complexity of the thing faked; the fake that fools people at scale in 2026 is a few sentences of text, because the receiver completes other minds on thin evidence, and the fake that does not exist is the one he thought cheap, the world. That is a verdict on his cost model, not on his ranking of two unbuilt machines. The narrower omission is that the chapter never proposes growing a general interlocutor by training on a text corpus at scale — language, in it, happens inside the vision; it is never what a machine is trained on. He also excluded the one-way image from the problem by classing it as art; deepfakes are the one-way image posing exactly his question. On the second half he was worse in a smaller way: reversible freezing "will be accomplished" is the chapter's cleanest MISS.

Still open

  • Whether the travel phantomat arrives at all. What would close it: a system that is bidirectional across the sensorium and defeats adversarial reality tests — the squats, the drawer — not a comfortable headset with a market; Meta's losses are the current price of the experiment.
  • Whether the cheap interlocutor meets Lem's own bar — an autonomy that survives a long game with a clever opponent (pp. 206–207); five minutes is not that. What would close it: a model a domain expert cannot distinguish from a peer over weeks — or a demonstrated ceiling.
  • Whether central phantomatics gets built. Reward-centre stimulation exists as therapy, not as his "technology of facilitated ecstasy" (p. 210). What would close it: a stimulation device sold or sought for pleasure — or a record of it tried and stopped.

Reading

Lem: pp. 197–208 carry the argument; "The Limits of Phantomatics" (pp. 211–215) is his own hedge; then keep going — "Cerebromatics" (pp. 215–221) and "Personality and Information" (pp. 224–236) are the half that has aged best. Zylinska, p. 21, for the afterwords.

2026 side: Jones & Bergen, "Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test" (2025); IDC's AR/VR tracker; Weizenbaum's 1966 ELIZA paper; Chalmers, Reality+ (2022); Bostrom (2003); Parfit, Reasons and Persons (1984), part III; Guess et al., Science (2023) and Gauthier et al., Nature (2026) on feeds; Han et al., Nature Communications (2023) on the vitrified kidney.
