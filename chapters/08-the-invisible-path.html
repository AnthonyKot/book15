Essay 8 · Lem ch. 8, "A Lampoon of Evolution" (2013 tr., pp. 294–353)

The invisible path

Most of this chapter is an argument, staged between an advocate of redesigning the human body and two opponents, about whether we may. It is the part everyone quotes. Then, four pages from the end of the book, Lem stops arguing and makes a prediction that nobody was making: the redesign will not arrive by surgery, and the reason it will succeed is that it will not look like a redesign at all. It will look like a dating service. Sixty years later the loud path has been criminalised and stopped, the quiet one is a consumer product, and his last sentence on the subject — that the two are "just two paths of different lengths that can both lead to analogous results" (p. 347) — reads like a description of the present. He got the route right and almost everything about the machinery wrong.

What Lem said

The chapter opens by laying out three ways to intervene in the human organism. Take the body as given and repair it — transplants, prostheses — which he calls "the most traditional and shortsighted approach" (p. 297). Or replace "Nature's evolutionary gradients with man's purposeful regulatory activity" (p. 297), a programme of biological autoevolution producing "the next model" of Homo sapiens over centuries. Or "replace reconstruction with a total rejection of the existing solution and then design a completely new one" (p. 297). Most of the chapter then stages a debate between an advocate of the third option and his opponents, and the advocate gets the best line: "Man cannot change the world without changing himself" (p. 302). Against him stands an argument Lem takes seriously — that our values are not separable from our bodies, that "there are no values that could have emerged without the presence of the corporeal factor" (p. 299).

Before he gets to the prediction he clears away the version of the future that was actually on the table in 1966. "Cyborgization", the Clynes–Kline proposal for adapting an astronaut to space, "consists in removing the digestive system" (p. 342), the jaw and teeth becoming redundant, osmotic pumps injecting nutrients and hibernation agents. Lem is unimpressed, and his objection is not squeamishness. The cyborg "is by no means a more universal human being than the 'current model'" — it is a variant fitted to the Moon and Mars, and the real cost is "the very idea of 'degeneralizing man'", producing human types "more or less in the likeness and image of various kinds of ants" (p. 343). He also says what will happen instead, in one clause: "It is possible to perform hibernation without osmotic pumps, just as it would be possible to equip an astronaut with a number of microattachments" delivering substances into his system (p. 343).

On lifespan he is equally deflationary. "Expanding the human life span beyond the period of one hundred years statistically (i.e., so it becomes an average life span for an individual existence), without interfering with man's genetic information, seems unrealistic to me" (p. 340). The people promising 140 years because some individuals reach it are arguing, he says, like someone who claims we could all be Beethoven. His own diagnosis is genetic and blunt: "Longevity is a product of particular genes; whoever distributes them across the population will make it statistically long-living" (p. 340).

Then comes "The Autoevolutionary Machine", and the tone changes. He notes that "For several years now in the United States, digital machines programmed to arrange marriages have been in operation" (p. 344), that they select couples "best matched physically and intellectually", and — a reported figure, not his own — that "the stability of machinically arranged relationships is twice as high as that of regular marriages" (p. 344). What he does with this is the interesting part. The machine, he argues, does not remove choice: it "does not lead to just one candidate", it offers a preselected group, and it can search millions where a person meets a few hundred in a lifetime. So it "fulfils the age-old myth about men and women being destined for each other yet looking for each other in vain" (p. 344). And then the forecast, which is about adoption rather than technology: "the advantages of arranging marriages from the position of 'better knowledge' at the machine's disposal are so much greater than the disadvantages that there is a significant chance for technology of this kind to be widely adopted" (p. 345). More than adopted — normative. "When it becomes a cultural norm, the marriage discouraged by the 'machinic matchmaker' will perhaps be a forbidden and thus tempting fruit" (p. 345).

The second half of the section is the part that has aged strangely. Once genotypes can be read, he says, the matchmaker's task will be to make a choice that "not only matches humans with other humans but also genotypes with other genotypes. It will thus be a two-level selection process" (p. 345). The first level is psychosomatic. The second is to "reject candidates who are highly likely to produce offspring that would be considered undesirable for various reasons—for example, disabled (which will be a correct decision), or endowed with a low level of intelligence, or with unstable personalities (which raises some doubts, at least for the time being)" (p. 345). He does not soften the conclusion: "matching people's genotypes amounts to controlling evolution" (p. 345). Nor the reason it will work: "This technology seems the least drastic one as it remains invisible—yet this also brings up a difficult moral dilemma" (p. 345). The dilemma is a problem about consent that he states better than most: "society needs to be informed about any significant transformations", but any justification "will only really be understandable to those who have extensive knowledge in the fields of medicine, evolutionary theory, anthropology, and population genetics" (p. 346).

Two further claims complete the forecast. That some traits would be easy: "It would be relatively easy, for example, to aim to distribute widely high intelligence as a natural characteristic of the species" (p. 346). And that all of it would be slow — "'Marriage machines' could take thousands of years before they arrive at the desired state of events because they are solely capable of maximizing evolution's natural speed" (p. 347). Then the sentence the whole essay turns on: "'Cutting up people's brains and bodies' evokes disgust, whereas 'machinic marriage counseling' seems to be quite an innocent intervention—yet these are just two paths of different lengths that can both lead to analogous results" (p. 347).

Note 2 to this chapter (pp. 368–369) is where he answers his own opponents, and it is sharper than the staged debate. Against the absolutist: "An opponent of 'bioconstructionism' cannot limit himself to opposing any 'plans for man's redesign' but should rather give up on all of civilization's achievements, such as medicine and technology, and retreat to the forest on all four" (p. 369). Against himself: "Will the elimination of, say, epilepsy not get rid of both epileptics and Dostoyevskys?" (p. 368). And the position he lands on: "The aura of moral responsibility must envelop the field of bioconstructionism", but "it is high time he took such a conscious and responsible risk as soon as the state of his knowledge allows for it, even if this knowledge remains incomplete" (p. 369). The mid-1960s baseline: the cyborg paper of 1960; computer date-matching demonstrated at Stanford on an IBM 650 in 1959 and running at scale as Operation Match from 1965; the genetic code being cracked; and no way whatever to read an individual's genome.

What happened

The adoption forecast came true, on a scale he did not name. Meeting online overtook meeting through friends around 2013 and is now the most common way heterosexual couples in the United States meet. Machine-mediated pairing is not a curiosity practised by a few thousand students; it is the default. What did not come true is the normative half. No app discourages a marriage, and no marriage is a forbidden fruit for having been chosen against the machine's advice, because the machines do not give that kind of advice. They rank and they queue.

The premise underneath was never established either. Lem's argument rests on the machine having "better knowledge" (p. 345), and the two-to-one stability figure he repeats is a 1960s marketing number, not a finding. When the question was put properly, the answer went the other way: machine learning over more than a hundred self-reported traits and preferences could predict, modestly, how much a person would be desired in general, and could not predict how two particular people would feel about each other before they met. Sixty years on there is still no demonstration that algorithmic matching produces better relationships. The technology he predicted arrived without the justification he gave for it.

The second level arrived too — and this is where the essay's argument sits. It did not arrive in the matchmaker. It arrived one step further down the reproductive pipeline, at the embryo. Companies now sell polygenic scoring of IVF embryos; in June 2025 one launched a product ranking embryos on IQ, height, hair and eye colour, anxiety and ADHD alongside disease risk, from about $5,999. That is his menu, item for item — the disabled, the low in intelligence, the unstably personalitied — sold to parents rather than applied to couples. Selecting embryos on predicted high IQ is prohibited in the United Kingdom and essentially unregulated in the United States.

Meanwhile the loud path ran its course exactly as his sentence about disgust predicts. In November 2018 He Jiankui announced two babies whose CCR5 genes he had edited; he was convicted, imprisoned for three years, fined and banned for life, and no further gene-edited children are known to have been born anywhere. In 2025 the major cell and gene therapy societies called for a ten-year global moratorium on heritable editing. Cutting up bodies evoked disgust, and stopped. Marriage counselling did not, and did not.

Where he was plainly wrong is the difficulty. "Relatively easy" is not what selection on intelligence turns out to be. Choose the top-scoring of ten embryos on a cognitive polygenic score and the expected gain is about 2.5 IQ points, with prediction intervals wide enough to swallow it; in real families, the sibling with the highest score for height was the tallest in only about a quarter of cases. The limit is not a technology gap. Siblings differ by only the fraction of genetic variance that segregates within a family, and no amount of better scoring enlarges that pool. He was also wrong about the clock, in the opposite direction: selection at the embryo is not "maximizing evolution's natural speed", because it does not run through mating at all. It acts in one generation. The speed limit he assumed dissolved; a harder limit he could not have seen took its place.

His deflations held. No population's average life expectancy is anywhere near a hundred; gains have decelerated since 1990 across the longest-lived countries, and radical extension without a breakthrough that slows aging itself is judged implausible for this century. And no astronaut has had a digestive system removed. What we did instead is precisely the "microattachments" clause: programmable implanted pumps, implanted insulin delivery, subdermal hormone implants. The cyborg was a costume; Lem said so at the time and named the cheaper thing that would happen instead.

One more thing happened that his consent problem did not survive. In March 2025 the largest consumer-genomics company entered bankruptcy holding genetic data on roughly fifteen million people, and that data changed hands twice in four months through a court auction, with an ombudsman appointed to consider what it meant for the people it described. Whatever "society needs to be informed" means, it does not describe fifteen million individual consents being resolved as a bankruptcy asset.

Where Lem was better than us

He put the moral weight in the right place, which almost nobody since has done. The 2018 scandal produced years of argument about editing, a criminal conviction and a call for a moratorium; the thing that actually changed the distribution of who gets born is a screening product with a price list, and it attracts a fraction of the attention. Lem saw in 1966 that this asymmetry was the whole problem — that disgust tracks the vividness of the procedure and not the size of its effect — and he said it in one sentence about brains and marriage counselling. He also saw that invisibility and consent are the same problem: a transformation society must be told about, whose justification only specialists can evaluate, is not a transformation anyone has agreed to. And he refused the easy exit in both directions. Note 2 will not let the objector keep medicine while refusing bioconstruction, and it will not let the enthusiast pretend the risk is small — "Will the elimination of, say, epilepsy not get rid of both epileptics and Dostoyevskys?" (p. 368) is a better sentence than most of what the polygenic-screening debate has produced.

Where he was worst

He got the unit of selection wrong, and everything else follows from it. His machine matches couples, because in 1966 the only place to intervene between two genomes was the choice of whose genomes met. That assumption produced the timescale — thousands of years, because you are riding on mating — and it produced the picture of a slow, gentle, statistical drift that nobody would notice happening. Then in vitro fertilisation moved the decision point past conception, and the whole forecast shifted a generation downstream: the selection is not among partners but among the embryos a given pair of partners has already produced. That is faster than he imagined and also weaker, because siblings vary less than strangers do, and it is the reason his "relatively easy" is wrong.

The deeper miss is that he assumed the selector would know what it was doing. He grants the machine "better knowledge" without asking what would establish it, and the two-to-one stability figure he reports goes unexamined — the one place in this chapter where he takes a number on trust. Sixty years later the matching still has no demonstrated efficacy, and the genotype screening is being sold with confidence intervals its buyers are not shown. His invisible path turned out to be invisible in a second sense he did not anticipate: not only does nobody see it operating, nobody can see whether it works.

Still open

  • Whether selection at the embryo does anything at population scale. The per-family effect is small and the uptake is small. What would close it: a measurable shift in a trait distribution in a birth cohort attributable to screening — or a demonstration that within-family variance caps it permanently below detection.
  • Whether the consent problem has any solution. His statement of it (pp. 345–346) has not been improved on and has not been answered. What would close it: a mechanism by which a population actually authorises a change to its own genetic composition, as opposed to individual purchases aggregating into one.
  • Whether the two paths really do converge. Lem asserts that they reach "analogous results". Editing writes variants that no parent carries; selection can only rearrange what they have. What would close it: either heritable editing at a scale that outruns selection, or a demonstration that the achievable ends of the two are the same after all.

Reading

Lem: "The Autoevolutionary Machine" (pp. 344–347) is the chapter, and the last two pages are the argument; "Cyborgization" (pp. 341–344) is the proposal he demolishes first. Read note 2 (pp. 368–369) before the staged debate at pp. 297–302 — it is where he argues with himself instead of with a constructed opponent, and it is better.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. Rosenfeld, Thomas & Hausen (PNAS, 2019) for what happened to matchmaking; Karavani et al., "Screening Human Embryos for Polygenic Traits Has Limited Utility" (Cell, 2019) for what selection can and cannot buy; and the reporting on Nucleus Embryo (2025) for what is now on sale. The rest is in the claims register.
