Essay 8 · Lem ch. 8, "A Lampoon of Evolution" (2013 tr., pp. 294–353)

The invisible path

Most of this chapter is an argument, staged between an advocate of redesigning the human body and two opponents, about whether we may. Then, near the end, Lem stops arguing and predicts something nobody else was predicting: the redesign will not arrive by surgery, and it will succeed because it will not look like a redesign. It will look like a matchmaking service that compares two people's genotypes before they meet. That machine exists. It has existed since 1983. What has not arrived is the thing he assumed would carry it — the authority to tell a couple no.

What Lem said

The chapter opens with three ways to intervene in the human organism: repair the body as given, "the most traditional and shortsighted approach"; replace "Nature's evolutionary gradients with man's purposeful regulatory activity"; or "replace reconstruction with a total rejection of the existing solution and then design a completely new one" (p. 297). Most of the chapter stages a debate between an advocate of the third and his opponents: "Man cannot change the world without changing himself" (p. 302) against "there are no values that could have emerged without the presence of the corporeal factor" (p. 299).

He then clears away the version of the future actually on the table. "Cyborgization", the Clynes–Kline proposal for adapting an astronaut to space, "consists in removing the digestive system" (p. 342). Lem is unimpressed, and not from squeamishness: the cyborg "is by no means a more universal human being than the 'current model'", and the cost is "the very idea of 'degeneralizing man'", producing types "in the likeness and image of various kinds of ants" (p. 343). He names what happens instead: "it would be possible to equip an astronaut with a number of microattachments" delivering substances into his system (p. 343). On lifespan he is equally deflationary: "Expanding the human life span beyond the period of one hundred years statistically… without interfering with man's genetic information, seems unrealistic to me", because "Longevity is a product of particular genes" (p. 340).

"The Autoevolutionary Machine" opens by naming the images it is not about: "Brain surgery, 'bottled fetuses developing under the control of genetic engineering'—these are the kinds of images offered by the science fiction literature on this topic. Yet the procedures that will be used may actually be invisible" (p. 344). His example: "digital machines programmed to arrange marriages have been in operation" (p. 344) in the United States, selecting couples "best matched physically and intellectually", with — he flags the evidence himself — "According to the (so far limited) results, the stability of machinically arranged relationships is twice as high as that of regular marriages" (p. 344). The machine does not remove choice; it "does not lead to just one candidate", offering a preselected group, searching millions where a person meets a few hundred. The forecast is hedged, and about adoption: "the advantages of arranging marriages from the position of 'better knowledge'… are so much greater than the disadvantages that there is a significant chance for technology of this kind to be widely adopted" (p. 345). And conditionally, about authority: "When it becomes a cultural norm, the marriage discouraged by the 'machinic matchmaker' will perhaps be a forbidden and thus tempting fruit" (p. 345).

Then the second stage. Once genotypes can be read, the matchmaker will make a choice that "not only matches humans with other humans but also genotypes with other genotypes. It will thus be a two-level selection process" (p. 345). The second level rejects "candidates who are highly likely to produce offspring that would be considered undesirable for various reasons—for example, disabled (which will be a correct decision), or endowed with a low level of intelligence, or with unstable personalities (which raises some doubts, at least for the time being)" (p. 345). The moral grading there is his: disability screening correct, the other two doubtful. He does not soften the conclusion — "matching people's genotypes amounts to controlling evolution" (p. 345) — nor the reason it works: "This technology seems the least drastic one as it remains invisible—yet this also brings up a difficult moral dilemma" (p. 345). The dilemma is consent: "society needs to be informed about any significant transformations" (p. 345), yet the justification "will only really be understandable to those who have extensive knowledge in the fields of medicine, evolutionary theory, anthropology, and population genetics" (p. 346).

Two claims complete it. That some traits would be easy — "It would be relatively easy, for example, to aim to distribute widely high intelligence as a natural characteristic of the species" (p. 346), easy by contrast with "a deep transformation of man's systemic organization". And that the vehicle is slow: "'Marriage machines' could take thousands of years… because they are solely capable of maximizing evolution's natural speed" (p. 347). Then the sentence the essay turns on: "'Cutting up people's brains and bodies' evokes disgust, whereas 'machinic marriage counseling' seems to be quite an innocent intervention—yet these are just two paths of different lengths that can both lead to analogous results" (p. 347).

Note 2 (pp. 368–369) is where he argues with himself rather than a constructed opponent, and it is sharper. Against the absolutist: "An opponent of 'bioconstructionism' cannot limit himself to opposing any 'plans for man's redesign' but should rather give up on all of civilization's achievements, such as medicine and technology, and retreat to the forest on all four" (p. 369). Against himself: "Will the elimination of, say, epilepsy not get rid of both epileptics and Dostoyevskys?" (p. 368). And the landing: "The aura of moral responsibility must envelop the field of bioconstructionism", but "it is high time he took such a conscious and responsible risk… even if this knowledge remains incomplete" (p. 369). The baseline: the cyborg paper of 1960; computer date-matching at Stanford on an IBM 650 in 1959, then at scale as Operation Match from 1965; the genetic code being cracked; no way to read a genome — bearing in mind that this translation follows the altered fourth edition of 1974, so the text is mid-1960s as revised.

What happened

The second stage was built, and it was built as a matchmaker. Dor Yeshorim has run since 1983: young people in Orthodox Jewish communities are tested and given an anonymous number, and when a match is proposed the two numbers are checked together, before the couple meets, returning one answer — proceed, or incompatible. It has screened hundreds of thousands of people across eleven countries. That is Lem's p. 345 device, operating on the branch he called "a correct decision": serious recessive disease. It is not operating on the other two. Nobody is screened out for a low predicted intelligence or an unstable personality, and the programme is bounded to communities that asked for it.

Outside those communities the second stage went somewhere else entirely — not to the matchmaker but one step down the reproductive pipeline, to the embryo. Companies sell polygenic scoring of IVF embryos; in June 2025 one launched a product ranking them on IQ, height, hair and eye colour, anxiety and ADHD alongside disease risk, from about $5,999. The trait list overlaps his and extends past it. This is a different device from the one he described, applied to a different object, and this essay counts it as a third path rather than as his second stage arriving late.

The first stage — the psychosomatic matchmaker becoming general — is harder to score than it looks. Meeting online overtook meeting through friends around 2013 and is now the most common way US heterosexual couples meet, but that is a finding about where people meet, not evidence that a compatibility algorithm chose for them. What certainly did not arrive is the second half of his sentence. No mass platform discourages a marriage or claims the authority to; none has become the norm against which a match could be a forbidden fruit. Dor Yeshorim is the exception that shows the condition: it acquired that authority because the community already had it, and Lem never named that as a requirement.

The premise underneath was never established either way. Lem rests his forecast on the machine's "better knowledge" and flags his own evidence as limited. The best-known test since is narrower than it is usually reported: machine learning over more than a hundred self-reported traits could modestly predict how much a person would desire partners in general, and could not predict how two particular people would feel before meeting. That is a result about initial attraction, not about whether matched marriages last, and the question he actually asked is still unanswered.

The loud path ran the course his sentence about disgust predicts. He Jiankui announced CCR5-edited twins in November 2018; a third edited child was born the following year, confirmed by the court that convicted him in December 2019 and sentenced him to three years, with a lifetime ban from reproductive medicine. In 2025 the major cell and gene therapy societies called for a ten-year moratorium on heritable editing — a professional call, not a ban. Cutting up bodies drew a prison sentence. Comparing two ID numbers over the telephone has drawn nothing.

His deflations held. No population's average life expectancy is near a hundred; gains have decelerated since 1990 and radical extension is judged implausible this century without slowing aging itself. No astronaut has had a digestive system removed; what exists is the "microattachments" clause — implanted intrathecal pumps, subdermal hormone implants. And on difficulty he was wrong in the direction he could not have checked: distributing high intelligence is not "relatively easy" by any known route. Selection among a couple's own embryos is bounded by the variance that segregates within one family — about 2.5 IQ points for the top of ten on current predictors, with wide intervals, though that figure rises as scores improve. Whether assortative matching over many generations could do more is exactly what his machine was for, and nobody has run it.

Where Lem was better than us

He put the moral weight where it belongs, which almost nothing written since has done as plainly. Editing three children produced a criminal trial, a professional moratorium and years of argument. A telephone line that tells two people they may not marry has run for four decades and is discussed, when at all, as a public-health success. Lem saw in 1966 that this asymmetry was the problem itself — that disgust tracks the vividness of the procedure, not the size of its effect — and said so in one sentence about brains and marriage counselling. He also refused both easy exits. Note 2 will not let the objector keep medicine while rejecting bioconstruction, and it will not let the enthusiast pretend the risk is small: "Will the elimination of, say, epilepsy not get rid of both epileptics and Dostoyevskys?" (p. 368). And his moral grading inside the forecast has held up better than his sociology — he marked disability screening as correct and intelligence and personality as doubtful, which is exactly the line the built system respects and the sold product does not.

Where he was worst

He assumed the machine would acquire authority by being right. The forecast is that once "better knowledge" is demonstrated the technology becomes "a cultural norm", after which a discouraged marriage is a forbidden fruit. Neither half happened where the machines actually went. The better knowledge was never demonstrated — the evidence he cites he calls limited, and it has not improved. And the platforms that matched hundreds of millions of people acquired no standing to discourage anyone; they rank and they queue, and a user who ignores them has broken no rule. The one place the norm exists, it was not earned by the machine at all: Dor Yeshorim works because a community that already had authority over marriage chose to route it through a test. That precondition is the load-bearing part, and it is the part he leaves out.

He also assumed the unit of selection would stay the couple, which is why the trait branch went somewhere he was not looking. In vitro fertilisation moved the decision past conception, and the menu he wrote — intelligence, personality — is now sold as embryo scoring rather than partner screening. That is a third path, not his second stage arriving late, and this essay scores it as one. Whether it can do anything at population scale is unsettled; whether his own route could have done more, over the millennia he allowed it, nobody has tested and probably nobody will.

Still open

  • Whether any of it moves a population. Neither the partner screening nor the embryo scoring has been shown to shift a trait distribution in a birth cohort. What would close it: a measured shift attributable to either — or a demonstration that within-family variance and uptake cap both below detection.
  • Whether the consent problem has a general answer. His statement of it (pp. 345–346) has not been improved on. One bounded community answered it by already being able to decide together. What would close it: a mechanism by which a population authorises a change to its own genetic composition, rather than individual purchases aggregating into one.
  • Whether the two paths converge. Lem asserts they reach "analogous results" (p. 347); this essay does not score that, because nothing tests it. Editing writes variants no parent carries; selection can only rearrange what they have. What would close it: heritable editing at a scale that outruns selection, or a demonstration that the achievable ends are the same.

Reading

Lem: "The Autoevolutionary Machine" (pp. 344–347) is the chapter, and it opens by telling you which images to ignore; "Cyborgization" (pp. 341–344) is the proposal he demolishes first. Read note 2 (pp. 368–369) before the staged debate at pp. 297–302 — it is where he argues with himself instead of with a constructed opponent, and it is better.

2026 side: three sources carry the argument. Dor Yeshorim's own description of its process, which is Lem's device in operation; Karavani et al., "Screening Human Embryos for Polygenic Traits Has Limited Utility" (Cell, 2019), for what selection can and cannot buy; and the reporting on Nucleus Embryo (2025) for what is now on sale. The rest is in the claims register.
